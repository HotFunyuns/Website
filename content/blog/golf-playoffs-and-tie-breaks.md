Golf has no single tie-break. In stroke play, the Rules say the lowest total wins and leave ties to the **Terms of the Competition**, which the Committee sets before the event: a sudden-death playoff, an aggregate playoff over a fixed number of holes, a matching of scorecards (a "count-back"), or simply a tie. In match play the default is different: a level match is extended hole by hole until someone wins a hole, unless the Terms say otherwise.

The guidance quoted here comes from Rule 3 of the 2023 Rules of Golf, still the edition in force on 2 October 2026, and the Committee Procedures published with it. (A different meaning of "golf playoffs", the season-ending points series some professional tours run, is a competition rather than a tie-break and is not covered here.)

## The four ways to settle a stroke-play tie

| Method | How it works | Main trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sudden death | Extra holes one at a time; anyone beaten on a hole drops out | Fast, but one hole decides it |
| Aggregate playoff | Lowest combined score over a set number of extra holes, then sudden death if still level | Fairer to the stronger player, but slower, and may need another day |
| Matching scorecards | Compare scores over the closing holes already played | No extra golf needed, but it decides the result on part of the round |
| Leave it as a tie | Prizes or places are shared | Simple, but unsatisfying when there is one trophy |

The Committee Procedures add two firm lines. A tie in stroke play should not be decided by a match, and a tie in a match should not be decided by a stroke-play playoff. Each format settles its own ties.

## Sudden death

The tied players play an extra hole. Whoever has the lowest score continues; anyone with a higher score is out. Repeat until one player is left. Sudden death is the most common playoff format in stroke-play tournaments, and it is quick, since it can end on the very first extra hole.

What the Terms should set in advance is when the playoff starts, which holes are used and in what order, how many holes it lasts and what happens if players are still tied. Getting that right beforehand matters, because the Committee Procedures allow the method to be changed only in exceptional circumstances, such as bad weather making a planned hole-by-hole playoff impossible, when a count-back can be used instead.

## Aggregate playoffs

Here the tied players play a set number of holes and add the scores; the lowest total wins. Two, three, four and eighteen-hole versions have all been used, and if the players are still level at the end, sudden death normally follows. The appeal is that one bad hole does not end it. The cost is time: a short aggregate playoff held straight after the round takes longer to complete than sudden death, and an eighteen-hole playoff may be held the following day.

## Matching scorecards, step by step

When a playoff is impractical, as it often is in club competitions where players have gone home, the Terms can specify matching scorecards. The method described in the Committee Procedures works backwards from the end of the competition:

1. **The last round**, if the competition had more than one.
2. **The last nine holes**, then **the last six**, then **the last three**, then **the 18th hole**.
3. If still level, **the last six, last three and last hole of the first nine**, in turn.
4. If even that fails, the Committee may **declare a tie** or use **a random method**, such as tossing a coin.

The first stage that separates the players settles it. If the round was shorter than 18 holes, the number of holes compared may be adjusted. For a competition with a multiple-tee start, the recommendation is that the "last nine" means holes 10 to 18, the "last six" holes 13 to 18 and so on, for everyone.

**A worked example, gross.** Two players tie on 79 in a single-round competition. Their back nines are 39 each, so the last nine does not separate them. Over the last six, one has 26 and the other 25. The player with 25 wins.

**A worked example, net.** In individual net stroke play the recommended approach deducts a fraction of each player's handicap at each stage: one-half for the last nine, one-third for the last six, one-sixth for the last three, and so on. Take two players tied on net 70, with handicaps of 18 and 12. If their back nines were 44 and 42 gross, the net figures are 44 − 9 = 35 and 42 − 6 = 36, so the player with the higher handicap wins the count-back. How to treat fractions of a stroke follows the World Handicap System guidance or the local handicapping authority. In formats that already use the stroke index hole by hole, such as Stableford or four-ball stroke play, handicap strokes are simply applied as they were during the competition.

Matching scorecards also settles places other than first. Even when the winner is decided by a playoff, the Terms may use a count-back for second, third and the rest.

## Handicaps in a playoff

A net playoff needs a handicap adjustment too. In regular stroke play over fewer than 18 holes, the Committee Procedures recommend deducting handicaps in proportion to the holes played, so a one-hole playoff deducts one-eighteenth of each handicap. In Four-Ball, Stableford or Par/Bogey, strokes are applied on the playoff holes as they were assigned in the competition, using the stroke index.

## Ties in match play

Rule 3.2a(4) extends a tied match one hole at a time, playing the holes in their usual order unless the Committee sets another, and the extra holes count as a continuation of the same round. The Terms of the Competition can instead declare that the match ends in a tie, start the extension at a particular hole, or use a fixed number of extra holes such as nine or eighteen. A match won on the first extra hole is described as won "at the 19th".

## Ties below the top

Not every tie is for the trophy, and the Terms should cover the others.

- **The cut.** If a competition has a cut, the Terms should state whether ties for the final qualifying position will be broken. [How a golf cut works](/blog/how-a-golf-cut-works/) explains why the line often lets every tied player through.
- **Qualifying for a match-play draw.** Ties for positions in the draw, other than the last place, can be settled by the order in which scores were returned, a scorecard playoff or a random draw. A tie for the very last place may be settled by a playoff, or by adding another round of matches.

## How much does the format favour the better player?

A short playoff is close to a coin toss between similar players, and a toy model shows why. Every number in it is an assumption chosen to make the arithmetic visible; none is a measurement of real golfers or real playoffs. Suppose two tied players, A and B. On any single hole A beats B 30% of the time, B beats A 20% of the time, and they halve the other 50%. Each hole is independent of the last, and the margin on a hole is never more than one stroke. That gives A an average edge of a tenth of a stroke per hole (0.30 − 0.20), or about 1.8 strokes over 18 holes: a real but modest advantage.

| Playoff format, under the toy model's assumptions | A's share of playoffs |
| --- | --- |
| Sudden death | 60% |
| Three-hole aggregate, then sudden death if level | About 62% |
| Eighteen-hole aggregate, then sudden death if level | About 74% |

Sudden death ends on the first hole that is not halved, so A wins 0.30 ÷ (0.30 + 0.20) = 60% of them. The aggregate figures come from adding up every possible run of hole results. Over three holes A finishes ahead 44.1% of the time and level 30.5% of the time, and level players go on to sudden death, so A's share is 44.1% + (30.5% × 60%), or about 62%. The same calculation over eighteen holes gives about 74%.

The direction is the point, not the exact figures: the longer a playoff runs, the more a genuine per-hole edge accumulates, and the shorter it is, the more it rewards one good or bad hole. That is the honest case for each format. Sudden death is quick and dramatic and fits a day's daylight; longer aggregates are slower and give the stronger player more of the result.

The arithmetic also changes strategy. In sudden death a par can lose immediately to a birdie, so a player facing an opponent in good position has less to gain from a safe par than they would in an aggregate playoff, where the next hole can repair it.
