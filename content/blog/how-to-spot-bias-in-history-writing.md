"Biased" is usually used to mean "I disagree with this", which makes it useless as a reading tool. A more workable approach separates four different things that get lumped together: perspective, selection, framing and distortion. Only the fourth is a reason to put a book down, and each of the four has its own check.

## Perspective: declared, unavoidable, and not a fault

Every work of history starts from a question, and questions come from somewhere. An economic historian and a historian of religion writing about the same decade will produce different books without either being dishonest.

The American Historical Association's Statement on Standards of Professional Conduct treats this as normal rather than regrettable, stating that "Multiple, conflicting perspectives are among the truths of history" and that "Disagreements and uncertainties enrich our discipline." What the standard asks is that historians present competing interpretations fairly, and it warns that "the desire to score points as an advocate should never tempt a historian to misrepresent the historical record."

**The check:** read the introduction for the argument. A book that states its thesis and names its opponents is easier to read critically than one that presents its conclusions as simple description. Concealed argument is the harder problem, not declared argument.

## Selection: what is missing, and why

Selection is the most common distortion and the least visible, because the reader sees only what is on the page. A century can be narrated entirely through treaties, or entirely through harvest yields, and both accounts can be accurate and incompatible.

**The check:** work from the apparatus rather than the prose. Scan the bibliography for the school of thought that is absent. Scan the index for the groups who do not appear — the enslaved, the colonised, women, labourers, the defeated. A Cornell University Library research guide organises historical research around background reading, books, articles, primary sources and images precisely because a single category of evidence produces a single-shaped answer.

Selection also operates at the level of the discipline. The Royal Historical Society's 2018 report *Race, Ethnicity & Equality in UK History* found that 96.1 per cent of UK-national university historians were White and that Black historians made up under one per cent of university-based history staff, and argued that curriculum content and workforce composition reinforce each other. Which questions get asked is not independent of who is in the room.

## Framing: the work done by verbs and agency

Framing is where the same event acquires a different moral shape without a single false statement. Watch for who is the grammatical subject of violent acts, and who merely experiences them. "Settlers arrived and the population declined" and "settlers seized land and killed the people living on it" can describe one sequence of events.

Watch, too, for asymmetric vocabulary: raid versus expedition, rebel versus freedom fighter, massacre versus incident, pacification versus conquest. The euphemisms are not neutral shorthand; they are arguments compressed into a word.

**The check:** find two passages where the book describes the same kind of act by two different actors, and compare the verbs. If one side acts and the other side only suffers or reacts, the framing is doing argumentative work the footnotes are not.

## Distortion: the line that matters

Distortion is misuse of evidence, and it is the only one of the four that is a professional violation rather than an interpretive choice. The AHA is explicit that historians "do not fabricate evidence" and that "Forgery and fraud violate the most basic foundations on which historians construct their interpretations of the past," and it requires documentation that leaves "a clear trail for subsequent historians to follow."

**The check:** follow a footnote. Take the single most striking claim in a chapter — the one you would repeat to someone else — and go to its source. Does the source say that? Is it a primary document or a chain of later citations? Does a quoted line survive being read in its full paragraph? One followed footnote tells you more about a book than fifty pages of reading. The method for doing this well is the same one covered in [analysing a primary source](/blog/how-to-analyse-a-primary-source/), and it rests on knowing which of your sources are contemporary in the first place, as [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) explains.

## What a transparent product looks like

Declaring limits is itself a signal worth learning to recognise, and it applies to apps as much as to books. World History Timeline Sim is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and its disclaimer screen states that it is "not a substitute for textbooks, peer-reviewed scholarship, or primary sources" and that "Historians disagree about many topics covered, and interpretations change over time."

We include it as a worked example rather than as a recommendation. The app's content carries no citations and has had no external review, which is exactly why the disclaimer matters — a product that tells you where its authority stops is easier to use responsibly than one that implies it has none of those limits. Apply the same test to any history source, printed or digital: does it tell you what it is not?

The useful corollary is that a source with a narrow, stated scope can be genuinely helpful. An app's era filters and event lists can show you the shape of a period and the names you need to look up. They cannot tell you which interpretation of that period is better supported, and no app, ours included, replaces a teacher, a scholarly book, an archive or a primary document.

## Reading a disagreement rather than picking a winner

When two historians conflict, the instinct is to decide who is right. A better first move is to work out what kind of disagreement it is: about the evidence, about what the evidence means, about which question matters, or about definitions. Most public arguments about history are really arguments about the third or fourth.

The UK National Archives builds its classroom resources around this, asking students what Elizabethan and Jacobite propaganda was designed to achieve, and whether the Great Fire of London is better explained by "treachery or chance". These are interpretation exercises with genuinely open answers, and they are free to work through.

Once you are comfortable with that, the rest of the method follows: choose a chronological spine, read for arguments rather than facts, and keep notes with sources attached, as [studying history on your own](/blog/how-to-study-history-on-your-own/) sets out. The [history learning methods hub](/blog/topics/history-learning-methods/) collects the related technique articles, more of our writing sits in the [education and brain training section](/blog/category/education-brain/), and the app itself is described on the [World History Timeline Sim page](/apps/world-history-timeline-sim/).

The goal is not to end up with a shelf of unbiased books, because there is no such shelf. It is to read any book precisely enough to know which of its claims you can carry away and which you cannot.
