Risings against colonial rule are usually taught one at a time, as local episodes with local causes. Placed side by side, three well-documented cases — the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, the Maji Maji war of 1905–1907, and the detention system built in Kenya in the 1950s — show recurring mechanisms: land and labour seized, religious practice suppressed, and a colonial response calibrated to what the colonising power believed it could do to people it did not regard as equals.

They also show how different these conflicts were from one another, which is the part comparison tends to destroy. What follows keeps both in view.

## Pueblo, 1680: coordinated in advance

The Pueblo Revolt began on 10 August 1680. The Indian Pueblo Cultural Center, a Pueblo-governed institution, records that it had been planned for 13 August and was brought forward after two of the runners carrying word of it were arrested and questioned. Those runners carried knotted cords; the knots counted down the days.

Leadership is attributed to Po'pay — Ripe Squash in Tewa — described as a farmer and a member of a medicine society from Ohkay Owingeh, the community also known as San Juan Pueblo. The detail that matters is the coordination. Separate communities, speaking different languages, agreed on a date and kept it secret well enough that the discovery of two messengers only advanced the timetable rather than preventing the rising.

Spanish colonial rule was expelled and did not return for twelve years. The Center describes 1680 as the most successful uprising against colonial intruders by an assembly of Indigenous people, and as the only war American Indians won against Europeans. That is the institution's own framing and worth reading as such — but the twelve-year interval is a matter of record, and it is exceptional. The Spanish reconquest came in 1692.

## Maji Maji, 1905–1907: a cash crop and a prophecy

John Iliffe's study in the *Journal of African History* remains the standard account of how the Maji Maji rising was organised. It began in the Rufiji Valley of German East Africa as peasant resistance to compulsory communal cotton cultivation — a specific colonial labour demand, not a general grievance.

What turned a regional refusal into a war across a large territory was religious. Ministers associated with the Kolelo spirit distributed a water-medicine — the *maji* that gave the rising its name — and it became the movement's unifying symbol among communities with no prior political relationship. As the rising spread, Iliffe argues, it took on the character of a millenarian sorcery-eradication movement, which by its nature challenged existing authority structures.

Iliffe then identifies the internal contradiction, and it is the most useful part of the analysis for comparative purposes. The movement's revolutionary claim was that it transcended established political divisions in order to strengthen resistance. But as it expanded into areas with stronger existing organisation, participants increasingly worked through those very structures. He suggests this tension — between an ideology of transcendence and a practice of reliance — is characteristic of mass movements generally.

German suppression was severe, and the war and the famine that followed it killed on a scale that is estimated rather than counted. Where a figure is quoted for Maji Maji deaths it should be traced to the study that produced it, because the underlying records are colonial, partial and produced by the side doing the killing.

## Kenya in the 1950s: the archive as part of the mechanism

The Kenyan case is different in kind because its documentation was deliberately controlled.

Aoife Duffy's analysis in *Law and History Review* examines detention camps in British Kenya through the Hanslope disclosure: more than 8,000 files found in 2011 at a Foreign and Commonwealth Office facility at Hanslope Park in Milton Keynes, relating to thirty-seven former colonies, and transferred to the National Archives between 2012 and 2013. Her argument is that emergency detention laws created what she calls an architecture of destruction with custodial violence built into it, and that the enduring legacy of detention without trial in colonial Kenya is an accountability deficit.

Concealment was not incidental. Duffy describes administrative suppression of evidence, official denial, and a propaganda apparatus that controlled the public account. The British government's settlement of Mau Mau claims in 2013 followed decades of that denial and of record destruction designed to obscure what had happened. This is a live matter rather than a closed one, and writing about it as settled history would be inaccurate.

Kim Wagner's work supplies the wider frame. Examining C. E. Callwell's influential manual *Small Wars* and the development of expanding ammunition, Wagner argues that a "rule of colonial difference" — treating non-white opponents as categorically unlike European ones — legitimised violence that would have been considered unacceptable between Western states. His conclusion is deliberately blunt: that slaughter, not restraint, was the British way in colonial warfare. Expanding bullets, he shows, were developed for use against colonised populations rather than European armies.

## What the comparison supports, and what it does not

Three things recur across the cases. Each began with a concrete extraction — land, labour, or a compulsory crop. Each depended on organisation that colonial administrators did not detect until late, and in two of the three that organisation was religious as well as political. And each met a response whose severity was licensed by the colonisers' view of who they were fighting.

What the comparison must not do is merge these societies. Pueblo communities, the peoples of the Rufiji Valley, and Kenyan communities in the 1950s had nothing in common except being colonised, and a category built on that fact alone describes the coloniser, not them. Nor should the outcomes be smoothed: one rising expelled a colonial state for twelve years, one was crushed, and one is still working through courts and archives.

The evidence bases differ too, which is why reading these cases together is good training in source criticism. The 1680 revolt survives largely through Spanish colonial testimony and Pueblo oral tradition; Maji Maji through German administrative records and later oral history; Kenya through files an administration tried to destroy or hide.
