A quiz you take for fun and a standardised psychological assessment can contain questions that look almost identical and still be entirely different objects. The difference is not in the items. It is in everything wrapped around them: who the comparison group was, who is allowed to administer it, what evidence exists that the score is consistent, and whether the conditions were controlled.

Four things routinely get collapsed into one idea, and separating them is the whole job of this article: **practice**, **entertainment**, **educational activity** and **standardised psychological assessment**.

## The four categories, kept apart

**Practice** is repeated work on a defined task with feedback. Its output tells you how the practice went. It is self-referenced by nature — the useful comparison is with your own earlier attempts.

**Entertainment** exists to be enjoyable. A quiz that assigns you a label for sharing is entertainment, and it is a perfectly respectable thing to be. Its output is a talking point.

**Educational activity** teaches something: a method, a fact, a technique. Its output is whether you can now do something you could not do before.

**Standardised psychological assessment** produces a score with a defined meaning, because the whole procedure was built to support that meaning. Its output is a position relative to a documented reference group, with a stated margin of error.

Problems begin when the output of one is read as the output of another — most often when an entertainment result is read as an assessment result.

## The checklist that separates them

Five structural features distinguish an assessment. None of them is visible in a screenshot of a question.

**A documented norm sample.** Somebody administered this instrument to a defined group under controlled conditions and published who they were. Without that, a percentile has nothing to be a percentile of.

**A technical manual.** The reliability evidence, the standardisation procedure, the validity studies and the scoring tables exist as a published document that a professional can scrutinise.

**Published evidence of consistency.** ETS defines reliable scores as those consistent across different occasions of testing, across different editions of a test, and across different raters scoring the same responses. An instrument that publishes no such evidence is asking to be taken on trust.

**A qualification requirement.** Pearson lists the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, Fifth Edition — a battery covering ages 16:0 to 90:11, drawing its Full Scale IQ from seven core subtests across five cognitive domains — at Qualification Level C, meaning a credentialled professional is required to purchase and administer it. A product anyone can open is not operating under that model.

**Fixed conditions.** Standardisation means every test taker meets the same material the same way the norm sample did. Unsupervised delivery gives this up immediately, for reasons set out in [are online IQ tests accurate](/blog/are-online-iq-tests-accurate/).

## Even a real assessment has a narrower job than people assume

It is worth adding a caution that cuts the other way, because "real test" is sometimes treated as a licence to decide things.

A 2026 framework paper in the *Journal of Intelligence* draws the distinction between **predictive validity** — whether scores relate to outcomes — and **decision utility** — whether using a score actually improves a decision. Cognitive measures do relate moderately to strongly with academic outcomes, and can add predictive value over an existing record, particularly at transition points. But that gain is context-dependent, low base rates inflate false positives, and a measure can improve average prediction while worsening outcomes for a subgroup. The authors argue that testing should be conditional and sequential, used only where it can plausibly change what happens next.

So the ladder is not "quiz bad, test good". It is that an assessment earns a specific, bounded kind of trust, and a quiz earns none of it.

## What an app store listing does and does not tell you

Category and rating information is frequently over-read, so it is worth being precise about what it means.

Google Play explains that content ratings inform you of potentially objectionable content within an app, and that they are the responsibility of the app developers and the International Age Rating Coalition. The ESRB EVERYONE rating, used in North and South America, indicates content generally suitable for all ages, possibly containing minimal cartoon, fantasy or mild violence, or infrequent mild language.

Google also states something that deserves quoting whenever a store listing is used as evidence of anything: content ratings do not tell you whether an app is designed for users of a specific age. They describe content, not suitability, and they say nothing whatsoever about whether a product's numbers mean anything.

## Three questions before you believe a result

Who was I compared with, and is that group described anywhere? What evidence is published that this score would be similar if I took it again next month? And who administered it, under what conditions?

If a product cannot answer all three, enjoy the questions and ignore the number.
