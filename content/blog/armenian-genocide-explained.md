The Armenian Genocide was the mass killing and forced deportation of the Ottoman Empire's Armenian population during the First World War. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum dates the core period from spring 1915 through autumn 1916, attributes it to the Ottoman government under the Committee of Union and Progress — the party also known as the Young Turks — and gives a death toll of "at least 664,000 and possibly as many as 1.2 million". Most readers meet the subject first as an argument about a word, which is the wrong way round. The sequence is documented; the disagreement is about how to characterise it, and about who has standing to do so.

## The documented sequence

The museum's account separates three categories of victim, and the distinction matters because the deaths happened in different ways: "people killed in local massacres that began in spring 1915; others who died during deportations, under conditions of starvation, dehydration, exposure, and disease; and Armenians who died in or en route to the desert regions."

That is a description of a policy with stages. Communities were removed from their homes in Anatolia and marched south and east; the routes ended in the desert regions of Syria and Mesopotamia. The Armenian Genocide Museum-Institute in Yerevan describes the same process as the "mass and premeditated annihilation of the Armenian population" and records that the deportees were "primarily the elderly, women, and children" — the demographic consequence of men having been conscripted, disarmed or killed first.

The USHMM states the intent as it understands it: to "solidify Muslim Turkish dominance in the regions of central and eastern Anatolia". That is a claim about purpose, and it is the claim the characterisation dispute turns on.

24 April is the date commemorated annually at the memorial complex at Tsitsernakaberd in Yerevan, and it is associated with the arrest and deportation of Armenian community leaders and intellectuals in the Ottoman capital in 1915. It functions as the conventional opening marker of the period rather than as the first violence.

## Why the dates differ between reputable sources

The USHMM's overview covers 1915-16. The Armenian Genocide Museum-Institute uses 1915-1923.

Neither is an error. The first frames the concentrated period of deportations and killings; the second extends through the further years of war, population removal and displacement in Anatolia that ended with the founding of the Turkish Republic. A reader who sees both and assumes one must be wrong has run into an ordinary feature of historical writing: endpoints are chosen to fit a question. Our guide to [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) covers why that happens across the discipline, and the same phenomenon produces the conflicting dates you find in reference works for [the Bronze Age collapse](/blog/what-caused-the-bronze-age-collapse/).

The practical rule is to state which frame you are using. "The genocide of 1915-16" and "the genocide of 1915-1923" are both defensible; using them interchangeably in one paragraph is not.

## Why the death toll is a range

The USHMM gives at least 664,000 and possibly as many as 1.2 million. A range that wide is not evasion; it reflects what the evidence can bear.

Deaths from exposure, starvation and disease during forced marches were not systematically registered by anyone with an interest in registering them. The pre-1915 Armenian population of the empire is itself an estimate, and Ottoman state figures and Armenian Patriarchate figures differ. Records were destroyed, both incidentally in wartime and deliberately. Survivor testimony is extensive and invaluable for what happened, and weak for arithmetic.

Presenting a single round number would imply a precision nobody has. Presenting the range, and saying who produced it, is the accurate move — the same discipline required when reading [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) on any contested event.

## Who applies the term, who rejects it, and on what grounds

This is the part that needs attribution rather than adjudication in our voice.

The Republic of Türkiye's Ministry of Foreign Affairs states that "Türkiye does not deny the suffering of Armenians, including the loss of many innocent lives, during the First World War", while holding that "it is factually problematic, morally unsound and legally unfounded to call this episode a 'genocide'". Its argument is that genocide is "a clearly defined crime" whose application should not be settled by legislatures, and it has proposed a joint commission of Turkish and Armenian historians working from the archives of both countries and of third states.

Against that, the International Association of Genocide Scholars — the principal professional body in the field — adopted a resolution on the Armenian Genocide in June 1997, and a further resolution on the Assyrian and Greek genocides in 2007. Many states, parliaments and research institutions apply the term. The USHMM notes that "the origin of the term genocide and its codification in international law have their roots in the mass murder of Armenians in 1915-16" — the case is not merely covered by the concept, it is part of how the concept came to exist.

The weight of scholarly opinion is not evenly divided, and saying so is not the same as adjudicating. The honest formulation is: this is the position of the Turkish state, this is the position of the genocide-studies profession and of many governments, these are the grounds each gives, and here is where the evidence sits.

Two further things should not be flattened. The Armenians, Assyrians and Ottoman Greeks were distinct communities whose experiences in this period were related but not identical, and the IAGS treated them in separate resolutions for that reason. And the Kurdish populations of the same regions occupied a complicated position that varied by locality and is the subject of its own scholarship. Collapsing any of these into a single story loses information that the sources contain.

## The First World War context, without using it as an excuse

The genocide happened inside a world war, and the Ottoman state's wartime situation is part of the causal account — mobilisation, the Russian front in the Caucasus, fear of an internal population regarded as disloyal.

Context is not justification, and the distinction is worth holding firmly. A wartime emergency explains why a state had the opportunity and the cover; it does not explain the decision to deport an entire population into a desert. Our article on [what started the First World War](/blog/what-started-the-first-world-war/) deals with the war's own causal argument, and one of the reasons the two are kept separate is precisely that folding a genocide into a war narrative tends to dissolve responsibility for it.

## What to take away

**The sequence is documented and should be stated first.** Deportations, massacres, and deaths from starvation, exposure and disease on the routes to the Syrian desert, in 1915-16 at minimum.

**Figures come as ranges with a source attached.** At least 664,000 and possibly as many as 1.2 million, per the USHMM.

**Attribute the dispute rather than adjudicating it — and do not pretend it is balanced.** The Turkish state's position is on the record and should be quoted accurately; so should the position of the genocide-studies profession, which is not the same size.
