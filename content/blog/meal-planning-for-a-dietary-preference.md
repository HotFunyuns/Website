Cooking two dinners every night is the thing nobody sustains. The household with one vegetarian, or one person avoiding a particular ingredient, usually tries it for a fortnight and then quietly reverts to either feeding everyone the restricted version or feeding the restricted person separately and worse.

There is a third option, and it is structural rather than heroic: plan meals that are the same until nearly the end, and let them diverge once. The work then goes into how the week is shaped rather than into how many pans are on the hob.

## Start with the constraint, not the recipes

Write down the actual rule before opening anything. Not "vegetarian" but the specific version in your house — eggs yes, fish no, gelatine matters. Not "low carb" but the threshold the person is working to. Not "no nuts" but whether that includes peanuts, tree nuts, traces, and shared equipment.

This is tedious and it saves the whole week, because the failure mode in mixed-household planning is planning against a label and discovering at 6pm that the label did not match the rule.

[Easy Recipes & Meal Planner](/apps/easy-recipes-meal-planner/) is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and it lets you browse by meal type, cuisine or dietary preference, which narrows the field quickly. Treat the filter as a shortlist rather than a verdict — the ingredient list is where the rule is actually satisfied or not.

## Component cooking is the whole technique

The single idea that makes a mixed household workable is to stop thinking in finished dishes and start thinking in parts.

A meal is usually a base, a protein and a sauce or seasoning. If the base is shared and the protein is the divergence, one dinner becomes one process with a branch:

- **Shared:** rice, roasted vegetables, a tomato and herb sauce.
- **Branch A:** chicken, cooked separately, added at the end.
- **Branch B:** chickpeas or halloumi, cooked separately, added at the end.

Nothing about the base changed. The extra work is one pan and five minutes, and both people ate the same meal in every respect that matters for shopping, prep and cleaning.

Three meal shapes take to this especially well, and a week built mostly from them tends to hold:

**Bowls and traybakes**, where every element is already separate. **Assembled dishes** — tacos, wraps, loaded potatoes — where the divergence happens at the table rather than the hob. **Sauced dishes with a late-added protein**, where the sauce carries the flavour and the protein is a topping rather than a foundation.

What resists component cooking is anything where the restricted ingredient is cooked into the structure from the start: a meat ragù simmered for two hours, a custard, a stock-based soup. Those are the meals to plan as either-or, not as branches.

## Put the branch points in the plan, not in your head

Once you know which meals branch, write it down in the slot. The planner has breakfast, lunch, dinner and snack slots so the week is decided before it gets busy, and a slot that says "traybake — swap chicken for halloumi in one portion" is a decision already made. A slot that just says "traybake" is a decision you will make while tired.

The same applies to shopping. A branched meal needs both proteins, and the most common midweek failure in a mixed household is buying for the majority version and improvising for the other. Matching the plan against the pantry inventory before you shop — seeing what you have and what is missing — is what stops that, and it is the same discipline described in [how to meal plan for the week](/blog/how-to-meal-plan-for-the-week/).

## Nutrients that need deliberate attention

When a food group leaves one person's plate, the rest of their week has to cover for it, and this is the part that benefits from naming rather than assuming.

NHS guidance on vegetarian eating is specific about three: iron, which is less readily absorbed from plant sources and is helped by including a source of vitamin C in the same meal; vitamin B12, which occurs naturally in animal products and otherwise needs dairy, eggs or fortified foods; and omega-3 fatty acids, where the guidance notes that plant sources may not carry the same benefits as those in oily fish. Its overall framing is that variety across the diet is what makes the pattern work.

Two more general points are worth holding alongside that. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans, updated jointly by the Department of Health and Human Services and the Department of Agriculture, give advice aimed at meeting nutrient needs across an overall eating pattern rather than a single meal. And the NHS Eatwell Guide makes the same point about time horizon: the balance does not have to be achieved at every meal, only across a day or a week.

For household planning that translates into a practical rule: **check coverage at the level of the week, per person.** A vegetarian member's week should contain iron sources with vitamin C alongside them, a reliable B12 source, and enough variety that no single food is doing all the work. Individual dinners do not each need to be complete.

Anyone with a medical condition, a pregnancy, a child's diet to manage or a diagnosed deficiency should take this to a qualified professional rather than to a planner. The app tracks what you tell it; it does not assess anyone's needs.

## Allergy is a different job

It is worth separating two things that often get planned together.

A preference can be handled with a substitution. An allergy cannot, because the risk is not confined to the ingredient list — it extends to preparation, shared utensils, storage and packaging. The app lets you record allergies and previous reactions with notes and timestamps and flags recipes when an ingredient matches something you logged, which is genuinely useful for keeping track, and its own guidance is that you should always confirm ingredients and food labels for your own needs.

Treat the flag as a prompt to check rather than as a clearance. [Keeping a food allergy and reaction log](/blog/tracking-food-allergies-and-reactions/) covers what a useful record looks like, and the checking itself belongs with the packaging in front of you.

## Scaling when appetites differ

A mixed household is rarely also an evenly sized one. Recipes in the app start at a practical one-person serving and scale up, with ingredient quantities and available nutrition adjusting to the serving size you choose, which is the mechanism that lets a branched meal be two portions of one thing and four of another.

Scaling is not always linear in the kitchen, though — pan size, cooking time and seasoning do not simply multiply, as [recipe scaling and serving sizes](/blog/recipe-scaling-and-serving-sizes/) explains. For branched meals that mostly shows up as needing a second pan rather than a bigger one, which is the same extra pan the branch already required.

Put together, the week looks like this: three or four branchable meals, one or two that everyone eats as written, one either-or meal where the restricted eater gets their own dish properly rather than as an afterthought, and one deliberately easy slot.
