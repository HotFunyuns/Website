By the test linguists most often apply, Shanghainese is a language. It belongs to the Wu group, and Wu is mutually unintelligible with Mandarin — a Mandarin speaker cannot follow it without study. That is the same criterion that separates Spanish from Portuguese, and it gives the same answer here.

It is nevertheless called a dialect constantly, including by people who speak it, and that is not simply a mistake. Understanding why is more useful than picking a side.

## Where the word "dialect" comes from

The English sentence "Shanghainese is a dialect" is usually a translation. The Chinese term is 方言 (*fāngyán*), and it is routinely rendered as *dialect* because there is no better single English word for it.

The two terms do not mean the same thing. English *dialect* implies a variant of some standard language — a version of the real thing, with the real thing being the standard. 方言 denotes regional speech without that built-in hierarchy. A term that means "the way people speak in a region" comes into English as a term that means "a non-standard version of Mandarin", and a claim gets smuggled in during translation that the original never made.

Almost all of the confusion in English-language discussion traces back to that one lossy word.

## The intelligibility test, applied carefully

If mutual intelligibility is the criterion, the result is clear. Shanghainese, like the rest of the Wu group, is mutually unintelligible with Mandarin. Not "hard to follow". Not "requires concentration". Not accessible without study.

But apply the same test *inside* Wu and the picture gets more interesting, and anyone who wants to use intelligibility as a clean rule should sit with this.

Northern Wu varieties — Shanghainese, Suzhounese, Huzhounese and their neighbours — form a group that is mutually intelligible internally. Southern Wu varieties, Wenzhounese among them, are not mutually intelligible with Northern Wu and do not form a single intelligible group among themselves.

So Wu is not one language with regional accents. It is a group in which the intelligibility boundaries run in places that do not match the name. If you take the test seriously, it does not hand you a tidy list of languages; it hands you a gradient with some sharp edges in it. [Wu Chinese and where Shanghainese fits](/blog/wu-chinese-and-where-shanghainese-sits/) maps that out.

## Why the dialect framing persists, and what is genuinely true in it

It would be easy to treat "dialect" as pure political framing. That would be unfair to the people using the word, because several of the things it points at are real.

**The writing system is shared.** Speakers of Wu, Mandarin and Cantonese all read written Chinese. A shared literate tradition is a powerful unifying fact and it does not exist between Spanish and Portuguese in the same way.

**The vocabularies overlap substantially.** These are related varieties descended from a common ancestor, not unrelated languages that happen to be neighbours.

**The identity is shared.** Shanghainese speakers are not generally claiming a separate linguistic nationality. They are describing how people speak at home, which is exactly what *fāngyán* means.

**And the varieties interpenetrate.** Inserting Mandarin into a Shanghainese conversation is ordinary, particularly for younger speakers, and the Shanghainese spoken by young people differs from that of older generations partly for that reason.

The honest position is that *dialect* is a poor English word for a real Chinese concept, and that the mutual-intelligibility answer is the one that matters if your question is "can I understand this without learning it".

## What the answer changes for a learner

This is where the abstraction pays out. The label you attach makes concrete predictions, and they are testable.

**If Shanghainese were a dialect of Mandarin**, a Mandarin speaker would get a substantial discount, graded materials would be plentiful, pronunciation would be recoverable from characters through a systematic mapping, and you could use Mandarin resources with adjustments.

**Because it is a separate language**, none of that holds. Mandarin gives you characters and general orientation and very little else — the sound system, the tone behaviour and the high-frequency vocabulary all differ, as [Shanghainese vs Mandarin](/blog/shanghainese-vs-mandarin-differences/) sets out. Materials are scarce. There is no standard romanisation. Nothing lets you derive the pronunciation of a character.

The practical cost of the wrong label is a wrong timeline. A learner expecting a dialect budgets a few weekends, discovers in month two that they are learning a language, and concludes that they are bad at it.

## Why our catalogue says Wu Chinese

[Learn Shanghainese Wu Chinese](/apps/learn-shanghainese/) carries "Wu Chinese" in its name, and that was a deliberate choice with a cost attached.

The cost is discoverability. Far more people search for "Shanghai dialect" than for "Wu Chinese", and putting the less familiar term in the name means some people looking for exactly this course do not immediately recognise it as such.

We kept it because the alternative framing changes what the course has to be. A course positioned as a Mandarin dialect module is under pressure to route everything through Mandarin — to teach a Shanghainese word as a transformation of the Mandarin word, to present the tones as Mandarin's tones with modifications, to assume the learner already reads pinyin and simply needs a conversion table.

That is a materially easier course to build and it teaches something that is not Shanghainese.

Naming it Wu meant committing to teaching the sound system in its own terms, including the voiced initial series that Mandarin lacks a slot for; recording audio from Wu speakers rather than adapting Mandarin recordings; and covering tone sandhi in the first third of the course rather than treating it as a refinement, because in Shanghainese the tone belongs to the word rather than the syllable.
