Three people watch the same twelve rounds and hand in three different scorecards. To most viewers that looks like incompetence. It is not — it is a direct consequence of how the scoring system is designed, and once you know what judges are actually being asked to do, the disagreements become predictable rather than mysterious.

The system is public. The Association of Boxing Commissions and Combative Sports publishes the unified rules and a manual for judges, and state regulators adopt them; Nevada's administrative code for unarmed combat contains provisions on the method of judging a contest. None of what follows is inside knowledge.

## The system in one paragraph

Under the unified rules, a bout is evaluated and scored by three judges, and the **10 Point Must System** is the standard method. The winner of a round *must* be awarded 10 points, and the loser gets nine or fewer. An even round is scored 10-10 and is treated as rare.

That word "must" is the part people misread. It does not mean the winner must be obvious. It means the arithmetic is anchored: the judge is never deciding whether to give the round winner 10, only how far below 10 the other boxer falls.

## What separates a 10-9 from a 10-8

The ABC's judge manual sets out a rule of thumb for each score.

| Score | When it applies |
| --- | --- |
| 10-9 | One boxer routinely won the round |
| 10-8 | One knockdown, or one boxer wins the round in an extremely decisive manner including hurting their opponent |
| 10-7 | Two knockdowns, or one boxer clearly dominates and has knocked their opponent down at least once |
| 10-6 | More than two knockdowns, and the score can go lower if more occur |
| 10-10 | The round ends with no clear winner — should rarely happen |

Two details in that table matter more than they look.

**A 10-8 does not require a knockdown.** A round can reach 10-8 on dominance alone, and the manual is specific that this is particularly true where one boxer has done nothing offensively — running, or simply surviving in a defensive posture for the whole round.

**A 10-7 essentially does require one.** The guidance says that to reach that score you should always have at least one knockdown. It is available with a single knockdown if the boxer who scored it also wins the balance of the round decisively and dominantly and hurts their opponent.

### The knockdown is not the whole round

This is the single most misunderstood point in boxing scoring, and the manual addresses it head on.

If Boxer A scores a knockdown, the round should normally be 10-8 for A. But if Boxer B decisively and dominantly wins the rest of the round and actually hurts A while doing it, the round may be scored 10-9 for A. Scoring that situation 10-10 is described as not advisable.

The instruction underneath is blunt: do not assume a knockdown gives an automatic 10-8, especially if it happens early in the round — you still have to score the entire round. And a landed punch, including a knockdown, counts the same whenever in the round it occurs.

## The four factors

Judges are directed to weigh four things.

**Clean and effective punching.** The manual's framing is that fights are won by clean, effective punches landing, and that not all punches are equal — the effect of the punch matters, hard punches count, and so does the number of them. There is no formula that converts one into the other, and judges are told to be fair to both. They are also warned not to concentrate on head shots and forget effective body work.

Where a punch lands matters too. Punches landing directly on the arms are generally not scoring punches unless they are high up, near or on the front of the shoulders — which is worth knowing before you are certain a boxer "landed" a flurry against a high guard.

**Effective aggressiveness.** The boxer taking the fight to their opponent and getting positive results from it. The qualifier is doing real work: if the aggressive boxer is being beaten to the punch by a counterpuncher, that is *not* effective aggressiveness. Moving forward is not the same as winning.

**Ring generalship, or ring control.** The thinking boxer — the one controlling the action, using feints, slips and footwork to keep an opponent off balance and to put themselves in position to score without being scored upon. The manual states plainly that running and simply avoiding action should not be rewarded.

**Defence.** Punching while not being punched: slipping, countering, working off the ropes. Again with a qualifier — good defence is not holding or clinching to avoid contact.

The order is not decorative. Clean and effective punching is the primary consideration, and the later factors do most of their work in rounds where the punching is close.

## Independence, and why cards diverge

Each round is scored on its own merits. The manual is explicit: do not let actions in a previous round affect your score in a later one. A six-round bout produces six independent judgements.

That design has a consequence people find counter-intuitive. **A boxer cannot bank a lead.** Winning a round overwhelmingly is worth the same as winning it narrowly unless the margin was large enough to reach 10-8. A fighter who wins three rounds by a street and loses four narrowly is behind.

It also explains the divergent cards. In a genuinely close round, two judges applying the same four factors in good faith can favour different boxers — one weighing a countering, defensive performance, the other weighing forward pressure that mostly landed. Neither is cheating. The manual even warns against the opposite temptation: do not award rounds simply to make your scorecard look closer.

### The arithmetic of a shutout

If one boxer wins every round 10-9 with no knockdowns, the totals are just multiplication. Twelve rounds at 10 points is 120; twelve rounds at 9 points is 108. That is where the familiar championship shutout card of **120-108** comes from, and the judge manual uses exactly that figure as its illustration — noting that if one fighter wins every round by the slimmest of margins, this is the card you should hand in.

## Beyond the rounds: knockdowns, fouls and partial rounds

A few unified-rules provisions shape scoring at the edges.

- The **mandatory eight count** after a knockdown is standard procedure in all bouts, and a boxer who has been knocked down cannot be saved by the bell in any round.
- **Point deductions for intentional fouls are mandatory.** Where an intentional foul causes an injury and the bout is allowed to continue, the referee notifies the authorities and deducts two points from the boxer who caused it. Where an intentional foul causes an injury severe enough to terminate the bout immediately, the boxer who caused it loses by disqualification.
- **Partial rounds are scored** if four rounds or more have been completed — which is why judges are advised to keep a running score in mind at all times, so that an early stoppage does not leave them guessing.

Point deductions land on the final tally after the rounds are added, which is how a boxer can win more rounds than their opponent and still not win the bout.

## How three cards become one result

Each judge's card is totalled separately, and the result describes how those three totals agree:

- **Unanimous** — all three judges scored for the same boxer.
- **Split** — two judges scored for one boxer, one for the other.
- **Majority** — two judges scored for one boxer and one scored the bout even.
- **Draw** — depending on how the three cards fall, a bout can be drawn unanimously, by majority, or by three differing cards that total to a draw.

Nothing about this system is a measurement in the way a stopwatch is. It is three trained people applying published criteria to a fast, ambiguous event, and the unified rules are an attempt to make their disagreements narrow and explicable rather than to eliminate them.
