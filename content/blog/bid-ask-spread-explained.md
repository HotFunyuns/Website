A stock does not have one price. At any moment it has two: the price buyers are bidding and the price sellers are asking. The gap between them is the bid–ask spread, and it is a cost of trading that never appears as a line on a statement. You pay it by buying a little higher, or selling a little lower, than the "price" in the headline.

## Bid, ask and spread

Investor.gov's glossary defines the terms precisely:

- The **bid** is the highest price a buyer will pay for a specified number of shares at any given time.
- The **ask** (also called the offer) is the lowest price at which a seller will sell.
- The **spread** is the difference between them.

Investor.gov adds that the bid will almost always be lower than the ask. A quote screen may show both, sometimes with the number of shares available at each, along with the last trade, which is a different thing: the price at which the most recent trade happened. [How to read a stock quote](/blog/how-to-read-a-stock-quote/) walks through the rest of the screen.

## Why you buy at the ask and sell at the bid

Investor.gov's page on types of orders explains that a market order generally executes at or near the current ask for a buy, and at or near the current bid for a sell. That is simply who is on the other side: to buy right now, you take the cheapest offer for sale; to sell right now, you take the best offer to buy.

**A worked round trip.** A stock is quoted at $20.00 bid, $20.05 ask.

| Step | Price | Value of 100 shares |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Market buy | $20.05 (the ask) | You pay $2,005 |
| Immediate market sell | $20.00 (the bid) | You receive $2,000 |
| Result | | −$5, before any fees |

Nothing about the company changed, and the quoted price did not move, but the round trip cost $5. For someone who trades often, spreads add up quickly, alongside commissions and taxes.

## Measuring the spread

The dollar spread alone is misleading: 5 cents on a $5 stock is very different from 5 cents on a $500 stock. Wikipedia's article on the bid–ask spread notes that it is often expressed as a percentage of the **midpoint** (the average of bid and ask):

spread % = (ask − bid) ÷ midpoint × 100.

| Bid | Ask | Spread | Midpoint | Spread % |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $20.00 | $20.05 | $0.05 | $20.025 | about 0.25% |
| $4.95 | $5.05 | $0.10 | $5.00 | 2.0% |
| $499.90 | $500.10 | $0.20 | $500.00 | 0.04% |

The second row's spread is only 10 cents, but on a $5 share it is eight times as expensive, proportionally, as the first. For quick mental estimates, our guide to [working out percentages in your head](/blog/percentages-in-your-head/) helps.

## Who earns it

Some firms make a business of standing in the middle. Investor.gov describes **market makers**, firms that stand ready to buy or sell a stock at publicly quoted prices, and notes that a broker that fills your order from its own inventory, called internalisation, may make money on the spread. Wikipedia describes the same logic from the other side: for a round trip, the trader who demands immediate execution pays the spread, and the party supplying liquidity earns it. [How the stock market works](/blog/how-the-stock-market-works/) covers who these firms are.

## Why some spreads are wide

Wikipedia calls the size of the spread one measure of a market's liquidity and of its transaction cost, and gives lower trading volume and less liquidity as reasons some markets have wider spreads. Investor.gov adds that prices can change quickly in fast-moving markets, so the price you saw may not be the one you get. [Trading volume](/blog/trading-volume-explained/) is one rough indicator of how actively a stock changes hands.

A wide spread is not in itself a sign of anything improper, but it is a cost, and it is worth noticing before you trade.

## Working with the spread

- **Look at the bid and ask, not just the last price,** before placing an order.
- **Consider a limit order.** A [limit order](/blog/market-order-vs-limit-order/) lets you choose a price, for example inside the spread, and wait. The trade-off is that it may not fill.
- **Count the spread as a cost** when comparing how often to trade.
