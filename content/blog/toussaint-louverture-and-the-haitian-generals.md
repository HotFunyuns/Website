Toussaint Louverture never saw Haiti become independent. He was seized by a French expeditionary force in June 1802, shipped across the Atlantic, and died in a prison in the Jura mountains on 7 April 1803. Independence was declared nearly nine months later, by Jean-Jacques Dessalines. If your account of the Haitian Revolution has only one name in it, it is missing both the ending and most of the people who produced it.

## The colony, and what was at stake

Saint-Domingue was the most profitable colony in the Americas by the second half of the eighteenth century, and its profitability rested entirely on the enslavement of African people on sugar and coffee plantations. Enslaved people were the overwhelming majority of the population, and the mortality rate of the labour regime was such that the colony required continuous importation to sustain its workforce. That is the fact from which everything else follows; any account that treats the revolution as an eruption without a cause has skipped it.

The French Revolution destabilised the colonial order. Free people of colour pressed for the civil rights the National Assembly had granted them; the white planter minority refused to recognise the decree. In August 1791 enslaved people in the northern plain rose in a coordinated revolt. Spain and Britain, both at war with France, intervened. What followed was more than a decade of multi-sided warfare.

## Toussaint before the revolution, and why it matters

The most significant recent change in Toussaint's biography came from the archives rather than from reinterpretation. Research by Philippe Girard and Jean-Louis Donnadieu, published in *The William and Mary Quarterly* in 2013, established that he was freed decades before 1791, and that as a free man he leased land worked by enslaved people.

That fact is uncomfortable, and it is not optional. It does not diminish what he later did; it locates him precisely in a colonial society in which a small population of free people of colour and freed Black people occupied a real and compromised position. A revolutionary who had himself participated in the institution he went on to destroy is a harder figure to use as a symbol and a more useful one to think with.

It also shows what archival work is for. A finding of this kind does not come from a new argument about an old story; it comes from someone reading notarial and parish records that nobody had connected before. The general point about where evidence comes from is the subject of [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/), and the specific problem of establishing what one named individual did is worked through in [how we know what historical figures actually did](/blog/how-we-know-what-historical-figures-did/). This is one of the cleanest recent examples of it in any field.

## What Toussaint actually did

Between 1793 and 1801 he moved from commanding forces allied with Spain to commanding French republican forces, expelled the British, defeated André Rigaud in the civil conflict of 1799 to 1800, and made himself the effective ruler of the colony.

The 1801 constitution he promulgated is the clearest statement of his position, and it pulls in two directions at once. It abolished slavery permanently and irrevocably. It also named him governor for life with the right to designate his successor, kept Saint-Domingue nominally within the French empire, and bound agricultural workers to the estates under a system of compulsion designed to keep the plantation economy running.

Read generously, that is a leader trying to protect emancipation by preserving the revenue that would let him defend it. Read critically, it is forced labour by another name, imposed by a government whose legitimacy rested on ending forced labour. Both readings have serious support, and a reader is entitled to hold the tension rather than resolve it.

## The French expedition, and the turn

Napoleon Bonaparte sent a large expeditionary force under his brother-in-law Charles Leclerc in 1802. Toussaint was arrested in June and deported. Dessalines and Christophe, among others, initially submitted to the French.

Two things changed that. Yellow fever destroyed the French army from within. And in May 1802 the French government restored slavery in Guadeloupe, which made the expedition's true purpose unmistakable. Generals who had calculated that accommodation was survivable recalculated. Dessalines took command of the resistance, and the war became one of independence rather than of autonomy within the empire.

The decisive engagement was fought at Vertières on 18 November 1803. On 1 January 1804 at Gonaïves, Dessalines declared independence, and the new state took the name Haiti — an Indigenous name for the island — rather than the French colonial one. That choice of name was a political statement about what the new country was refusing to be.

Scholarship on Dessalines, including Deborah Jenson's work in *The William and Mary Quarterly*, has examined how far the revolution's leadership and its forms drew on the experience and languages of people born in Africa rather than in the colony. That is a corrective to older accounts that read the whole event as a Caribbean echo of Paris.

## The violence, stated plainly

In 1804 Dessalines ordered the killing of the remaining French colonists on the island. Thousands of people were killed. This has to be stated directly rather than passed over or dissolved into abstraction.

The context is also documented and also has to be stated: the French had just conducted a war of re-enslavement, had killed prisoners and civilians in large numbers, and the leadership of the new state believed, correctly, that France intended to return. Context explains a decision. It does not convert a massacre into something else, and an article that used it for that purpose would be doing propaganda rather than history.

The same standard applies in both directions. The French expedition's conduct was not an excess within a legitimate enterprise; the enterprise was the restoration of slavery, and the violence was instrumental to it.

## Who wrote the record, and what they wanted from it

Almost all the contemporary written material is French: colonial administration papers, army correspondence, the expedition's own reporting, and the published accounts of men with reputations to defend. Toussaint's own proclamations and letters survive, and the memoir he composed in prison has been edited and translated into English and published by Oxford University Press — a primary text written by a man arguing for his life, which is its own kind of source problem.

Outside France the record is scarcely more neutral. The United States, whose political leadership included many slaveholders, reacted to a state founded by formerly enslaved people with deep unease. The U.S. Department of State's Office of the Historian summarises the position: American policy shifted between supporting the suppression of the revolt and later aiding Toussaint's forces, and the United States did not extend official recognition to Haiti until 1862.

France went further. In 1825 it agreed to recognise Haitian independence on condition that Haiti pay an indemnity, with French warships positioned to attack if the terms were refused. The debt and the loans taken out to service it shaped Haitian public finance for generations. Recognition was sold, and the price was set by the state that had lost the war.

## Getting the sequence right

The single most common error in popular accounts of this revolution is chronological: Toussaint is placed at the declaration of independence, where he was not, and Dessalines is left out, where he was central.

## What to carry away

Toussaint Louverture was a formerly enslaved man who became the most powerful figure in the richest colony in the Americas, destroyed slavery there, wrote a constitution that made himself ruler for life, and died in a French cell without seeing the country his army made possible. Dessalines declared it. Christophe and Pétion then divided it.

None of those sentences fits a hero narrative or a debunking one. They fit the evidence, which is the only test that matters.
