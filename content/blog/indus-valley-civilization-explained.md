The Indus civilization is the largest ancient urban society we cannot read. Its cities were planned, drained, and built to standard brick proportions across a vast area of what is now Pakistan and north-western India, and the people who built them left thousands of short inscriptions that no one has convincingly deciphered. Almost everything popularly said about Indus religion, politics and social structure is therefore inference from objects. Separating what the material record shows from what has been guessed on top of it is the whole task, and it is unusually important here because the gaps have been filled by competing modern politics.

## What the material record shows

UNESCO's description of Moenjodaro — the spelling used on the World Heritage list — records a huge city built entirely of unbaked brick in the third millennium BCE, with an acropolis set on high embankments, ramparts, and a lower town laid out according to strict rules that provide evidence of an early system of town planning. The site splits into two sectors: a citadel area to the west, over whose ruins a Buddhist stupa was built in the second century CE, and the lower city spreading east along the Indus. Buildings there sit along streets intersecting at right angles, with systems of sanitation and drainage. UNESCO calls it the best-preserved urban settlement in South Asia from the beginning of the third millennium BCE, and notes that it was only discovered in 1922.

The scale is confirmed from another direction. The 2019 *Cell* study of an Indus-period genome opens by describing the mature Indus Valley Civilization, also called the Harappan civilization, as spread over north-western South Asia from 2600 to 1900 BCE and as one of the first large-scale urban societies of the ancient world, characterised by systematic town planning, elaborate drainage systems, granaries and standardised weights and measures. It names the major regional centres: Harappa in Punjab, Mohenjo-daro in Sindh, Rakhigarhi in Haryana, Dholavira in Kutch and Ganweriwala in Cholistan, and describes the inhabitants as cosmopolitan, with multiple cultural groups living together.

Note the two date ranges already in play. UNESCO says the civilization flourished between about 2500 and 1500 BCE; the genetics literature brackets the mature phase at 2600 to 1900 BCE. They are measuring different things — the wider cultural tradition including its earlier and later phases, versus the period of large planned cities — and reading them as a contradiction is a mistake of the kind discussed in [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/).

## The labels on the buildings are interpretations

Read any account of Mohenjo-daro and you will meet the Great Bath, the granary and the college of priests. These are not captions the builders left. They are functional identifications made by excavators, some of them nearly a century ago, and several have been questioned since on the grounds that the architectural evidence supports more than one reading.

The same caution applies to the famous objects. The National Museum in New Delhi, which holds a substantial Harappan collection, describes a seal with a buffalo-horned seated figure as almost unanimously identified as Shiva in his form as Pashupati. That identification is an interpretation of an image, made by reading later religious iconography backwards onto an object whose accompanying signs cannot be read, and it is not shared by all specialists. A museum label is a secondary source with a house style, and treating it as a transcription of the object is the standard trap — which is why the distinctions in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) matter even in a gallery.

## The script: rival readings, no accepted decipherment

The inscriptions themselves are the central problem. They are numerous and they are very short, which sharply limits what any method can establish. There is no agreed decipherment, and the state of the field is visible in the fact that the leading proposals are mutually incompatible.

A research archive dedicated to the subject gathers them side by side: Asko Parpola's sustained argument for a proto-Dravidian reading with astral and religious content; Iravatham Mahadevan, who finds common ground with Parpola and also much to disagree with; Ahmad Hassan Dani, who disagrees with both; and statistical analyses by teams including researchers at the Tata Institute. When specialists of that standing cannot agree on the language family involved, claims that the script has been "cracked" — and such claims appear regularly — should be read as proposals awaiting acceptance.

This has a direct consequence for everything else. Without readable text there are no names of rulers, no law codes, no treaties, no chronicles. Statements about Indus governance, warfare or belief are inferences from architecture and objects, and should be labelled as such.

## What ancient DNA has and has not established

Two studies published in 2019 changed the evidential landscape, and both are frequently misreported.

The *Cell* study sequenced a genome from an individual of the Indus period. The ancestry profile fits a mixture of people related to ancient Iranians — the largest component — and to Southeast Asian hunter-gatherers, a combination matching ancient DNA from eleven genetic outliers found at sites in Iran and Turkmenistan that were in cultural communication with the Indus world. Those individuals had little if any Steppe pastoralist ancestry, showing that such ancestry was not widespread in north-west South Asia during the Indus period as it is today. The study also reports that the Iranian-related ancestry derives from a lineage leading to early Iranian farmers, herders and hunter-gatherers *before* those groups separated.

The companion study in *Science* sequenced 523 ancient individuals and describes the primary source of ancestry in modern South Asians as a prehistoric genetic gradient between populations related to early hunter-gatherers of Iran and of Southeast Asia. Following the decline of the Indus civilization, that population mixed in one direction with groups to the south-east and in another with descendants of Steppe pastoralists who spread via Central Asia after about 4,000 years ago.

Two honest qualifications. First, these are not two independent confirmations: the author teams overlap substantially, and they form one research programme rather than two. Second, the number of genomes actually from Indus-period contexts is very small, because preservation conditions in the region are poor.

## The Aryan question, stated carefully

This evidence sits on politically contested ground in South Asia, and it is routinely conscripted by both of the positions it fails to support.

The nineteenth-century invasion model — a conquering population destroying Indus cities — is not what the archaeology shows, and the genetics does not restore it. Steppe-related ancestry appearing after the mature Indus period is a population process. It carries no information by itself about violence, displacement or cultural conquest, and treating a change in ancestry proportions as a description of an event is a category error.

Equally, the evidence does not support a claim of complete indigenous continuity with no external contribution, since Steppe-related ancestry is present in later South Asian populations and absent from the Indus-period sample. Scholars continue to argue about timing, scale and the relationship between genetic and linguistic change, and those arguments are legitimate. What is not legitimate is presenting either national-political framing as the settled reading of the science. We set out what the studies report and who reports it; we do not adjudicate the politics.

## Decline, and the limits of the word

The end of the urban phase is described in the literature as a decline rather than a destruction, and the dates given depend, again, on what is being bounded. Explanations have been proposed for more than eighty years and none commands consensus. An article that offered you a single cause here would be telling you more than the evidence supports.

What can be said is that the cities ceased to function as large planned centres, that regional traditions continued afterwards, and that the population did not vanish — the genetics places its descendants squarely within later South Asian ancestry. That is a real historical outcome and it is not a collapse in the cinematic sense.
