Nobody can tell you how long Russian will take you, and anybody who does is selling something. What does exist is one published institutional figure, produced by an organisation that has taught the language to thousands of students under conditions it documents. Understanding exactly what that figure measures is more useful than any estimate someone could invent for your situation.

## The figure, and what it describes

The US Foreign Service Institute trains American diplomats in the languages they will work in, and it groups those languages by how long its students typically need to reach an integrated score of **3** on the Interagency Language Roundtable scale — the level ILR describes as **general professional proficiency**.

Russian is in **Category III**, at approximately **44 weeks of full-time instruction**, commonly stated as **1,012 class hours**.

Every part of that sentence is load-bearing:

- **Full-time.** FSI describes a typical week as roughly **23 hours of class plus 17 hours of self-study**. That is a 40-hour week, and the arithmetic is consistent: 44 weeks at 23 class hours is 1,012.
- **Class**, meaning small-group instruction with trained teachers, correcting you continuously.
- **Typically**, meaning an average across students who have been selected and are being paid to do this as their job.
- **ILR 3**, a professional standard — using the language for work, not ordering coffee.

Where Russian sits relative to its neighbours is the genuinely useful part:

| Category | Weeks | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | shortest | Spanish, French, Italian, Dutch |
| II | 36 | German, Malay, Indonesian, Swahili |
| III | 44 | Russian, Polish, Thai, Khmer, Vietnamese |
| IV | 88 | Mandarin, Cantonese, Japanese, Korean, Arabic |

Russian is demanding, and it is a **full category below** the hardest group — the Category IV figure is exactly double. If your mental model was that Russian sits alongside Mandarin in difficulty, FSI's own accounting disagrees.

## Why you should not divide

The obvious next move is arithmetic. Take 1,012 hours, divide by your daily study time, and read off an answer. At 30 minutes a day every day, a year gives you about 182 hours, and the division suggests something in the region of five and a half years.

That number is arithmetically correct and close to meaningless, for a reason worth being precise about: **FSI hours and app hours are not the same unit.**

An FSI hour is small-group instruction, with a trained teacher hearing every sentence you produce and correcting it immediately, inside a programme where the language is your full-time occupation for most of a year. A solo hour on a phone is genuinely valuable — retrieval practice, controlled listening, vocabulary that comes back — and it is a different thing. Neither is a fraction of the other in a way you can compute.

There is a second problem with the division: it assumes hours are the only variable. They are not. A learner who talks to Russian speakers weekly and a learner who never speaks aloud can log identical hours and end up in very different places.

So use the FSI figure the way it is genuinely reliable — as a **relative scale between languages** — and be sceptical of anyone converting it into a personal timeline, including yourself.

## Russian's curve has an unusual shape

More useful than a total is knowing where the effort lands, because Russian distributes it very unevenly.

### The alphabet is fast

Thirty-three letters. Most adults working consistently can read Russian slowly and accurately within about a fortnight, and can sound out signs and product names well before that. It is the fastest-completed stage of the whole language.

It also pays out immediately, because Russian carries a large stock of internationally recognisable vocabulary that becomes legible the moment you can decode the letters. That early win is worth engineering deliberately — it is the clearest possible evidence that the language is a solvable problem, and it arrives before the grammar has had a chance to discourage anyone.

The trap is treating the alphabet as the gate rather than the doorstep. Our guide to [the Russian Cyrillic alphabet](/blog/learn-russian-cyrillic-beginners-guide/) covers the letters worth over-practising, and the two things the alphabet does not give you: stress, which is unmarked in ordinary text, and vowel reduction, which means the spelling gets you to a word rather than to a pronunciation.

### The grammar is slow

This is where the time actually goes.

**Six cases**, marking the role each noun plays, with adjectives, pronouns and numerals agreeing. The endings are learnable in an afternoon and the *reflex* — choosing a form while you are mid-sentence, without stopping — takes months. That gap between knowing and using is the defining feature of the Russian curve, and it is the main reason honest time estimates for Russian run longer than the modest letter count suggests.

**Verb aspect**, which most English speakers find harder than the cases. Russian verbs largely come in pairs, and choosing between them is a decision about how you view an action rather than when it happened. There is no English structure to map it onto, so there is nothing to transfer and nothing to guess from.

**Three genders and agreement**, which means gender is not a label you can postpone; it determines which endings a noun takes.

### The vocabulary is moderate

Better than it looks. Russian shares almost no everyday vocabulary with English, which sounds bad — but the internationalism layer is large, word formation is highly regular once you know the prefixes and suffixes, and knowing one root frequently gives you a family of related words. Compared with an unrelated language that has no shared borrowings at all, Russian vocabulary is not the bottleneck.

### And some things are simply absent

**No articles.** Nothing corresponding to *a* or *the*, at all.

**No present tense of "to be".** It is normally just omitted, so a large number of sentences are shorter than their English equivalents.

**Free word order**, because the case endings carry the roles. You are rarely wrong for arranging a sentence unusually.

## Milestones as a sequence, not a schedule

The order in which things happen in Russian is predictable. The pace is not, and it depends on factors nobody can see from here — how much you already know about grammar, whether you have studied a Slavic language, how much contact you have with speakers, how consistently you show up.

So here is the sequence, with no times attached, because attaching times to it would be inventing them.

**You can decode the alphabet.** Signs, brand names, place names. Slow and accurate.

**You can read internationalisms without effort** and start recognising word shapes rather than spelling them out letter by letter.

**You can produce fixed phrases correctly**, including the polite words that are heavily reduced in ordinary speech and do not sound like their spelling.

**You can build a sentence with a subject and an object** and get the accusative right. This is the moment cases become real rather than theoretical.

**You can say where you are and where you are going**, which means you have the в/на motion-versus-location distinction working — arguably the single highest-value grammar point in early Russian.

**You can be understood while making case errors.** An underrated milestone. Russians are used to hearing the language spoken imperfectly and the errors are usually recoverable from context.

**You stop translating in your head for common situations.**

**You can read a paragraph of unmodified text** — a news lead, a product description — and get the gist without a dictionary.

**Aspect starts feeling like a choice rather than a coin flip.** This one arrives late and is the clearest sign that the grammar is becoming internal.

Progress in Russian is not linear, and the plateau between "I can build sentences" and "I can build them without stopping" is long enough that a lot of people conclude they have stopped improving. They usually have not; they have moved from acquiring rules to automating them, which produces much less visible change per week.

## What actually changes the rate

Four things, roughly in order of impact.

**Whether you speak aloud with people.** The single biggest divergence between learners with identical study hours. Production under real-time pressure is the skill; everything else supports it.

**Whether you learned stress with each word.** Russian stress is mobile and unmarked in ordinary text. Learners who treat it as optional accumulate hundreds of words stored with the wrong stress, and repairing those later is far more expensive than storing them correctly.

**Whether you reviewed, or only advanced.** Case endings, gender assignments and stress patterns are exactly the kind of material that decays quietly and then fails under speaking pressure. Review that pulls from your own history, rather than fresh lists, is what keeps that from happening.

**Prior languages.** Any previous experience with a case-marked language — German, Latin, Polish, Czech — reduces the conceptual load considerably, because the idea of a case is already there and only the specifics are new.

## A note on the other scale you will see

If you look into Russian courses you will meet **CEFR** levels — A1 through C2 — maintained by the Council of Europe. That framework describes what a learner can *do* at each level rather than how long it took, which makes it useful for a different purpose: judging whether a course, a textbook or a reader is pitched where you actually are.

FSI answers "how long for our students". CEFR answers "what does this level look like". Neither answers "how long for you", and mixing the two produces most of the confident timelines you will find online.

## The honest answer

Russian takes longer than Spanish and half as long as Mandarin, by the only public institutional accounting that exists. The alphabet takes a fortnight, the case system takes months to automate, and verb aspect takes longer than either. The FSI figure of 44 weeks and 1,012 class hours is real, it is checkable, and it describes full-time professional training rather than anything you are about to do.

The useful question is not "how long" but "how consistently". A learner doing twenty focused minutes daily for two years will pass a learner doing four-hour weekend sessions for the same period, and both will be passed by someone with a weekly conversation partner.
