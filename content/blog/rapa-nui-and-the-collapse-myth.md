Rapa Nui is the most widely used parable in popular history: a society that cut down its own forest, wrecked its food supply and collapsed, offered as a warning about the planet. Almost every element of that parable is now contested in the peer-reviewed literature — and the part of the island's demographic history that is not contested at all is the part the parable leaves out, which is what happened after Europeans arrived.

This article separates three things: what is documented, what is argued about, and what is plainly attested and routinely omitted. Rapa Nui is the island; Rapanui are its people, who are living today and whose researchers and institutions are now co-authors on the studies described below.

## What is not in dispute

The island lost its palm forest. Pollen records from lake and mire sediments show palm pollen being replaced by grass pollen, and the palaeoecological literature treats the deforestation itself as established.

Nor is the island's difficulty in dispute. It covers about 164 square kilometres, lies over 2,000 km from the nearest inhabited island and over 3,700 km from the South American mainland, and has limited soil productivity and few freshwater sources.

And the monumental record is real. UNESCO describes a society of Polynesian origin that established a powerful and original tradition of monumental sculpture and architecture, building shrines and erecting the stone figures known as *moai* between the tenth and sixteenth centuries.

## What is argued about

The ecocide account, as summarised in a 2016 integrative overview in *Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution*, runs as follows: settlers arriving between about 800 and 1200 CE overexploited the island's resources, causing an ecological catastrophe that led to cultural collapse. The main evidence was the abrupt replacement of palm pollen by grass pollen in the sediments of Raraku, Kao and Aroi, read as thorough deforestation between roughly 1200 and 1400–1600 CE. That overview is explicit that this view became widely accepted not only among scientists but in the wider public, helped by popularisation in the mass media.

The competing account in the same overview holds that colonisation came later, between about 1200 and 1300, that deforestation was not completed until around 1650, and that it was substantially driven by rats carried to the island by the first settlers eating palm fruit — and, crucially, that the cultural collapse did not occur until after European arrival in 1722.

Two more recent lines of evidence have pushed hard against the pre-European collapse.

A 2021 paper in *Nature Communications* applied Approximate Bayesian Computation to the radiocarbon and palaeoenvironmental record and reported population resilience rather than crash. A 2024 paper in *Science Advances* used shortwave infrared satellite imagery and machine learning to produce an island-wide estimate of rock gardening — the lithic mulching that made cultivation viable — and found the extent of that agricultural infrastructure substantially less than previously claimed, concluding it likely could not have supported the large population sizes that had been assumed. If the island never held a large population, there was no large population to collapse.

Then the genomes. A 2024 study in *Nature* sequenced 15 ancient Rapanui individuals, radiocarbon dated to between 1670 and 1950 CE. Through effective population size reconstruction and extensive simulation, the authors reject a scenario involving a severe population bottleneck during the 1600s, as proposed by the ecocide theory. The same study found that ancient and present-day Rapanui carry similar proportions of Native American ancestry, around 10 per cent, and estimated that admixture event at about 1250–1430 CE using a Bayesian model combining genetic and radiocarbon evidence.

Note what is and is not settled by that. Contact with the Americas before Europeans arrived is supported. Who travelled which way, and in what circumstances, is not answered by ancestry proportions alone.

## What the parable omits

The demographic catastrophe on Rapa Nui is documented, and it is colonial.

The 2024 *Nature* study states the sequence directly: Europeans reached the island in 1722; over the following years European visitors killed local inhabitants and introduced deadly pathogens to which the islanders had never been exposed; in the 1860s Peruvian slave raiders kidnapped a third of the population, with only a few repatriated after international condemnation of the slaving practices; and a subsequent smallpox outbreak reduced the Rapanui population to an estimated 110 individuals.

That is not an aside to the collapse story. It is the collapse, it has named perpetrators, and it happened to people whose descendants live on the island now. A popular account that spends its attention on whether islanders felled their own trees, and treats mass kidnapping and epidemic death as background, has chosen where to look.

The same study notes that its finding — that the ancient individuals are Polynesian in origin and most closely related to present-day Rapanui — will contribute to repatriation efforts, which is a reminder that the remains involved are in overseas collections, and that the research has stakes for a living community.

## A dating discrepancy worth seeing

Readers checking these claims will hit an immediate conflict. UNESCO's inscription text says the island was settled about 300 CE. The recent genomic and radiocarbon literature places Polynesian arrival at around 1250 CE.

Neither source is careless. The UNESCO text reflects the chronology accepted when the property was inscribed; the later date reflects systematic re-dating of Polynesian settlement across the Pacific using stricter sample criteria. Institutional descriptions are durable and slow to revise, which is precisely why they should be read with a date attached — the habit set out in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/).

## Why this matters beyond one island

Collapse is an unusually attractive shape for a story, and Rapa Nui shows what that attraction costs.

The Rapa Nui case now sits alongside the argument over [what caused the Bronze Age collapse](/blog/what-caused-the-bronze-age-collapse/) and the long dispute over [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/) as a case where a vivid single-cause narrative outran the evidence and proved very hard to dislodge. In all three, the correction has come from unglamorous work — sediment cores, satellite survey, radiocarbon modelling, genome sequencing — and in all three the resulting picture is slower, messier and less quotable.

## The honest summary

The forest went. The population probably never reached the size the collapse story requires. There is no genomic support for a seventeenth-century crash. And the catastrophe that is documented beyond argument was caused by killing, kidnapping and smallpox after 1722.
