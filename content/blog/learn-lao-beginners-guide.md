**The hardest thing about learning Lao is not Lao.** It is that there is very little to learn it from. Widely taught languages have shelves of graded readers, hours of structured audio and a choice of courses; Lao has a fraction of that.

That scarcity shapes everything about a sensible study plan, and it is worth naming before anything about grammar.

## What you are dealing with

**Script.** Lao is written in its own abugida — a system where each consonant carries an inherent vowel that is modified by attached marks. It is written **without spaces between words**, so decoding and segmenting are one skill rather than two. [The Lao alphabet explained](/blog/lao-alphabet-explained/) covers the system in detail.

**Tone.** Lao is a tonal language. The tone of a syllable is determined by the interaction of the consonant class, the vowel length and any tone mark, rather than by a mark alone. This is the same architecture Thai uses, and it is the part that takes longest to internalise.

**Grammar.** Comparatively simple. No verb conjugation for person or tense, no grammatical gender, no case system. Word order, particles and classifiers do the work. Once reading is established, sentence construction moves quickly.

**Vocabulary.** Substantially shared with Thai, and with a layer of Pali and Sanskrit vocabulary in formal registers, as is common across mainland Southeast Asia.

## Why the script comes first

The temptation with an under-resourced language is to work from romanisation, because romanised material is easier to find. This is a trap, for a specific reason:

**Romanisation of Lao is not standardised.** Different sources transliterate the same word differently. A learner working from romanisation is learning several conflicting spellings of every word and has no way to tell which is right.

The script is the only stable representation. Learning it early means every subsequent resource — including inconsistent ones — becomes checkable.

The practical cost is a few weeks. The benefit is that everything after those weeks is usable.

## A realistic first month

**Week 1 — Consonants and the first vowels.**
Learn characters in small batches with daily review. Do not attempt the whole inventory at once. Alongside this, learn about ten survival phrases by ear, so you have something to say.

**Week 2 — Vowels, tone marks, and how tone is determined.**
This is the conceptual centre of the language. Understand *that* tone comes from a combination of factors before trying to memorise which combination gives which tone.

**Week 3 — High-frequency vocabulary, in script.**
Greetings, family, food, numbers, transport, directions. Write everything in Lao script, not in romanisation, from the start.

**Week 4 — Sentence patterns and listening.**
Basic word order, questions, negation, classifiers. Start listening to connected speech even if you understand very little; segmenting spoken Lao is a skill that only develops with exposure.

## The specific problem of connected speech

Because Lao is written without spaces, learners often assume the difficulty is visual. It is also auditory: spoken Lao does not pause between words either, and a beginner hears an undifferentiated stream.

The fix is the same in both cases — **exposure to material you partly understand.** Listening to speech that is 90% incomprehensible builds very little. Listening to speech where you can catch a word here and there builds segmentation quickly. This is the practical argument for comprehensible input, covered in [comprehensible input explained](/blog/comprehensible-input-explained/).

## Working with scarce resources

Three habits that matter more for Lao than for a well-resourced language:

**1. Reuse material.** With few resources, re-reading and re-listening to the same content is not a failure of variety — it is the efficient use of what exists. Repeated exposure to a text you have partly decoded is how it becomes fully decoded.

**2. Build your own review.** Because graded material is scarce, you will need to convert what you have into practice yourself: turning a phrase list into recall practice, a dialogue into dictation.

**3. Find speakers early.** For a language with limited materials, contact with speakers moves from "helpful" to "necessary" much sooner. Lao communities exist well outside Laos, and Lao speakers are generally pleased to meet a learner, precisely because there are so few.
