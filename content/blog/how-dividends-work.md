A dividend is the most direct way a company shares its profits with its owners. Investor.gov defines it as a portion of a company's profit paid to shareholders, adding that companies that pay dividends usually do so on a fixed schedule but can pay them at any time, and that unscheduled payments are called special or extra dividends. Understanding the handful of dates involved explains who gets paid, and why buying a stock just to collect its dividend is not a free gain.

## The four dates

| Date | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Declaration date | The board announces the dividend: the amount per share, the record date and the payment date |
| Ex-dividend date | Set by exchange rules: buyers from this day on do not receive the dividend |
| Record date | The company checks its books to see who is a shareholder |
| Payment date | The dividend is paid to holders of record |

Investor.gov explains the key one. Once the company sets the record date, the ex-dividend date is set by stock exchange rules, and for stocks it is **usually the record date itself, or one business day before if the record date is not a business day**. If you buy on the ex-dividend date or later, the seller gets the dividend; if you buy before it, you do.

That timing fits the one-business-day [T+1 settlement cycle](/blog/trade-settlement-t-plus-1/): a purchase made the business day before the record date settles on the record date.

## A worked example from Investor.gov

On 2 March 2026, Company XYZ declares a dividend payable on 17 March 2026 to shareholders of record on 16 March, a Monday.

- **Ex-dividend date:** Monday 16 March, the record date.
- **Buy on Friday 13 March or earlier:** you receive the dividend.
- **Buy on 16 March or later:** the seller receives it.

If the record date had been Sunday 15 March instead, the ex-dividend date would move back one business day, to Friday 13 March.

## Why the price may drop on the ex-date

On the ex-dividend date, a new buyer no longer gets the upcoming payment, and the company is about to hand out cash. Investor.gov notes that with a significant dividend, the price of a stock may fall by that amount on the ex-dividend date. So a $1 dividend on a $50 stock does not make a shareholder $1 richer overnight: the shareholder ends up with roughly a $49 share plus $1 of cash, before taxes. Other market movements on the day can hide this effect.

That is why "buying the dividend" just before the ex-date and selling straight after is not a reliable gain. Dividends are also generally taxable in a taxable account, as is any gain or loss on the shares; [capital gains on stocks](/blog/capital-gains-tax-on-stocks/) covers the US rules for gains.

## Special cases

- **Very large dividends.** If a dividend is 25% or more of the stock's value, Investor.gov explains that special rules apply: the ex-dividend date is deferred until one business day after the dividend is paid.
- **Stock dividends.** A company may pay in additional shares, or shares of a subsidiary being spun off. For these, the ex-date is generally set at the first business day after the stock dividend is paid. If you sell before then, you may be obliged to deliver the extra shares to the buyer.

## Who pays dividends, and how reliable they are

Investor.gov's stocks guide distinguishes **income stocks**, which pay dividends consistently, from **growth stocks**, which rarely pay them because they reinvest earnings. Even consistent payers can cut or suspend a dividend, and common stock dividends are never promised. Preferred stockholders, the same guide notes, receive dividends before common holders do; [what is a stock](/blog/what-is-a-stock/) explains the difference.

Some companies offer **dividend reinvestment plans**, which use your dividends to buy more shares automatically; Investor.gov advises checking whether there is a fee.

## Dividends and splits

A stock split changes the number of shares but not the value of the company, so the dividend per share falls in proportion. Investor.gov's example: after a two-for-one split, dividends paid per share also fall proportionately. [Stock splits](/blog/stock-splits-explained/) covers the mechanics. To compare dividends between companies, investors use the yield, the annual dividend as a percentage of the price, explained, with its pitfalls, in [dividend yield](/blog/dividend-yield-explained/).
