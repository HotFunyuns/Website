Partition divided British India into two independent Dominions with effect from 15 August 1947, and the boundary between them was not published until 17 August. That two-day gap is not a curiosity. Millions of people were deciding whether to stay or flee without knowing which country their village would be in.

What followed was mass killing and one of the largest forced migrations in recorded history. How large, and how many died, is genuinely unsettled — and the reasons the numbers diverge are worth understanding before any figure is quoted.

## What the law set out

The Indian Independence Act 1947 was enacted on 18 July 1947. Its first section provides that "as from the fifteenth day of August, nineteen hundred and forty-seven, two independent Dominions shall be set up in India, to be known respectively as India and Pakistan", with that date defined as "the appointed day".

Section 2 allocates territory. India comprised the territories under British sovereignty that were part of British India immediately before the appointed day, minus the areas assigned to Pakistan. Pakistan comprised East Bengal and West Punjab, Sind and British Baluchistan, and the North West Frontier Province — the last conditional on a referendum. The Act allowed subsequent territorial adjustment by mutual consent, and provided for the Indian States to accede to one Dominion or the other.

Two features of that text matter. It creates two states on the same date, though Pakistan today marks 14 August. And it delegates the actual line to a process that had not finished when the Act was passed.

## The compressed timetable

The National Archives' account of the surviving official papers sets out the sequence. The Cripps Mission of 1942 proposed post-war independence in exchange for wartime cooperation. The Cabinet Mission of 1946, sent by Attlee's Labour government to discuss the shape of an independent India, failed to reach agreement. Once withdrawal was decided, Lord Mountbatten advanced the departure date from June 1948 to August 1947.

The partition plan was announced by Mountbatten on 3 June 1947, in a radio broadcast; the Partition Museum dates the public revelation of the scheme to 4 June, which is the press conference the following day. Sir Cyril Radcliffe, a London barrister, was given roughly six weeks to draw boundaries in Punjab and Bengal with local advisers. The Radcliffe award was announced on 17 August.

Pakistan emerged as two separate territories — West and East, the latter becoming Bangladesh — because the populations the plan was built around were not contiguous. The Partition Museum records particular outcomes of the line: three tehsils of Gurdaspur district went to India while Shakargarh went to Pakistan, and Lahore was awarded to Pakistan.

Speed is not an excuse and it is not an explanation, but it is a documented condition. A boundary drawn in six weeks and published two days after the states existed produced a period in which no one on the ground had authoritative information.

## Why the numbers diverge

This is the part most summaries skip, and it is where intellectual honesty is most required.

Three reputable institutions give three different figures for deaths. The Partition Museum states that estimates suggest somewhere between 200,000 and one million people lost their lives, and adds that no accurate accounts exist. The National Archives' education account says approximately one million died. The 1947 Partition Archive, which has recorded over 12,000 oral testimonies from witnesses since 2008, states that nearly two million died in the communal violence.

Displacement figures diverge similarly. The National Archives gives ten million displaced. The Partition Museum estimates up to twenty million people affected, and documents continuing movement well beyond 1947 — roughly a million Sindhi Hindus migrating to India in the first half of 1948, and around 1.5 million people crossing the Bengal border between February and April 1950. The 1947 Partition Archive puts the total uprooted through the 1950s at up to twenty-five million, which it notes was about one per cent of the world's population at the time.

These are not contradictions so much as different questions. Deaths in communal violence, deaths from disease and exposure during flight, people displaced in the weeks around independence, and people displaced across the following decade are four distinct quantities. There was no functioning enumeration during the violence; records were destroyed or never created; and people were moving in both directions across borders that were themselves new. Understanding how any historical number is produced is the subject of our guide to [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/), and the same scrutiny applies to counts as to dates.

Sexual violence was widespread and is documented rather than inferred: The National Archives records that over 75,000 women were subjected to it. That figure, like the others, rests on incomplete records, and the scale is not in doubt even where the count is.

## Three national accounts, and why we do not adjudicate

Indian, Pakistani and Bangladeshi historiographies differ fundamentally on responsibility — on whether partition was the inevitable outcome of a two-nation logic, a failure of negotiation, a British strategy of withdrawal on the cheapest terms, or a consequence of decisions taken by particular parties and individuals. Each position is held by serious scholars, is supported by archival argument, and is entangled with the political identity of a state.

The responsible approach for a general article is to say who holds which position and on what grounds, and to leave the adjudication to readers working through the scholarship of more than one country. What should not happen — and is common in popular accounts — is that one national narrative is presented as the neutral background and the others as partisan.

It also matters that no religious community is collectively responsible for the violence. Killing was carried out by, and against, Hindus, Muslims and Sikhs, in different proportions in different districts. Accounts that assign agency to a community rather than to organised groups and individuals are doing politics, not history.

## Testimony as evidence

The 1947 Partition Archive's 12,000-plus recorded memories represent a deliberate response to the documentary problem: where official records are thin, testimony from people who were there becomes central. Oral history has its own methodological requirements — it is shaped by memory, later framing, and the circumstances of the interview — and it is evidence, not anecdote, when collected and handled properly.
