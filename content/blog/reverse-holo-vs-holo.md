Hold the card under a lamp and tilt it. If the **picture** shines, it is a holo. If the picture stays flat and everything **around** the picture shines, it is a reverse holo.

That is the whole identification test, and it takes two seconds. The reason it is worth knowing is downstream: the two printings are physically different cards that usually carry the same set number, price separately, and get confused by any lookup that goes by number alone.

## The two printings, precisely

**Holo Rare.** A Rare card that has a holographic illustration. The foil is *in the artwork*.

**Reverse Holo.** A card of any rarity with holographic texture on the card surface *except* the main illustration. The foil is everywhere *but* the artwork.

The word "Rare" in the first definition is doing real work. Holo Rare is a rarity sub-group. Reverse holo is a *treatment* applied across rarities — which is why you can hold a reverse holo Common, and why a shiny frame around an unremarkable card is normal rather than an error.

Rarity itself is marked on the card. In English-language Pokémon sets, rarity is indicated by a shape in the bottom corner: a circle for Common, a diamond for Uncommon, a star for Rare. Japanese-edition cards use letters instead, from C and U through R, RR, SR and UR.

The pack-composition row is worth reading carefully, because it is the closest thing to an answer about relative scarcity and it does not settle the value question. A standard ten-card Pokémon booster has historically been described as five Commons, three Uncommons, a reverse holographic card of any rarity, and sometimes a Rare; from the Scarlet and Violet series onward each pack contains one holographic card as well as two reverse holographic cards.

That tells you something about how often each treatment appears in packs. It tells you nothing reliable about price.

## Why "which is worth more" has no general answer

This is the question the comparison is usually asked in service of, and the honest response is that the printing type does not determine the price.

Card value depends on rarity, on the card's popularity within competitive play, on its age, on how many were printed, and on various other factors — and lower-rarity cards can be worth more than higher-rarity ones. A reverse holo of a card people want can outprice a holo of a card nobody plays. The reverse happens too.

So the correct procedure is not to reason from the printing to the price. It is to identify the printing, then look up a completed-sales figure for *that* printing, on the marketplace you would actually use, on the day you care about.

This is a genuinely unsatisfying answer and it is the only one that survives contact with the market.

## The identification problem, and why apps have to ask

Because the two printings usually share a set number, an identifier alone cannot separate them. Marketplaces solve this by splitting listings by variant. Price references do the same.

An image-matching scanner runs into the same wall from the other direction: the artwork, the name, the set symbol and the number are all identical between the printings. The thing that differs is a reflective surface treatment, which is exactly the property a phone camera renders least reliably — foil under a room light can look matte in one photograph and blown out in the next.

[TCG Card Grading Scanner Value](/apps/tcg-card-grading-scanner/) handles that by not pretending to have solved it. Normal, holo and reverse holo copies of the same card are priced separately, you choose which variant you are holding, and the selected variant stays visible with the result rather than being collapsed into one average figure.

That last part matters more than it sounds. A single averaged price across three printings is a number that is wrong for all three, and it is wrong in a way that is invisible — there is nothing on screen to tell you an assumption was made. Keeping the variant attached to the result means the figure is either right for what you are holding or obviously attached to the wrong thing.

The cost of that design is a tap. You have to tell the app which printing it is looking at, every time, and there is no clever inference doing it for you. We would rather add the tap than average away the distinction the whole market is built on.

## What this changes for the rest of the process

Identification comes first because everything downstream inherits it.

**Pricing.** Covered above: a variant-blind figure is not a figure.

**Condition assessment.** Foil surfaces show handling differently from matte ones, and a reverse holo puts foil across most of the card. The condition scales do not change, but where flaws become visible does — the scales themselves are set out in [card condition scales](/blog/card-condition-grading-scales/).

## The two-second habit worth building

Every time you pick up a card you intend to record, sell or submit: tilt it once under a light, note whether the artwork or the frame catches, and check the rarity symbol in the corner.

It is the cheapest step in the entire process and the one that everything else depends on. Collectible card games have been printing multiple treatments of the same card for decades, and the market has long since organised itself around the distinction. The only party who can fail to notice it is the person holding the card.
