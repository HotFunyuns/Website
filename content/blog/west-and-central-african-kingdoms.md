Benin, Oyo, Kongo, Asante and Sokoto were five distinct states with five distinct constitutions, economies, religions and lifespans. One was a Christian monarchy in Central Africa documented continuously from 1483. One was an Islamic confederation founded in the nineteenth century. One left a walled city of nearly 3,000 hectares. Treating them as a single category called "precolonial Africa" throws away everything worth knowing about each of them.

This article takes them one at a time, anchors each to physical evidence, and then deals directly with the thing they had in common: all five met the Atlantic or trans-Saharan slave economies, in different roles.

## Benin: the kingdom whose art was taken

The Kingdom of Benin, in what is now Edo State, Nigeria, is known to most people outside Nigeria through objects held in European and American museums. How those objects got there is not in dispute.

The Museum am Rothenbaum in Hamburg, which leads the Digital Benin project, states it plainly: the colonial occupation of the Kingdom of Benin by British troops in February 1897 led to a worldwide dispersal of an estimated 3,000 to 5,000 objects plundered from the royal palace and other ceremonial sites. The works — in bronze, ivory and wood, commonly called the Benin bronzes — are at the centre of the public debate on restitution of colonial heritage.

Digital Benin exists because the plunder scattered a coherent body of material across a multitude of institutions, making it impossible for anyone to see the whole. The project, funded with more than €1.2 million by the Ernst von Siemens Kunststiftung and directed by Barbara Plankensteiner with German, Nigerian, European and American specialists, set out to reunite the object data digitally. Its own framing is careful: prospective restitutions can help mitigate the tragic loss of local knowledge and cultural values, but there is also a need to make existing knowledge resources accessible.

Digitising a catalogue is not restitution, and nobody involved says it is. The restitution question remains live, and it is being decided institution by institution and government by government.

## Oyo: a city measured in kilometres of wall

Oyo was a Yoruba state in what is now south-western Nigeria, and its old capital gives some of the clearest surviving evidence for how large a West African city could be.

UNESCO's tentative listing for Old Oyo describes a site deserted around 1837. The old palace compound and a former water reservoir lie within a 200-hectare area enclosed by a wall 7.5 kilometres long. Beyond that, two concentric outer walls with ditches — 18 and 28 kilometres long — enclose nearly 3,000 hectares. About a third of that area is covered by the ruins of mud buildings; another third carries scatters of pottery sherds, presumed to mark lighter, more temporary housing. Guard houses stood in the wall gateways, and the site preserves wells, cisterns and grinding hollows. A further 16-kilometre wall loop encloses another 2,000 hectares.

Those numbers describe a large, defended, densely used urban landscape with a planned water supply — and record its end, a capital abandoned in the 1830s amid regional warfare and political fragmentation.

## Kongo: a Christian monarchy with five centuries of paperwork

Mbanza Kongo, on a plateau at 570 metres in what is now Angola, was the political and spiritual capital of the Kingdom of Kongo, which UNESCO describes as active from the fourteenth to the nineteenth century and as one of the largest constituted states of the region.

It was already prosperous when the Portuguese arrived in the fifteenth century. To the existing town, built in local materials, the Portuguese added stone buildings in European construction methods, including several churches. UNESCO records the subsequent expansion of Christianity alongside the Westernisation of local elites — and adds, carefully, that this happened "without however renouncing its culture." The kingdom kept the ritual and symbolic powers embodied in the brotherhood of the Leopard Ngo while adopting Christianity.

What makes Kongo unusual for a historian is the paper trail. UNESCO notes documentation available covering five centuries, from 1483 to the present. That is an extraordinary depth of written record for any state, and it means Kongo can be studied through correspondence, treaties and church records rather than only through archaeology.

It also means the hardest fact about Kongo is documented rather than inferred. UNESCO states that the Kingdom of Kongo was at the centre of the most important route for the trade in enslaved persons, who were deported to the Americas and the Caribbean — and then notes that no material vestige attesting to the slave trade has yet been found at the site. A trade that removed millions of people left, at its central node, almost nothing for an archaeologist to excavate. That absence is itself a finding.

## Asante: a state still visible in its shrines

The Asante kingdom, centred on Kumasi in what is now Ghana, reached its peak in the eighteenth century, in UNESCO's assessment, and remained influential from the late eighteenth to the late nineteenth centuries.

What survives on the World Heritage List is a group of ten shrine buildings near Kumasi — Abirim, Asawase, Asenemaso, Bodwease, Ejisu Besease, Adarko Jachie, Edwenase, Kentinkrono, Patakro and Saaman — arranged around courtyards and built of timber, bamboo and mud plaster with originally thatched roofs. Their walls carry bold bas-relief decoration: spirals, arabesques, animals, birds and plants, linked to traditional Adinkra symbols. UNESCO is explicit that these designs are not merely ornamental but carry symbolic meanings tied to Asante ideas and beliefs.

The shrines are not ruins. Traditional religion is still practised in them, which is why they have been maintained complete with their symbolic features. That continuity is part of the reason UNESCO inscribed them: they are the last surviving examples of a significant architectural tradition that is also a living one.

## Sokoto: a nineteenth-century Islamic confederation

Sokoto is the latest of the five and the one whose institutions are closest to living memory. The standard account is that a reform movement led by Usman dan Fodio in the early nineteenth century reorganised the Hausa states of what is now northern Nigeria into a caliphate governed as a confederation of emirates, which was absorbed into British Northern Nigeria in the early twentieth century.

What can be checked against a heritage record is the urban world the caliphate inherited. UNESCO's tentative listing for the Ancient Kano City Walls describes a 14-kilometre-radius earth structure whose foundations were laid under Sarki Gijimasu between 1095 and 1134, completed in the middle of the fourteenth century and extended to their present position in the sixteenth. Kurmi Market, laid out in lanes by trade, exchanged North African and European goods for domestic ones through trans-Saharan commerce. The Emir's Palace was built by Muhammadu Rumfa, who ruled Kano from 1463 to 1499.

Kano was an old walled commercial city with centuries of institutional depth before the caliphate absorbed it. Sokoto did not build northern Nigeria's urban system; it reorganised one that already existed.

## The part that cannot be skipped

All five states intersected with the trade in enslaved people, and the precise role differed in each case.

Kongo sat at the centre of a route that deported people to the Americas, as UNESCO states directly. In the Bight of Biafra, UNESCO's tentative listing for the Arochukwu cave temple complex — listed under the name "Slave Route" — describes an oracular shrine where those found guilty "walked into dark tunnels", a hill of rags where the condemned left their clothes, and a river coloured red to signal to relatives that a victim had died. The mechanism described is the disappearance of people into a trade under the cover of religious judgement.

Two errors are worth naming. The first exculpates European and American demand: the Atlantic system existed because plantations across the Americas paid for it. The second erases African agency: African rulers, merchants and raiders made choices within that system, and some of those choices were to supply it. Both errors serve somebody's politics. Neither is history.

The trans-Saharan trade, which moved enslaved people for far longer than the Atlantic one, belongs in the same paragraph. It is routinely left out of accounts of the Sahel that dwell on gold and manuscripts.

Reading this material well requires the distinctions set out in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/), because much of what reaches a general reader about these states was written by people with a stake in how they were described — traders, missionaries, colonial officials and, later, national governments.

That is the limit of what it should be used for here. The app's content carries no citations and no external scholarly review, and its own disclaimer says it is not a substitute for textbooks, peer-reviewed scholarship or primary sources. For a subject where the sources are contested and the stakes are current, go to UNESCO's [General History of Africa](https://www.unesco.org/en/general-history-africa), to Nigerian, Ghanaian and Angolan institutions, and to the project catalogues such as Digital Benin that are assembling the dispersed record.

A last framing note. Readers arriving from European history often look for the collapse narrative discussed in [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/) and find instead states conquered on a schedule set elsewhere. Oyo's capital emptied in the 1830s for internal reasons; Benin fell to a British expedition in 1897; Sokoto was absorbed shortly after. Three different endings, only one of them a story about internal decline.
