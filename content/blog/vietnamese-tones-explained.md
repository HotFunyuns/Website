**Standard northern Vietnamese is usually described as having six tones.** Many southern varieties merge two of them, so descriptions of southern speech commonly count five. Both numbers are correct about different varieties, which is why sources appear to disagree.

## The six tones and their marks

Five tones are written with a diacritic. The sixth — the level tone — is marked by having no diacritic at all, which is an important detail: **an unmarked syllable is not "toneless", it carries the level tone.**

| Vietnamese name | Mark | Written example | Common description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ngang | (none) | a | Level, mid |
| huyền | grave | à | Falling |
| hỏi | hook above | ả | Dipping, then rising |
| ngã | tilde | ã | Rising with interruption |
| sắc | acute | á | Rising |
| nặng | dot below | ạ | Low, short, constricted |

The descriptions in the right-hand column are approximations, and they vary between references. Treat them as a starting map rather than as a specification.

## Two tones are not only about pitch

This is the point most beginner explanations skip, and it is why learners who master the pitch contours still sound wrong.

**Ngã** and **nặng** are commonly described as involving a change in voice quality — a creaky or glottalised element — rather than a pure pitch movement. A learner producing only the pitch shape produces something recognisable and not quite right.

There is no way to convey this in writing, which is a limitation of any article including this one. It is learned by listening.

## How tone marks combine with vowel marks

Vietnamese uses diacritics for two independent jobs:

1. **Vowel quality** — marks such as the circumflex, breve and horn distinguish vowels (a / â / ă, o / ô / ơ, u / ư).
2. **Tone** — the five marks above.

A single syllable can carry both. That is why Vietnamese requires such a large set of precomposed characters, and it is why the [Latin Extended Additional Unicode block](https://www.unicode.org/charts/PDF/U1E00.pdf) is largely devoted to Vietnamese.

The practical consequence for a learner: **a vowel mark and a tone mark are different things, and confusing them produces a different word rather than a slightly different sound.**

## The regional difference, honestly stated

The most commonly described north–south difference is the merger of **hỏi** and **ngã** in many southern varieties. There are other differences too, in consonants as well as tones.

Two implications:

- **Materials are not interchangeable.** A course teaching northern pronunciation and a speaker from the south will not line up perfectly.
- **Neither is more correct.** Northern speech is the basis of most standard descriptions; that is a convention about description, not a judgement about speech.

If you are learning for a specific family, region or workplace, the accent of the people you will actually speak to matters more than which variety a textbook chose.

## Practising without a teacher

The order that works:

**1. Perception before production.** Listen to the same syllable in different tones repeatedly and try to identify which you heard. Do this until you are right most of the time. Producing a distinction you cannot reliably hear is guesswork.

**2. Minimal pairs.** Two words differing only in tone are the most efficient practice material, because they isolate exactly the variable you are training.

**3. Whole syllables, not tone shapes.** Practise real words rather than abstract contours. The goal is recognising tone in speech, not performing a diagram.

**4. Then get feedback.** This is the step no app replaces. You cannot hear your own production the way a listener does, and a fluent speaker telling you which tone you actually produced is worth more than any amount of solo drilling.
