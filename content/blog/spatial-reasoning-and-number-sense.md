Spatial reasoning is the manipulation of shape, position and orientation in the mind: rotating an object to see whether it matches another, folding a flat net into a solid, working out what a cut through a shape would reveal. Number sense is something else — an intuition for magnitude, relationships between quantities, and whether an answer is the right size. The two are correlated, they are not the same, and no amount of arithmetic drill has been shown to move the spatial one. This article exists so that nothing else on this site implies otherwise.

## The tasks that define the construct

Spatial ability is usually treated as a family rather than a single capacity, because performance on its members does not move in lockstep. Four task types do most of the measuring.

**Mental rotation** shows two figures and asks whether one is a rotated version of the other, or a mirror image. **Paper folding** describes a sheet folded several times and punched, and asks where the holes fall when it is unfolded. **Form assembly** shows components and asks which arrangement produces a target shape. **Perspective taking** asks what a scene looks like from a different position.

Employer diagrammatic and inductive tests borrow from this family, which is why they are often grouped with it. The item-construction side of that is covered in [how abstract reasoning items are built](/blog/abstract-reasoning-tests-explained/), and the fairness claims attached to shape-based formats in [nonverbal reasoning tests](/blog/nonverbal-reasoning-tests-explained/).

One caution travels with all of these. Research in cross-cultural testing has found that some of the most consistent group differences appear on tests of basic visuoperception and on executive tasks using visual stimuli — the tasks widely assumed to be culture-neutral. Familiarity with two-dimensional conventions for representing three-dimensional objects is learned, and it varies with schooling.

## The relationship with mathematics is real, and moderate

This is where popular writing overshoots. A review of the connection across development reports "a moderate correlation between the two factors (rs = 0.50–0.53), even when controlling for verbal ability, suggesting that although the spatial and mathematical domains are distinct, there is a significant relation between these domains."

Read that carefully. A correlation around 0.5 is substantial — it is a genuine, replicated relationship, not a statistical curiosity. It also leaves roughly three-quarters of the variance in each domain unexplained by the other. Two people with the same spatial score can differ widely in mathematics, and the reverse.

The relationship is also narrower than "maths". A separate study reports meta-analytic evidence of a significant positive correlation of about 0.25 between visual-spatial skills and arithmetic ability specifically, and finds that visual-spatial ability predicted academic achievement entirely through mediators — with arithmetic ability carrying the large majority of the indirect effect and no direct path remaining. In other words, the spatial contribution to school achievement appears to travel through arithmetic rather than around it.

## Does training one improve the other?

The training question is the one people actually want answered, and the honest answer is that it is unsettled in the direction that has been studied, and untested in the direction most readers ask about.

On spatial training improving mathematics, the review quoted above says the "evidence is more mixed about training spatial skills to improve mathematical skills", and adds that "the mechanisms by which training spatial thinking can promote mathematical thinking are still not well understood". Some studies find transfer and some do not, and the field has not explained the inconsistency.

On arithmetic training improving spatial ability — the question a mental-maths app naturally raises — there is no body of evidence to point at. The general prior is not encouraging either. The consensus statement issued by the Stanford Center on Longevity and the Max Planck Institute for Human Development concluded that "there is little evidence that playing brain games improves underlying broad cognitive abilities", and that claims promoting brain games "are frequently exaggerated and at times misleading". A claim that drilling times tables sharpens mental rotation would be exactly that kind of claim.

What practice does reliably produce is improvement on the practised task, and some improvement on tasks that closely resemble it. The scope and limits of that are the subject of [do brain training apps work](/blog/do-brain-training-apps-work/), and the specific case of trained capacity versus general capacity is handled in [working memory and training](/blog/working-memory-and-training/).

## Where number sense actually helps

Number sense earns its keep in a different place: judging magnitude, choosing a method, and noticing that an answer is the wrong size before committing to it. That is why estimation and mental methods are worth practising on their own terms rather than as a proxy for something else, and why [the mental math training guide](/blog/mental-math-training-guide/) treats them as a skill with its own ceiling.

It is also why the construct question matters. A battery that samples several abilities and reports one composite can make two very different profiles look identical, which is the point developed in [what IQ tests actually measure](/blog/what-iq-tests-actually-measure/).
