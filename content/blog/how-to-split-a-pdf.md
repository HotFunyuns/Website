To split a PDF, open it in a PDF tool, choose split or extract, enter the page ranges you want and save the parts as new files, keeping the original. Splitting is merging in reverse: a long PDF, such as a full scanned contract or a year of bank statements, may need to go out in pieces, one section to one person or the whole thing in parts small enough to email. This guide covers splitting and extracting pages, page ranges and how to avoid sending the wrong pages.

## Splitting versus extracting

The two operations are related but different:

| Operation | What you get | What happens to the original |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Split | Several new files covering the whole document | Unchanged, if you save the parts as new files |
| Extract | One new file with only the pages you chose | Unchanged |
| Remove pages | The same file, minus some pages | Changed |

Wikipedia describes a PDF as encapsulating a complete description of a fixed-layout document, including the text, fonts, graphics and images needed to display it. Because each page is fully described, a tool can copy pages into a new file whole, without re-encoding them, so splitting need not change quality. Do not expect the parts to add up exactly to the original size, though: resources such as embedded fonts can end up stored in more than one part.

## Writing page ranges

Page-range boxes differ between apps, so check your tool's own example first. This guide uses the common hyphen-and-comma style:

- **1-3** means pages 1, 2 and 3;
- **7** means page 7 alone;
- **1-3, 7, 10-12** combines them.

One trap: a document's printed page numbers may not match its position in the file, for example when a cover and contents pages come first. Check whether your tool counts from the first page of the file or uses the printed numbers, and look at a page or two before extracting.

## A worked example: splitting for size

A 60 MB scanned document has three sections: pages 1-40, 41-75 and 76-120. Google's Gmail help gives a **25 MB** attachment limit for personal accounts, and Gmail turns larger attachments into Drive links. Splitting by section gives three files, but their sizes depend on their pages: if the pages are similar, the third section, with 45 of the 120 pages, would be about 60 × 45 ÷ 120 = **22.5 MB**, the second about 17.5 MB and the first about 20 MB. All three fit. If one part is still too large, compress it, or split that section again; [how to compress a PDF](/blog/how-to-compress-a-pdf/) covers the trade-offs.

## Splitting by meaning

Size is not the only reason to split. Another rule is **one file per purpose**:

- one file per invoice, statement or month, so each can be found and filed alone;
- one file per chapter or unit, for course readings;
- the signature pages separately, when only they need to come back signed.

## Common splitting mistakes

- **Off-by-one ranges**: ending a section at 40 when it really ends at 41 leaves a page in the wrong file. Check the last page of each part.
- **Forgetting shared pages**: a cover sheet or table of contents may belong in every part, or in none.
- **Splitting a signed document**: if a file carries a certificate-based digital signature, the extracted pages form a new file that the original signature does not cover. Send the complete signed original when the signature itself matters.
- **Overwriting the original**: always save parts as new files until you have checked them.

## Name the parts

Parts with names like "document_part1.pdf" get lost. Use names that describe the content and order: "Lease 2026 - 1 of 3 - terms.pdf", "Lease 2026 - 2 of 3 - schedules.pdf". The app's PDF manager lets you review, organise and rename files before sharing.

## Send less, expose less

Extracting is also a privacy habit. If a landlord needs page 4 of a bank statement, extract page 4 rather than sending all twelve. For sensitive pages that must travel, [password-protecting a PDF](/blog/how-to-password-protect-a-pdf/) explains what protection actually does.
