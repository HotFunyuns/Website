Indian history is still commonly divided into three blocks — a Hindu period, a Muslim period and a British period — and that division is not a finding about the subcontinent. It follows the arrangement of James Mill's *The History of British India*, first published in 1817, and it survives in study guides long after professional historians stopped using it. This article sets out where the scheme came from, what its author said about his own qualifications, and what replaced it. Periodisation is politically charged ground in India today; the aim here is to describe the historiography and the evidence, not to adjudicate present-day political claims.

## What Mill actually wrote

It helps to look at the book rather than the reputation. In the fourth edition, the one edited with notes by Horace Hayman Wilson, Book I covers the commencement of British commercial intercourse with India and the establishment of the East India Company. Book II is titled simply "Of the Hindus", and its chapters are not a narrative at all: chronology and ancient history, the classification and distribution of the people, the form of government, the laws, the taxes.

That structure is the argument in miniature. Everything before Muslim rule becomes one undifferentiated subject, described as a static social type rather than a sequence of events. Centuries of dynastic change, regional states, religious movement and economic transformation are absorbed into a single anatomy. The later habit of labelling those centuries "Hindu" and treating them as a block follows directly from that arrangement.

## What he admitted about himself

Mill anticipated the obvious objection and answered it in his preface. In his own words, it would be said that the writer "has never been in India; and, if he has any, has a very slight, and elementary acquaintance, with any of the languages of the East." He continues: "I confess the facts" — and then argues that enough information had been collected in European languages that a residence in India, or a knowledge of the languages of India, "was, to express myself moderately, not indispensable." He notes in support that nobody objected to a President of the Board of Control or a Governor-General who had never been to India and knew none of its languages.

That is worth reading twice, because it states the epistemology the scheme rests on: that the subcontinent could be adequately known from a distance, through the filings of the people governing it.

## His own editor's verdict

The most useful criticism of Mill's history is printed inside Mill's history. Horace Hayman Wilson, Professor of Sanskrit in the University of Oxford, edited the fourth edition and added a preface of his own. He writes that the work "presents inaccuracies both of fact and opinion, which have arisen from the author's imperfect knowledge of the country, and unacquaintance with any of the languages spoken in it." On Mill's defence of those gaps, Wilson is dry: "His arguments are ingenious: they will carry conviction but to few." Residence in a country, command of its dialects and conversancy with its literature, he allows, "are but humble elements in the formation of the historical character; but they are elements, and cannot be" dispensed with.

This matters for how the story is told. The rejection of Mill's framework is not a late political revision imposed on a neutral classic. The most serious objection was published with the book, by its editor, in 1840.

## The evidence the scheme has no room for

The strongest practical argument against the three-part division is that it predates most of what we know. UNESCO records that the ruins of Moenjodaro were discovered in 1922, and describes the city as built entirely of unbaked brick in the third millennium BCE, the best-preserved urban settlement in South Asia from that period, with a planned lower town, drainage, wells and public baths.

That is the subcontinent's first urban civilisation, and the tripartite scheme has no category for it — not because anyone decided to exclude it, but because it was invented a century before the evidence existed. A periodisation that cannot accommodate the oldest cities in its own region is a period scheme describing the state of one library in 1817. The same test applies to any inherited era vocabulary, which is the point made more generally in [the major eras of world history](/blog/major-eras-of-world-history/).

## What replaced it, and what is still argued

Modern work on South Asia mostly builds periods from local evidence: dynastic and regional sequences, agrarian and commercial phases, and categories argued from archaeology and inscriptions. Nothing about that is settled. Whether the centuries often labelled early medieval are best described as political fragmentation or as the formation of durable regional states has been argued for decades, and the argument runs through journals such as *Modern Asian Studies*. Historiography of this kind is taught formally in India — the Indira Gandhi National Open University's syllabus on historiography and research method carries a dedicated unit on colonial historiography in India by Sabyasachi Bhattacharya.

A deeper objection concerns the premise underneath the whole colonial scheme: that India had no historical writing of its own and therefore needed to be supplied with a history from outside. Romila Thapar's *The Past Before Us*, published by Harvard University Press, takes that claim on directly. Her publisher summarises her position in terms that are worth keeping: the often-repeated claim that India uniquely lacks historical writing distracts from the more pertinent question of how to recognise the historical sense of societies whose past is recorded in ways very different from European conventions. Her survey argues for a deep and sophisticated consciousness of history embedded in classical Indian literature.

Reading that literature well requires the distinction between a document made inside a society and one made about it — the skill set out in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/), and the one Mill's method most conspicuously lacked.

## A note on the present

Periodisation in India is not a quiet academic subject. Different political positions favour different framings of the medieval centuries, and reading those centuries as a straightforward religious conflict between communities is a framing many historians dispute on evidentiary grounds. Our position is limited and deliberate: we set out who holds which historiographical position and what evidence they cite, and we do not treat any government's or party's preferred framing — in India or anywhere else — as scholarly consensus.
