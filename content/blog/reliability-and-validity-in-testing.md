Two questions separate a measuring instrument from a quiz that produces a number. **Reliability**: would this test give a similar answer if it were repeated? **Validity**: is the conclusion someone wants to draw from the score actually supported by evidence? Ask both of anything that offers to tell you something about yourself, and most of what circulates online answers neither.

The order matters. Reliability is a precondition — a result that swings wildly cannot support any interpretation — but it is not sufficient. A test can be beautifully consistent and still be measuring the wrong thing, or be measuring the right thing and still not support the decision being made with it.

## Reliability: does the test repeat itself?

Reliability is estimated in several ways, and each answers a slightly different version of "would we get this again".

**Test-retest reliability** compares scores from the same people on two occasions. The interval is a design decision with consequences: short intervals inflate agreement because people remember items, long ones risk the underlying attribute genuinely changing. Practice effects sit right in the middle of this, which is why a higher score on a second sitting is ambiguous evidence.

**Internal consistency** asks whether the items within a test agree with one another — whether people who get one item right tend to get the others right. It is cheap to compute from a single administration, which is why it is the figure most often quoted, and it says nothing about stability over time.

**Alternate-form reliability** compares two supposedly equivalent versions. This is why item banks matter: a test used repeatedly needs many interchangeable items, a problem now often solved by generating them systematically, as [abstract reasoning item construction](/blog/abstract-reasoning-tests-explained/) describes.

**Inter-rater reliability** applies wherever a human judges the response. It is irrelevant to a multiple-choice quiz and central to anything scored by an examiner.

Every reliability estimate implies a margin of error around an individual score. A score of 104 from an instrument with a meaningful error band is not distinguishable from 99 in any way that should change a decision, and reporting it as a bare number hides that.

## Validity: is the interpretation justified?

The modern framing is the one that trips people up. Validity is not a property a test owns permanently; it attaches to a **specific interpretation for a specific use**. The same instrument can be well supported for one decision and unsupported for another.

Several kinds of evidence contribute. **Content evidence** asks whether the items represent the domain claimed — a numeracy test made entirely of percentage questions covers a narrow slice. **Criterion evidence** asks whether scores relate to an outcome they are claimed to predict. **Construct evidence** asks whether the pattern of relationships behaves as theory says it should: scores should correlate with measures of related things and not with measures of unrelated things.

The last of those is where popular instruments fail most often. A published critical review of emotional-intelligence measurement makes the problem concrete for one field, noting that "one clear challenge faced by early EI test developers was constructing emotion-focused questions that could be scored with objective criteria", and that "One disadvantage of self-report measures is that people are not always good judges of their emotion-related abilities". A questionnaire can be highly internally consistent while measuring self-perception rather than the ability it names. That case is developed further in [IQ and emotional intelligence](/blog/iq-and-emotional-intelligence/).

## Fairness is the third question

Professional practice treats fairness as a technical requirement rather than a courtesy. The *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, published in its 2014 edition by the American Educational Research Association with the American Psychological Association and the National Council on Measurement in Education, addresses professional and technical issues of test development and use across education, psychology and employment, and a companion volume in the same series is devoted specifically to fairness.

Test publishers document their own compliance machinery for the same reason. ETS describes its *Standards for Quality and Fairness* as providing "benchmarks of excellence" used throughout design, development and delivery to produce "technically fair, valid, and reliable tests", and states that its Office of Professional Standards Compliance "performs audits of all ETS tests". Whether or not you find a given publisher persuasive, the existence of published standards and an audit function is itself the signal — it is what a claim of validity looks like when someone intends it to be checkable.

## Applying the two questions to anything

A short checklist does most of the work.

1. **Is there a technical manual?** Not a marketing page — a document describing item development, scoring and evidence.
2. **Is there a norm sample, and who was in it?** A score is a comparison, so the comparison group is part of the result.
3. **Are reliability coefficients published, with the method named?**
4. **Is validity evidence tied to a stated use?** "Validated" with no use specified is a slogan.
5. **Is administration restricted?** Published cognitive batteries usually are. Pearson lists its 2024 Wechsler adult battery, which reports a Full Scale IQ derived from seven subtests, at qualification level C.

Applied to a free web quiz, this checklist usually terminates at step one. That is not a scandal; it is a category difference, and it is the same distinction that runs through [who invented the IQ test](/blog/who-invented-the-iq-test/) and through [what IQ tests actually measure](/blog/what-iq-tests-actually-measure/).

## What our own numbers are

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and it publishes no reliability or validity evidence. That is not an omission — it follows from making no measurement claim. The app is an educational game. It contains no IQ test, no cognitive assessment and no percentile scoring, and the numbers it shows you describe what happened in a session: score, accuracy, streak, average and median response time, fastest correct answer, and personal bests per mode.

Those figures are useful for exactly one purpose, which is comparing your own runs on the same mode at the same difficulty. Read across modes and they mislead — a 60-second sprint and a 75-second trainer are different tasks. Read them as a statement about your abilities and they mislead more. The scepticism that the evidence review in [do brain training apps work](/blog/do-brain-training-apps-work/) applies to the category applies to us as well, and the specific case of a performance number dressed as an age estimate is dismantled in [what a brain age score means](/blog/what-a-brain-age-score-means/).
