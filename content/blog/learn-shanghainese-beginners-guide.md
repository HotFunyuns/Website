The first thing to do is put your Mandarin down. Not throw it away — it will be useful for characters and for general orientation — but stop expecting it to carry you, because Shanghainese is a Wu Chinese variety and Wu is mutually unintelligible with Mandarin. That is a technical statement about comprehension, not a rhetorical flourish.

The second thing is to stop learning syllables. Shanghainese reshapes tones across whole words and phrases, and a syllable pronounced in isolation is often pronounced in a way that never occurs in real speech.

Those two moves cover most of what makes Shanghainese awkward to start.

## Why the transfer assumption fails

Learners arriving from Mandarin expect a discount, the way a Spanish speaker gets a discount on Italian. Some of it is real. Most of it is not, and it is worth knowing which is which before you build a study plan on it.

**Real:** characters, the general grammatical shape of a Sinitic language, classifier constructions, topic-comment sentence structure, and the experience of already having learned a tonal language once.

**Not real:** the pronunciation of any given character, the tone system, a large slice of basic vocabulary including the pronouns, and the sound inventory itself.

That last point deserves detail. Shanghainese has voiced obstruent initials — a feature rare outside the Wu and Xiang groups. Where Mandarin contrasts unaspirated and aspirated voiceless stops, Shanghainese adds a third, voiced series. A Mandarin speaker has no slot for it and will collapse it into one of the two they already have.

It is also vowel-rich, with around twenty distinct vowel qualities, twelve of which are phonemic. That is a lot of contrasts to learn to hear.

## Start with phrases, and here is the mechanism

The single most consequential fact about Shanghainese pronunciation is tone sandhi, and it is not a refinement you add later.

In Shanghainese, word-level tone sandhi is **left-prominent**: the first syllable dominates the tone contour of the whole domain, and the underlying tones of the following syllables have essentially no effect on the result. Tone has, in a real sense, migrated from the syllable to the word.

Take the consequence seriously. If you drill a two-syllable word by learning each syllable's citation tone and then joining them, you have learned two facts that do not combine into the answer. The word has its own contour, determined by its first syllable and its length.

This is why our Shanghainese lessons cover tone sandhi and connected speech in the **first third of the course** rather than filing it under advanced pronunciation. Teaching citation tones first and sandhi later means teaching a habit and then asking for it back, and learners who have spent two months producing careful, syllable-by-syllable Shanghainese find that the habit is expensive to dismantle. [Shanghainese tones and tone sandhi](/blog/shanghainese-tones-and-tone-sandhi/) works through why the tone count itself is a disputed number.

## The romanisation problem, stated honestly

There is no single standard. There are four broad schools of Wu romanisation, and the letter *b* does not mean the same thing in all of them.

The practical rule for a beginner is short: **pick one system and stay inside it.** Mixing a phrasebook that uses one scheme with an app that uses another produces a learner who is confidently mispronouncing half their vocabulary and cannot work out why. We set out which scheme our course uses and what that choice costs in [romanising Shanghainese](/blog/shanghainese-romanisation-choices/).

## What about characters?

Shanghainese is written with Chinese characters, but there is no standard written orthography in the way Mandarin has one, and the readings are not the Mandarin readings.

This makes characters a *partial* help. If you can read Chinese, you will often work out what a written Shanghainese sentence means. You will not work out how it sounds, and the confident wrong guess is a real hazard — a character you have read a thousand times in Mandarin has a pronunciation attached in your head, and that pronunciation is wrong here.

Our approach is to show characters and romanisation together on every line, so that the character carries the meaning and the romanisation carries the sound, and neither is asked to do the other's job.

## An order that works

1. **Greetings and the pronouns**, as whole phrases, with audio. 侬好 (*nóng hō*) is hello, and 侬 is the everyday second-person pronoun — not the Mandarin one, which is the first small shock.
2. **Listening before producing.** Spend the first sessions distinguishing sounds, particularly the voiced series, before worrying about making them.
3. **Tone sandhi at two-syllable words**, early. Not the theory — the pattern, through examples.
4. **The twelve-unit vocabulary path**: greetings, family, food, numbers, time, home, transport, shopping, school, work, health, social life.
5. **Sentence patterns**, including the yes-no question particle 伐 (*va*), which is not Mandarin's 吗.
6. **Extended material** — idioms, informal speech, business, culture — only once the core is comfortable.

## What our course cannot do

[Learn Shanghainese Wu Chinese](/apps/learn-shanghainese/) runs twelve units across everyday topics, with characters, romanisation, grammar and sentence patterns, tone sandhi lessons, travel and emergency language, and themed packs for idioms, business, etiquette and advanced conversation. It also runs in reverse for Shanghainese speakers studying English.

What it cannot do is give you the range a language with institutional backing has. Shanghainese has no standard written form, no state-sanctioned teaching curriculum, and a thin reference literature in English. Every course in this space — ours included — is assembling material rather than adapting an existing syllabus, and that shows up as narrower topic coverage than you would get in a Mandarin course of comparable effort.

It also cannot give you the variation. Shanghainese spoken by younger people in the city differs from that of older speakers, with a great deal of Mandarin mixed in, and a course has to pick a register. We picked a fairly conservative one. A learner using it with a twenty-year-old in Shanghai will sound slightly more careful than the person they are talking to, which is a better failure than the reverse.
