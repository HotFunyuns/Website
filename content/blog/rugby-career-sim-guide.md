[Pro Rugby Career Sport Sim](/apps/rugby-career-sim/) is a rugby career simulator built around one player instead of a whole club. You create the athlete — a portrait and a position — and decide what kind of player they should become: one who carries the ball, one who makes the tackles, or one whose value lies in supporting the players around them. Then you train toward that, follow your player through simulated matches, and judge each performance by three numbers: **carries, metres and tackles**. It is free on Google Play, supported by ads, and Google Play lists no in-app purchases.

That is the whole loop, and it is deliberately narrow. The useful questions are which role to pick, how to train without spreading yourself thin, and how to read three stats honestly. This guide takes them in that order.

## What the game asks you to decide

| Decision | When you make it | What it shapes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Portrait and position | Creating the athlete | Who your player is on the field |
| Role: carrier, tackler or support player | Creating the athlete | Which skills suit them — and, for your own review, which numbers to watch |
| Training priorities | Before matches, revisited between them | The skills your player builds next |
| Your goals | Whenever you like | What counts as a good season for you |

Nothing in that table happens during a match. The game's listing describes matches as simulated on the field, with you following your own player through the action, and it does not mention in-match controls, so as described your influence comes before the whistle and after it.

## Pick the role first, then the position

The three roles map onto real jobs in rugby union, and World Rugby's laws define them more precisely than most game menus do.

### The carrier

The laws call the player with the ball the **ball-carrier**. Carrying is about taking contact and making ground, and the reference point for ground is the **gain line** — the imaginary line across the pitch at each breakdown, beyond which any advance is a gain in territory. A carrier's job is to get over it.

### The tackler

A **tackle**, in law, is holding a ball-carrier and bringing them to ground; Law 14 says a tackle happens only when the ball-carrier is both held and brought down. Players who help bring a carrier down without going to ground themselves are tackle assists in the laws' terms. The game's listing does not say whether its tackle count includes assists, so treat the number as tackles your player was credited with and compare it with your own earlier matches rather than with outside statistics.

### The support player

Support is the least visible role and the one the laws describe in the most detail. Law 14 lists the players who arrive to contest possession at a tackle as participants in it, and Law 15 says a ruck exists so that players can compete for the ball on the ground; arriving players must be on their feet, join from behind their offside line and bind on. A support player's value often shows up in team-mates' numbers rather than their own, which matters when you review a match.

### Matching role to position

Rugby union's fifteen are split into eight forwards and seven backs, and Law 19 builds the scrum from the forwards: two props and a hooker in the front row, two locks behind them and three back-row players. [Rugby positions by number](/blog/rugby-positions-and-numbers/) and [forwards and backs](/blog/rugby-forwards-and-backs/) describe what each shirt usually does.

Contact load differs by group in the real sport. A 2022 systematic review of collision studies found that forwards experience more impacts and tackles than backs. That is a reason to choose a position whose real-world job matches the role you have in mind — a tackling forward is a natural pairing, for example — though how the game weights positions internally is not something its listing describes.

## Train toward one goal at a time

The game frames training as turning your next goal into a plan and building the skills that suit your role, "rather than trying to improve everything at once". The practical way to use that is to phrase every goal as a number you will check:

- **Carrier:** "more metres from the same number of carries."
- **Tackler:** "more tackles than last match."
- **Support player:** "be involved more often" — then look at whether your carries and tackles both rise, since support work is harder to see in a single count.

Pick the skills that serve that one sentence, play, and check. If the number moved, keep the plan for another match to confirm it was not noise; if it did not, change one thing rather than everything. Career games typically sample outcomes rather than compute them, as [how player career modes work](/blog/how-player-career-modes-work/) explains, and where that is the case a single result is weak evidence either way, so a run of matches is the fairer test.

## Reading carries, metres and tackles

The game tracks these three numbers because they describe an individual contribution to a team sport, so a performance reads as more than the final score. Its listing does not define each stat precisely, so the meanings below are the usual rugby ones. Each answers a different question:

| Stat | What it measures | What it cannot tell you | Read it with |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carries | How often your player ran with the ball | Whether those carries went anywhere | Metres |
| Metres | Ground made with the ball | Whether the ground came from many short carries or a few long ones | Carries |
| Tackles | How often your player brought an opponent down | How much defending was asked of them that match | Your role and earlier matches |

The most useful thing you can do with these numbers is a division the game does not need to do for you: **metres ÷ carries**. Here is a worked illustration with made-up numbers, not data from the game:

| | Carries | Metres | Metres per carry | Tackles |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Match A | 12 | 48 | 4.0 | 6 |
| Match B | 7 | 63 | 9.0 | 11 |

For a carrier, Match B is the better performance despite fewer carries: each carry made more than twice the ground. For a tackler, Match B is also better — nearly double the tackles. For a support player, neither row tells the full story, because the work of arriving at breakdowns does not appear in any of the three columns; the honest reading is whether the player you built is involved more often over a run of matches.

That last point is the main trap. Judging every role by carries makes tacklers and support players look like failures, and judging every role by tackles does the same to carriers. Decide which number your role should move, and read the others as context.

## The season loop: prepare, follow, review

The rhythm the game describes is prepare, follow the action, review. Training, results and stats carry from one match to the next, and each new season gives the player you built another run of matches. A short review routine keeps that loop honest:

1. Which number was this match about, given the role?
2. Did it move compared with the last two or three matches, not just the last one?
3. What is the single training change for the next match?

Goals are yours to set. The game's own examples range from becoming more effective in your position to contributing more consistently, or simply learning what the match numbers mean — and if you are new to rugby, [how rugby scoring works](/blog/how-rugby-scoring-works/) is the quickest way into what is happening around your player.

This [one-player rugby career game](/apps/rugby-career-sim/) suits rugby fans who want to follow one player rather than run a club, people who enjoy training plans and steady development, and anyone who likes reading match stats to see what improved.
