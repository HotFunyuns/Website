Amateur and professional boxing use the same punches and the same ring, but they are close to being different sports. Bout length, protective equipment, scoring, governance and money all differ. This guide compares them using Wikipedia's articles on amateur and professional boxing.

## The differences at a glance

| | Amateur ("Olympic-style") | Professional |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rounds | Three rounds of three minutes, with one minute's rest | Up to twelve; minor fights can be four |
| Headgear | Removed from men's competition in March 2016 | Not worn |
| Scoring | Several systems over the years, depending on the tournament | Mostly the 10-point must system |
| Governing bodies | International federations; World Boxing has provisional IOC recognition | Sanctioning bodies award belts and appoint officials |
| Money | Medals and titles | A purse divided by contract |

## Rounds and pace

Wikipedia gives the amateur format plainly: **three rounds of three minutes**, with a one-minute interval, for men and women. Men's senior bouts moved to that format from four two-minute rounds on 1 January 2009, and women's on 19 December 2016.

Professional bouts are scheduled for a fixed number of rounds, **up to twelve**. They used to be longer: Wikipedia notes a fifteen-round limit became the norm in the 1910s and 1920s and lasted for decades, until the WBC cut its championship bouts to twelve rounds from 1983 and other major bodies followed.

Wikipedia gives professional rounds as generally three minutes, so a twelve-round fight is 36 minutes of boxing against 9 in an amateur bout, and pacing has to stretch four times as far. Wikipedia also notes that amateur tournaments can feature several bouts over several days, while professionals typically rest several months between fights; [what a boxing corner does](/blog/what-does-a-boxing-corner-do/) explains the team that works with a boxer between rounds.

## Headgear

Wikipedia records that in **March 2016**, protective headgear, used in men's amateur competition since 1982, was removed because concussion rates were higher in fights with headgear than in fights without it. In professional boxing, Wikipedia notes, protective headgear is not permitted, and boxers are generally allowed to take substantial punishment before a fight is halted.

## Ten-point must versus amateur scoring systems

**Professional** boxing mostly uses the **10-point must** system, which Wikipedia describes as the most widely used scoring system since the mid-20th century and credits the WBC with introducing in 1968: the winner of each round gets 10 points and the loser fewer. [Boxing scoring explained](/blog/boxing-scoring-explained/) works through it round by round.

**Amateur** boxing has used several systems, depending on the tournament and the sanctioning authority. Wikipedia describes older vote-based systems, in which judges simply voted for a winner, computer scoring introduced at the Olympics in 1992, with judges pressing buttons for each scoring punch, and a new system introduced by AIBA in 2011. Amateur scoring has produced disputed results: Wikipedia cites disputed decisions at the 1960, 1988 and 1996 Olympics.

### A four-round card with a deduction

Under the 10-point must system, Wikipedia explains, a judge must award 10 points to at least one fighter each round, before deductions for fouls, and a lesser score to the loser; 10–9 is commonly seen. Suppose a judge gives fighter A rounds 1, 3 and 4 of a four-round bout 10–9, fighter B round 2 10–9, and A loses a point for a low blow in round 3, making that round 9–9. The card reads A 38 (10 + 9 + 9 + 10), B 37 (9 + 10 + 9 + 9): A still wins, but by one point instead of two, and a second deduction would have turned it into a 37–37 draw. That is why fouls matter in a close professional fight.

## Who runs the sport

Amateur boxing's governance has been turbulent. Wikipedia records that the **International Boxing Association (IBA, formerly AIBA)**, founded in 1946 and the oldest active body, set the de facto international standard but was suspended by the IOC in 2019. **World Boxing**, formed in 2023 as a split from the IBA over governance concerns, received provisional IOC recognition in **February 2025**.

Professional boxing is organised differently. Bouts are fought for a **purse**, divided between the boxers according to the contract, and most high-profile fights are endorsed by a **sanctioning body**, which awards belts, sets rules and assigns judges and referees. [Boxing belts and sanctioning bodies](/blog/boxing-belts-and-sanctioning-bodies/) explains the main ones.

## Olympic does not mean amateur-only

Wikipedia notes that boxers at the Summer Olympics compete under amateur rules but can be amateurs or professionals. "Olympic-style" now describes the rules, not the fighter's status. Boxing has been part of every Summer Olympics since 1904, apart from 1912.
