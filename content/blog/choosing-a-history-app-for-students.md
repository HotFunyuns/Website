Before comparing anything, answer one question: **which of these three jobs do you need done?**

1. **Structure** — you do not have a mental map of the period and need one.
2. **Retention** — you have the material and it will not stay in your head.
3. **Coverage** — you have a syllabus and need to be sure you have met all of it.

Almost every disappointing app purchase in this category comes from buying for one job and needing another. Nothing here is strong at all three, ours included.

## The three jobs, and what each one wants

### If you need structure

You want chronology, geography and connection: what happened when, where, and what was going on at the same time somewhere else.

This is what timeline and map products are for, and it is the one job where they have a genuine advantage over a book. A text survey has to describe simultaneity in sequence, because prose is linear. A timeline shows it.

### If you need retention

You want spaced repetition, and you want it applied to material you have already understood.

The strongest tool in this category is not a history app at all. Anki's own page describes it as "a flashcard program that helps you spend more time on challenging material, and less on what you already know", scheduling each review "for when you're most likely to forget the information." The desktop version is free on Windows, macOS and Linux; AnkiDroid on Android is described on that page as free and developed by contributors; the iOS app is a paid purchase that funds development.

The critical property is that **it ships with no history content.** You build the deck, or download a shared one. For a student who already has the material and needs it to stick, that is a feature rather than a gap: the deck matches your syllabus exactly because you made it.

### If you need coverage

You want structured courses aligned to a curriculum.

Khan Academy's own about page describes it as "a free tool for anyone — students, teachers, and parents alike", states that it is "a 501(c)(3) nonprofit organization", and describes "840+ free courses covering core academic subjects for K-12 and early college", with subject areas including Social Studies.

If your actual requirement is coursework coverage at no cost, that is the category to look at before any timeline app, including ours.

## What ours does not do

Stated plainly, because a decision guide that hides this is useless:

[World History Timeline Sim](/apps/world-history-timeline-sim/) **is not a curriculum.** There is no syllabus, no prescribed order and no completion path — the design principle is explicitly learning at your own pace, following curiosity from one event to the next.

It has **no assessment reporting.** Nothing is exported, nothing is graded against a standard, and no record is produced that a teacher or examiner would accept.

It has **no teacher dashboard.** A class cannot be enrolled, monitored or assigned work through it.

If any of those three are your requirement, this is the wrong product and you should look at a course platform instead. That is the honest answer even though it costs us the install.

## What it is built for

Exploration and sequence. More than 2,000 events organised chronologically and browsable by year, era, region, country or category; a world history map showing where major events happened; Time Travel Mode for stepping into a turning point with its real context and outcome before choosing between realistic options; Civilization Survival Mode for steering famous civilizations through food shortages, invasion risk, trade collapse, disease, political instability and climate stress; Timeline Detective for working backwards from clues to causes. Daily quizzes, flashcards, word search, crosswords, timeline ordering, matching games and map challenges cover the retention side, and artifacts unlock as you go.

The population it suits: a student who has the coursework and lacks the mental map, a trivia player who needs breadth across eras, a teacher or homeschooler who wants a visual angle alongside a curriculum they already have, and the reader who falls down late-night history rabbit holes. The [world history timeline guide](/blog/world-history-timeline-guide/) covers how to get real value out of that structure rather than just browsing it.

## The test that matters more than features

Whatever you pick, check one thing before committing: **does it tell you when something is disputed?**

History is substantially an argument about causes and evidence. A product that prints 476 as *the* date of the fall of Rome, or names a single cause for the Bronze Age collapse, is teaching a confidence the evidence does not support. Our articles on [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/) and [what caused the Bronze Age collapse](/blog/what-caused-the-bronze-age-collapse/) go through how thin some of those settled-sounding claims are.

This is a fair test to apply to us as well. We show contested events as ranges rather than single years, and users do complain that it makes the timeline harder to scan. We think the complaint is the price of the product being accurate.

## Honest recommendations

- **If you need syllabus coverage at no cost**, start with a structured free course platform such as Khan Academy, not with a timeline app.
- **If you need material to stick and already have the material**, use a spaced-repetition tool. Anki is free on Android, ships with no content, and will outperform any quiz mode built into a content app.
- **If you need structure, sequence and geography** — the mental map that makes everything else easier to place — that is what ours is for.
- **If you need to understand historical argument**, no app in this category will do it. Read. [How to study history on your own](/blog/how-to-study-history-on-your-own/) sets out a routine.
- **If you are a teacher needing reporting**, none of the above is your answer; look at products built for classroom management.
