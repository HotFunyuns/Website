The hardest part of journaling is the blank page. A prompt solves that by giving you one question to answer instead of a whole day to summarise. The prompts below are grouped by what you want to notice, with a note on what research says about each kind of writing, so you can choose the group that fits the day.

## How to use a prompt

Pick **one** prompt, set a short limit, such as five minutes, and answer it honestly. Do not edit, and do not worry about spelling. Two or three sentences count as an entry. If a prompt does not fit today, pick another; the aim is writing, not completing the list.

## 1. To notice what went well

- What went well today, and what part did I play in it?
- Who made today easier, and how?
- What is something small I enjoyed that I would normally overlook?
- What am I glad I did this week?

**What the research says.** In a 2003 set of studies, Emmons and McCullough randomly assigned people to list hassles, things they were grateful for, or a comparison topic such as neutral life events, and to keep weekly or daily records of their moods and health. The gratitude groups showed **higher well-being on several, though not all**, of the outcome measures, and the effect on **positive mood was the most robust** finding. That is encouraging, not a promise.

## 2. To name a feeling

- What am I feeling right now, in one word? What brought it on?
- Where in my body do I notice that feeling?
- What would I say to a friend who felt this way?
- Which moment today had the strongest feeling, good or bad?

Naming feelings is the basis of a mood tracker too; [keeping a mood tracker](/blog/mood-tracking-journal/) covers turning these notes into patterns.

## 3. To clear unfinished business

- What is on my mind that I have not dealt with?
- What is the next specific step, and when will I take it?
- What am I putting off, and what is the smallest version of it?

**What the research says.** In 2011 experiments, Masicampo and Baumeister found that **unfinished goals caused intrusive thoughts** and interfered with unrelated tasks, and that letting people **make specific plans** for those goals **eliminated** the effect. A plan only worked if it was earnest: the people who went on to carry it out were the ones whose intrusions stopped. A prompt that ends in a dated next step asks for the same kind of specific plan, though a journal entry is not the lab task those studies used.

## 4. To work through a hard day

- What happened, in plain facts?
- What did I feel then, and what do I feel now?
- What do I wish had gone differently, and what is in my control next time?

**What the research says.** Wikipedia describes **expressive writing** as a technique developed by psychologist **James Pennebaker** in the late 1980s, in which participants wrote their deepest thoughts and feelings about a past trauma or major emotional issue for **15 minutes a day on 4 consecutive days**. Wikipedia notes that many participants reported being **upset by the writing but found it valuable**, and that the theory behind it has **mixed empirical support**. If writing about something painful leaves you more distressed rather than less, stop, and consider talking to someone instead.

## 5. To capture what you learned

- What did I learn today that I did not know yesterday?
- What mistake taught me something this week?
- What question am I still curious about?

## 6. To look ahead

- What would make tomorrow a good day?
- What am I looking forward to this month?
- Where do I want to be with one goal a year from now, and what is the first step?

## A worked example: one five-minute entry

Prompt: "What is on my mind that I have not dealt with?"

> The dentist appointment I keep forgetting to book. It's small but it nags every time my tooth twinges. Next step: call at 9:00 tomorrow, before work. Put it in the planner.

Three sentences: the worry, why it matters, and a dated step. That is a complete entry.

## Rotating prompts for a month

Rotate through the six groups, one per day, and each group comes round **every six days**, five times in a 30-day month. Mix a lighter group (1, 5 or 6) after a heavier one (4), so the journal does not become a place you only go when things are hard.

## When journaling is not enough

A journal is a tool for reflection, not a treatment. The NHS advises seeing a GP if you have had a low mood for more than **2 weeks**, are struggling to cope, or what you are trying is not helping.
