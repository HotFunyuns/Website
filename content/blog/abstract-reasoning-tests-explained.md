An abstract reasoning item is an engineered object with four parts: a stem, a hidden rule, one correct key and a set of distractors. Nothing about it is arbitrary. The rule is chosen from a short catalogue, the difficulty is tuned by how many rules operate at once, and the wrong answers are selected because real candidates produce exactly those errors. Once you can see the construction, practice stops being a guessing game and becomes a search with a short, finite checklist.

## The four parts of an item

The **stem** is the material you are shown: a row of frames, a grid with a gap, or a run of numbers. The **rule** is the transformation that turns one element into the next. The **key** is the single option consistent with that rule. The **distractors** are the other options.

The rule catalogue for figural items is small. Elements rotate, reflect, move around a frame, change shading, change size, change in number, or appear and disappear. A hard item combines two of those on different features — shape count increasing while shading alternates, say — so that spotting one rule is not enough to eliminate the near-misses.

Numeric items work the same way with arithmetic in place of geometry. A published study of automatically generated number-series items describes three stimulus features that drive the design: the type of number element, the type of arithmetic operation, and the number of arithmetic operations. The operations used were arithmetic progressions with a constant increment, geometric progressions with a constant ratio, Fibonacci-style addition sequences, and multiplicative sequences.

## A worked example, and what the wrong answers mean

Take the series **3, 6, 11, 18, 27, ?**

Work the first differences: 6 − 3 = 3, 11 − 6 = 5, 18 − 11 = 7, 27 − 18 = 9. The differences themselves rise by 2 each time, so the next difference is 11 and the answer is 27 + 11 = **38**. The same series has a second description — each term is a square plus two: 1 + 2 = 3, 4 + 2 = 6, 9 + 2 = 11, 16 + 2 = 18, 25 + 2 = 27, 36 + 2 = 38. Two descriptions of one rule is a good sign you have found the real structure rather than a coincidence.

Now consider three plausible wrong answers and what each one means:

- **36** — you reused the last difference (+9) instead of extending the ladder. This is the single most common sequence error.
- **37** — you extended the ladder by 1 rather than 2, giving +10.
- **54** — you abandoned the difference structure and doubled the last term.

An item writer who includes 36 and 37 is not padding the options. Each one catches a named misconception, which is why a near-miss on a real test usually tells you something specific about your method.

A two-rule version looks like this: **2, 6, 5, 15, 14, 42, 41, ?** Here the operation alternates. Multiply by 3, then subtract 1: 2 × 3 = 6, 6 − 1 = 5, 5 × 3 = 15, 15 − 1 = 14, 14 × 3 = 42, 42 − 1 = 41, so the next term is 41 × 3 = **123**. Nothing in it is harder arithmetic than the first example; the difficulty is entirely in noticing that two rules take turns.

## Difficulty is designed, and it is predictable

The same number-series study quantified the effect. Items requiring two steps of arithmetic operation had an average difficulty of 1.28, against minus 0.70 for one-step items, and using fractions or irrational numbers pushed difficulty up further compared with integers. Most usefully, the authors reported that "77.4% of the variance in item difficulty... was explained by the stimulus features implemented in the automatic item generator."

That last figure is the practical takeaway for anyone preparing. Difficulty comes overwhelmingly from structural features you can learn to recognise — how many rules, how many steps, what kind of number — rather than from some ineffable quality of the item. The checklist that follows falls straight out of it: count the terms, take first differences, take ratios if the differences are not stable, check whether the operation alternates, and check whether the series interleaves two independent sequences.

## How item banks are made now

Large-scale testing needs many items that target the same construct without repeating, and hand-writing them is slow. Automatic item generation addresses this. One review defines it as "the process of generating items/questions from various inputs, including models, templates, or schemas", where an item model is "a template that specifies the features in an item that can be manipulated". Subject-matter experts build a cognitive model of the skill, express it as a template, and an algorithm varies the manipulable features to produce a bank.

The listed promises include reduced item-writing time and cost, support for continuous development of large item pools, and the ability to tailor items for learning. For a candidate this has one direct consequence: memorising specific items is close to worthless, because the bank is large and the surface features vary while the rule family does not. Learning the rule family is the only portable skill.

It is also worth carrying over a caution from the fairness literature. Research on cross-cultural testing has found consistent group differences on visually mediated tasks that are widely assumed to be neutral, which is covered in more detail in [nonverbal reasoning tests](/blog/nonverbal-reasoning-tests-explained/). A generated bank inherits whatever assumptions the template encodes.

## Practising the move without pretending it is a test

If you want to practise rule discovery, practise the search procedure rather than collecting answers. Run the checklist in a fixed order, and when you get one wrong, name the misconception before moving on. That habit is the difference between repetition and useful practice.

What it does contain is **Number Pattern Challenge**, a 75-second rule-discovery drill in numbers with Easy, Medium and Hard settings, alongside arithmetic modes such as Mixed Operations and Missing Number Puzzle that exercise the same habit of asking what operation links two quantities. That is an analogue of abstract reasoning, not an equivalent, and it is not an assessment of anything. The app records score, accuracy, streak and response time for your own runs, and nothing more.
