Squad rotation in a rugby management game is not a stylistic choice. It is the consequence of two constraints that exist in the real sport and are reproduced in a season model: the laws cap how many players you can bring on and dictate who must be able to cover the front row, and the physical load of the sport means the fifteen you would pick every week will not be available every week.

Most draft guides stop at the board. This one starts the morning after it, because the squad you assembled is now a resource you have to spend across a long campaign rather than a lineup you get to field on repeat.

## The laws already wrote half your rotation plan

Rugby union limits each side to no more than fifteen players in the playing area during play. Replacements are nominated before the match — up to eight in international fixtures, with other matches capped at a maximum of eight as determined by the match organiser — and they can only come on when the ball is dead and the referee permits it.

The part that shapes squad building most is the front row. World Rugby's Law 3 sets a minimum number of suitably trained and experienced front-row players according to squad size: three for a squad of fifteen or fewer, four for sixteen to eighteen, five for nineteen to twenty-two, and six for a squad of twenty-three. Teams must tell the match officials in advance which players can play in the front row and in which positions, and only those players may pack down when the scrum is contested.

Read that as a design constraint rather than a technicality. **Before you have chosen a single tactic, the laws have told you that roughly a quarter of a full matchday squad has to be prop-and-hooker capable.** Every other position competes for what is left. [Forwards and backs](/blog/rugby-forwards-and-backs/) explains why that cover is the least substitutable resource on a draft board, and it is the same scarcity that decides whether rotation is available to you in week fifteen.

## Why the bench decides a long campaign

Rotation exists because absence is normal, not exceptional. The Rugby World Cup 2019 injury surveillance study reported 79.4 match injuries per 1,000 player-match-hours with a mean severity of 28.9 days, and attributed most match injuries to tackling, collisions, running and being tackled. Ligament sprains and muscle strains were the most common injury types, while knee ligament and cartilage injuries accounted for over a third of all days absent despite being less frequent.

A systematic review and meta-analysis of amateur male rugby union found a lower but still substantial match injury incidence of 46.8 per 1,000 player hours, with forwards carrying more risk than backs and the tackle the highest-risk event.

Two planning lessons fall out of those figures, and neither depends on the exact number:

- **Absence is frequent enough to budget for.** A squad plan that only works while everyone is fit is not a plan.
- **Severity is uneven.** A handful of injury types dominate total days lost, so the tail matters more than the average. One long absence in a position with no cover does more damage than several short ones spread across a deep group.

## What the season model actually tracks

[24-0 Rugby Draft Pro League](/apps/rugby-draft-pro-league/) is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and its GM mode runs a 24-match campaign under a $200M in-game salary cap. Rotation, depth, morale, confidence and job security all move with your results, and the description of the mode is blunt about the consequence: a thin bench becomes a problem in week fifteen.

That phrasing is worth taking literally, because it describes a failure shape rather than a gradual decline. A thin squad plays its strongest combination every week and looks convincing early. Minutes accumulate. An absence forces a change in a position where the second option is a long way behind the first. Results dip, and the squad is now playing weaker players in worse form under more pressure — which is exactly when morale and confidence start working against you.

The same pattern appears across the studio's other management titles, and [injuries in sports career games](/blog/injuries-in-sports-career-games/) covers the general version of it. Rugby's variant is sharper only because the front-row rule removes the option of covering a gap with whoever happens to be nearest.

## A rotation plan you can hold to

Rotation works when it is decided in advance and applied consistently. Four habits do most of the work.

**Set the minutes budget at the draft, not in week ten.** Decide roughly how many of the 24 matches each first-choice player should start, then treat any deviation as a decision rather than an accident. [Rugby draft and squad building](/blog/rugby-draft-and-squad-building/) covers the board itself; the budget is what turns those picks into a season.

**Protect the positions with no substitute.** Front row first, then the decision-makers at nine and ten, then the goal kicker. These are the roles where a drop from first choice to third choice costs the most per match, because the replacement is not doing a slightly worse version of the same job.

**Rotate into the manageable fixtures, not the hard ones.** The instinct is to rest players when a match looks winnable without them, which is correct, but the schedule rarely cooperates. Planning rest around the run of fixtures at the start of the season gives you more usable windows than reacting to fatigue later.

**Treat a rotated match as a real match.** A second-choice combination that has never played together is not depth. Depth is a second option with minutes behind it.

## Morale, confidence and job security are one loop

It is tempting to read those three as separate meters. In practice they behave as a single feedback loop, and understanding the direction of travel matters more than the individual numbers.

Results move morale. Morale moves the quality of performance you get from the same players. Poor performances force changes you did not plan, and unplanned changes are usually worse than planned ones because they land in the positions you were least prepared to cover. Job security then tightens, which narrows your options further — a manager under pressure stops rotating, which accelerates the thing that created the pressure.

The exit from that loop is unglamorous: stabilise selection, accept a worse expected result in one or two fixtures to get minutes into the second-choice group, and let the loop run in the other direction. It is the same logic as [rebuilding or retooling](/blog/rebuild-vs-retool/) a roster, compressed into a single season.

## When not to rotate

Rotation is a tool, not a virtue. Three situations call for fielding your strongest available fifteen and accepting the cost.

The first is a match that decides qualification. Spreading minutes has value across a campaign; it has none if the campaign ends. The second is immediately after a bad run, when a squad needs a result more than it needs rest. The third is when your second option in a key position is not merely worse but categorically different — a scrum-half covering fly-half, say — because the drop there is not linear.
