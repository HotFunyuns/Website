Italian plural nouns are made by changing the last vowel rather than adding an ending, so *libro* becomes *libri* and nothing is tacked on. Three swaps cover the core of the system, and the rest is a set of exceptions that each follow from something you can see: a stressed final vowel, a hard c or g, or a noun's Latin past. This explainer takes them in that order. The main source is Wikipedia's article on Italian grammar, with spelling points from its article on Italian orthography.

## The three main swaps

Wikipedia's table of general endings shows the pattern, with the article and adjective changing alongside the noun.

| Singular ending | Plural ending | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -o (masculine) | -i | *il cappello nero* → *i cappelli neri*, the black hats |
| -a (feminine) | -e | *la bella macchina* → *le belle macchine*, the beautiful cars |
| -e (either gender) | -i | *il/la comandante* → *i/le comandanti*, the commanders |
| -a (masculine) | -i | *l'atleta* → *gli atleti*, the athletes |

The fourth row is the one to watch. A masculine noun ending in *-a*, such as *atleta* or *poeta*, takes **-i**, not *-e*: *i poeti*. The *-e* row also hides gender, since *-e* nouns can be either; *il cane* "the dog" and *la parete* "the wall" both become *-i* (*i cani*, *le pareti*). Knowing the gender, which the article tells you, is the job of [Italian articles and gender](/blog/italian-articles-and-gender/).

One smaller rule: Wikipedia notes that most masculine nouns ending in *-io*, with the i pronounced as a glide, drop the *-o* and end in a single *-i*: *esempio* → *esempi*, *funzionario* → *funzionari*.

## Nouns that do not change

Wikipedia says nouns ending in **any letter other than -a, -e or -o**, and nouns ending in a **stressed vowel**, are normally invariable. Only the article shows the plural:

- *la città* → *le città*, the cities
- *il caffè* → *i caffè*, the coffees
- *la gru* → *le gru*, the cranes
- *il film* → *i film*, the films
- unadapted loanwords: *il manager* → *i manager*

Feminine nouns in *-ie* also stay put: *la specie* → *le specie*. In all of these, as Wikipedia puts it, only the article carries the information about gender and number, so *le città* is plural purely because of *le*.

## Keeping c and g hard or soft

The spelling rules from Italian pronunciation shape the plurals of nouns ending in c or g plus a vowel. Because *-ci* and *-gi* are soft, a hard sound needs an h.

**-ca and -ga** always keep the hard sound: *amica* → *amiche*, female friends.

**-co and -go** are the unpredictable group. Wikipedia quotes the observation that "the grammarians are skeptical of any attempt at giving a ruling about this area", and offers rules of thumb:

| Before the ending | Plural | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a consonant or a stressed vowel | -chi, -ghi (hard) | *fungo* → *funghi*, *fuoco* → *fuochi* |
| an unstressed vowel | -ci, -gi (soft) | *medico* → *medici*, *comico* → *comici* |
| -logo meaning an expert | -logi | *archeologo* → *archeologi* |
| -logo meaning speech or reasoning | -loghi | *catalogo* → *cataloghi* |

The exceptions are common words: *amico* → *amici* and *greco* → *greci* soften despite the pattern, while *valico* → *valichi* stays hard.

**-cia and -gia** depend on the letter before. Wikipedia's orthography article gives the rule, in place since the 1950s: after a vowel the i is kept (*camicia* → *camicie*, *valigia* → *valigie*); after a consonant it is dropped (*provincia* → *province*, *faccia* → *facce*). If the i is stressed, it always stays: *farmacia* → *farmacie*.

## Applying the rules to five nouns

Work each noun through the tables above in order: is the final vowel stressed? What is the ending? Is there a c or g before it?

1. **università**: stressed final vowel → invariable → *le università*.
2. **ragazzo**: masculine *-o* → *-i* → *i ragazzi*.
3. **lago**: *-go* after a stressed vowel → hard → *i laghi*.
4. **pesca** (peach): *-ca* → always hard → *le pesche*.
5. **arancia**: *-cia* after a consonant → drop the i → *le arance*.

Five nouns, five different routes, but only one table lookup each. Of the five, 1 stays the same and 4 change, and the two hard-consonant plurals (*laghi*, *pesche*) both gained an h to keep their sound.

## Feminine plurals and true irregulars

A small group of nouns, Wikipedia explains, come from Latin second-declension **neuters**, whose plurals ended in *-a*. They are masculine in the singular and become feminine *-a* in the plural:

- *l'uovo* → *le uova*, the eggs
- *il braccio* → *le braccia* (or *i bracci*), the arms
- *il ginocchio* → *le ginocchia* (or *i ginocchi*), the knees
- *il dito* → *le dita*, the fingers

Where two plurals exist, the meaning can split. Wikipedia gives *le braccia*, a person's arms, against *i bracci*, inlets or isthmuses; and *le corna*, an animal's horns, against *i corni*, the musical instruments.

A handful are **genuinely irregular**, mostly inherited from Vulgar Latin: *uomo* → *uomini* (men), *dio* → *dei* (gods, with *gli* as the article), *bue* → *buoi* (oxen) and *tempio* → *templi* (temples).
