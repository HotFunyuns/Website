Japanese history is taught as a list of period names, and the list quietly mixes three different kinds of label. Nara, Heian and Edo are capital cities. Kamakura, Ashikaga and Tokugawa are warrior houses or the places they governed from. Meiji is an imperial era name. Because they are not the same kind of unit, the transitions between them are not the same kind of event, and a learner who treats the list as uniform ends up assuming that every handover was a change of dynasty. Most were not.

Columbia University's Asia for Educators timeline, prepared by the director of its C. V. Starr East Asian Library, gives the sequence: a tomb or Kofun period from about 250, Asuka from 538, Nara from 710, Heian 794–1185, Kamakura 1185–1333, the Kemmu Restoration 1333–1336, Ashikaga or Muromachi 1336–1573, civil war, unification 1568–1598, Tokugawa 1600–1867, and Meiji 1868–1912.

## Yamato: clan rulers and the beginning of a dynasty

The earliest regime here is not a state in the later sense. Columbia's timeline associates the tomb period with great earthen grave mounds and their funerary objects — including the clay *haniwa* figures of people, animals, buildings and boats — and reads them as evidence for the emergence of powerful clan rulers. Among these was the Yamato clan, whose rulers, in the timeline's phrasing, began the imperial dynasty that has continued to the present.

What is documented here is archaeological: monumental burials on a scale that implies organised labour and hierarchy. Narratives about individual early rulers come mostly from chronicles compiled centuries later for a court with an interest in the result, and should be read with the caution set out in [how calendars and dating systems work](/blog/how-calendars-and-dating-systems-work/), where a date transmitted through a later source is only as good as the chain that carried it.

## The court state: Nara and Heian

The second regime is a centralised court modelled on Chinese practice. Columbia's timeline marks the introduction of Buddhism in 552 and the Taika Reform of 645 as a reorganisation based largely on learning imported from China — Buddhism, a writing system, bureaucratic organisation and legal theory.

Nara followed as the first permanent capital. UNESCO records it as the capital of Japan from 710 to 784, a period in which the framework of national government was consolidated, and lists the city's Buddhist temples, Shinto shrines and excavated imperial palace remains as evidence of eighth-century life in the capital.

Here is a small, instructive discrepancy. Columbia dates the Nara period 710–814; UNESCO says Nara was capital 710–784. Neither is wrong. One is naming a cultural and political period, the other the tenure of a specific city as capital, and the gap between them covers a short-lived intermediate capital and the move to Heian-kyō. UNESCO dates the founding of Kyoto to 794, built on the model of the capitals of ancient China, and notes it remained the imperial capital until the middle of the nineteenth century. This is exactly the phenomenon [the major eras of world history](/blog/major-eras-of-world-history/) describes: period boundaries are analytical choices, and two careful sources can choose differently.

The Heian court itself is best known for what its aristocracy wrote. Columbia's timeline highlights the body of poetry, diaries and fiction produced by court women, including *The Tale of Genji*.

## The warrior regimes: Kamakura, Muromachi, Tokugawa

The third, fourth and fifth regimes share a structure that has no close European equivalent: an emperor who remains the legitimate sovereign, and a military government that actually rules.

Columbia dates the Kamakura period 1185–1333 and describes samurai replacing nobles as the real rulers, with the imperial court remaining in Kyoto while the shogunal organisation was based at Kamakura. The Ashikaga or Muromachi period, 1336–1573, put the warrior government back in Kyoto but with weak control over the country, and the timeline notes that from its Muromachi base it became a patron of an artistic tradition shaped by Zen Buddhist culture as well as by samurai and court society. Then came prolonged civil war, and unification between 1568 and 1598.

The Tokugawa period, 1600–1867, is described by Columbia as some 250 years of unified military government and secluded peace that produced a vibrant urban culture with innovations in economic organisation, literature and the arts. The castles are the visible residue: UNESCO calls Himeji-jo the finest surviving example of early seventeenth-century Japanese castle architecture, comprising 83 buildings with highly developed defensive systems dating from the beginning of the shogun period.

"Secluded" needs qualifying rather than repeating. Trade and diplomatic contact were restricted and regulated, not abolished, and Japanese silver had already tied the archipelago into a wider economy: UNESCO records the Iwami Ginzan mine as worked from the sixteenth century, contributing substantially to the economic development of Japan and Southeast Asia in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, with ore transported to the coast and shipped from port towns to Korea and China.

## Meiji: a restoration that was a construction

The sixth regime is the one whose English name misleads most. Columbia dates the Meiji era 1868–1912 and describes an emergence, with Western stimulus, into the modern international world, marked by dramatic alterations in institutions, traditional social organisation and culture.

Formally, imperial authority was restored and the shogunate abolished. Practically, what followed was the building of something that had not existed before: a centralised state with a conscript army, a national tax system, universal schooling, new legal codes and industrial policy. Calling it a restoration was a political argument made at the time, and repeating it uncritically adopts that argument.

This is the same trap as the one at the centre of [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/), where the vocabulary used by contemporaries — decline, restoration, renewal — encodes claims that later readers absorb as description.
