A professional golfer does not enter tournaments. They are eligible for them, or they are not, and eligibility is decided by what happened last season. That single fact is what makes golf careers compound in a way that team-sport careers do not: a bad year does not just cost you a year, it costs you access to the events where the next year's points are available.

Here is how the three routes into a field actually work, and how to think about a schedule once you understand them.

## Three doors into a field

**Exemption.** You meet a published category and you are in. No qualifying round, no application.

**Position.** You sit high enough on a ranking or a season-long standings list that the cut-off catches you. This is an exemption category too, but it behaves differently because it moves week to week.

**Qualifying.** You play a separate competition and take one of the places on offer.

Most professionals use all three across a career, and usually in that order as status is gained and lost.

## Exemptions are categories, not invitations

The clearest public example of how an exemption list is actually built is the one published each year for The Open. The R&A states that the exemption categories "are reviewed each year to ensure that avenues are available for golfers around the world," and the published list reads as a set of precise, checkable conditions rather than a shortlist of names.

A few representative lines from the exemptions published for the 154th Open give the flavour:

- "The Open Champions aged 60 or under on 19 July 2026."
- "First 50 players on the OWGR for Week 21, 2026."
- "First 30 players from the Final 2025 FedExCup Points List."

Notice what those three have in common. Each one is a rule with a date attached, so a player can look at it in January and know precisely what they need to do. There is no discretion in it, which is the point: an exemption category is a published contract between the championship and the field.

Categories typically cover past champions, high finishers in the previous edition, players inside a world ranking cut-off at a named week, leading players from each tour's season standings, and winners of designated amateur championships.

## The world ranking is an average, not a total

This is the part most people get wrong, and it changes how a season should be planned.

The Official World Golf Ranking is administered over "a two year rolling period from the Ranking Date and to the date falling 104 weeks before the relevant Ranking Date." Within that window, a player's position is set by their **average** points: total points divided by the number of eligible tournaments played.

Two constraints shape that average:

- A **minimum divisor of 40** eligible tournaments over the period.
- A maximum of the **most recent 52** eligible tournaments counting toward the position.

The consequences are worth stating explicitly, because they are counter-intuitive.

If you have played fewer than 40 eligible events in the window, your total is divided by 40 anyway. Additional events in that range cannot dilute you — every point you add is pure gain.

Once you are past 40, every additional event increases the divisor. An event that returns fewer points than your current average therefore pulls the average down, even though you gained points by playing it. A crowded schedule is not automatically a good schedule.

## Old results fade on a published curve

The second mechanic is decay, and it is specified precisely rather than left vague. Ranking points are "maintained at full value for a 13-week period from the relevant Ranking Date on which they were awarded," and are then reduced by 1/92 of their original value each week across the remaining 91 weeks of the two-year period.

So a result is worth its full value for three months, then bleeds away evenly over the following twenty-one. The design intent is stated openly: to place additional emphasis on recent performances.

For a player, the practical reading is that a ranking is a rolling assessment of the last two years with a heavy tilt toward the last three months. A strong stretch protects you for a quarter and then starts costing you unless it is renewed.

## Qualifying is the route when status runs out

Because status is temporary, every championship keeps doors open for players who do not have it.

The Open's qualification page describes both routes: a qualifying series of events through which golfers around the world can earn a place, and Final Qualifying, which for the 154th Open produced 20 places, with a further last-chance qualifier filling one more spot afterwards.

Qualifying is a different competition from a tour event in a way that matters. It is short, the field is large relative to the places available, and the scoring dynamic is unusual: there is no benefit to finishing second in a qualifier if two places are on offer and you are third. It rewards a specific kind of aggression.

## How a bad season compounds

Put the pieces together and the mechanism is clear.

A poor year means fewer high finishes. Fewer high finishes mean fewer points and less prize money. Less of both means falling outside the ranking cut-offs and the season standings that supply next year's exemptions. Falling outside those means a schedule made up of smaller events, where fewer points are available. The following year starts from a lower ceiling.

Nothing about that is a death spiral — it is a narrowing. And the narrowing is reversible, because the decay curve cuts both ways: two years is not very long, and a strong recent stretch is weighted heavily.

Missing cuts accelerates the whole thing, because a missed cut generally means no earnings for the week as well as no points. Our piece on [how a golf cut works](/blog/how-a-golf-cut-works/) covers that mechanism in detail.

## Playing a schedule rather than a week

Three habits follow from all of the above, and they apply to a simulated career as much as a real one.

**Know which door you are using.** A player inside the exemptions should protect position. A player outside them should be chasing points, which sometimes means entering events a higher-ranked player would skip.

**Count your divisor.** Below the minimum, play more. Above it, only enter events where you expect to beat your own average.

**Watch the thirteen-week window.** A great result buys you a quarter of stability. Use it to take the risks that need a cushion, not to coast.
