Phone screens are great for watching alone and poor for watching together. Getting a video from an Android phone onto a TV takes one of three routes: **casting**, **screen mirroring** or a **cable**. Each needs different hardware, and each has its own way of going wrong. This guide explains them, using Google's Pixel help and Wikipedia's articles on Miracast and USB-C.

## Three routes compared

| Route | What it needs | Strengths | Weak points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Google Cast | A Google Cast device, such as a Chromecast set up on your Wi-Fi network | Wireless, simple, can send one app | Depends on Wi-Fi quality |
| Miracast screen mirroring | A TV that supports Miracast, and a phone that does too | Wireless, no extra device on supporting TVs | Support varies; quality limits |
| Cable (USB-C to HDMI or DisplayPort) | A phone that supports video output over USB-C, and the right cable | No network involved, stable picture | Not all phones support it; you are tethered |

## Casting with Google Cast

Google's Pixel help describes projecting your screen through casting:

1. Open **Settings**.
2. Tap **Connected devices**, then **Connection preferences**, then **Google Cast**.
3. Choose to cast **a single app** or **your entire screen**.
4. Tap the **device** you want to show it on.

Google suggests adding Cast to **Quick Settings** for one-tap access, and to stop, swipe down and tap **Disconnect** on the Cast notification. These steps are for Pixel phones. Other Android phones may put casting in a different menu or give it another name, so check your phone maker's help if you cannot find it. Google's Chromecast setup help adds that a Chromecast is set up through the Google Home app on a secure wireless network.

## Screen mirroring with Miracast

Some TVs mirror phones without a separate streaming device. Wikipedia describes **Miracast** as a wireless standard created by the **Wi-Fi Alliance** to send video and sound from devices such as smartphones to TVs, monitors or projectors. It uses **Wi-Fi Direct** to make a direct connection, and can carry up to **1080p** video using the H.264 codec, with stereo sound and optional 5.1 surround. Whether it works depends on both the TV and the phone supporting it, so check your TV's manual for "screen mirroring" or Miracast.

## Connecting with a cable

A cable takes Wi-Fi out of the picture. Wikipedia explains that **USB-C** is a connector, not a protocol, designed to replace separate cables for technologies such as HDMI and DisplayPort, and that video can travel over it through **alternate modes**. It adds that USB-C-to-HDMI adapters which use DisplayPort Alternate Mode need active conversion circuitry, so the cable itself matters. Not every phone supports this. Google's Pixel help says **Pixel 8 and later** can connect to a display to **mirror the screen**, using a display that supports a **DisplayPort or HDMI to USB-C cable**: plug the cable into the display, plug the USB-C end into the phone, then confirm the mirror dialog on the phone.

## A worked example: a family video night

You have a two-hour film saved on your phone and a TV with Google Cast. Casting works, but the picture stutters whenever someone in the house starts a download. Moving the phone closer to the router may help; a USB-C to HDMI cable, if your phone supports video output, removes the network from the equation entirely. If the sound runs ahead of or behind the picture, check the TV: Wikipedia notes flat-panel TVs can delay video more than audio, and that consumer TVs, soundbars and receivers now offer audio-delay adjustments; [audio out of sync on Android](/blog/audio-out-of-sync-on-android/) covers the causes.

## Quality, HDR and data

- **Resolution**: mirroring shows your phone's screen, so the TV picture can only be as good as what the phone sends.
- **HDR**: HDR needs every link in the chain to support it; [HDR video on Android](/blog/hdr-video-on-android/) explains why.
- **Privacy**: mirroring shows everything on the phone's screen, including notifications, so turn on Do Not Disturb before showing the screen to a room.
- **Battery**: mirroring keeps the phone's screen and radio busy for the whole film; plug the phone in for anything long.
- **Data**: local files cast over home Wi-Fi do not use mobile data, but streaming apps on mobile data can; [stopping apps using mobile data](/blog/stop-apps-using-mobile-data/) covers the settings.
