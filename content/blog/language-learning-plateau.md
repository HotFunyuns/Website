**The intermediate plateau is mostly a measurement problem.** Progress does not stop; the units get bigger, and the same rate of improvement produces a much smaller visible change.

Understanding that first is important, because the usual response — study harder, in the same way — is the one thing that reliably does not work.

## Why the levels get bigger

Proficiency frameworks are not evenly spaced. Each level covers a wider range of situations than the one before, so moving up takes more.

At the start, a week's study visibly changes what you can do: yesterday you could not order coffee, today you can. At the intermediate stage, a week's study adds a handful of words and a construction to a repertoire that already handles most familiar situations. The same effort, a fraction of the visible return.

The [CEFR](https://europa.eu/europass/en/common-european-framework-reference-language-skills) makes this explicit by describing what a learner can do at each level rather than how long each takes — [CEFR levels explained](/blog/cefr-levels-explained/) covers the framework and the specific place most learners stall.

## Why beginner methods stop working

Structured courses are built around high-frequency material, and they are right to be: the most common words and structures do most of the work, so teaching them first is efficient.

The consequence is that a course has a natural end point. Once you have the frequent material, there is less for a sequenced course to give you, because what remains is individually rarer and does not sort neatly into lessons.

This is not a criticism of courses. It is what a course is *for*. But it means the plateau frequently arrives at exactly the point where your course finishes, and that coincidence is easy to misread as personal failure.

## The vocabulary curve

The same effect appears in vocabulary. Word frequency is very uneven: a small set of common words covers a large share of speech, and each additional thousand words covers progressively less.

At the beginning, every word you learn appears constantly. At the intermediate stage, the words remaining are individually rarer — so you learn one and do not see it again for a fortnight, which feels like no progress at all.

It is progress. It is just progress you cannot feel. [How many words do you need to be conversational](/blog/how-many-words-to-be-conversational/) covers the shape of that curve.

## What actually restarts movement

Four changes, in rough order of impact.

**1. Increase volume of input.** This is the big one. The intermediate stage needs breadth, and breadth comes from quantity: reading and listening to a great deal of material you mostly understand. [Comprehensible input explained](/blog/comprehensible-input-explained/) covers how to pitch the difficulty, and [how to practise listening in a new language](/blog/listening-practice-in-a-new-language/) covers the listening half.

**2. Move into unfamiliar topics.** Lower levels reward handling familiar situations better. Above that, the requirement is handling *unfamiliar* material. If everything you read is about the same subjects, you are practising rather than expanding.

**3. Get production feedback.** Errors that survive to the intermediate stage are usually ones input has not corrected, because you can understand a sentence perfectly without noticing the detail you get wrong. Someone has to tell you.

**4. Change how you measure.** Stop counting what you know and start testing what you can do. Reread something that defeated you three months ago; have a conversation you would have avoided. The evidence is usually there.

## What does not help

**Studying harder in the same way.** More flashcards from the same deck, more lessons at the same level.

**Restarting from the beginning.** A surprisingly common response, and a costly one — you re-learn material you already have and arrive back at the same place.

**Switching languages.** The plateau is a property of the stage, not of the language. The new one will produce the same thing at the same point.

**Waiting for motivation.** The demoralisation is downstream of the invisible progress. Fix the visibility and the motivation usually follows.

## Keeping the habit while the returns are thin

One practical note. Because the visible returns fall, the habit gets harder to maintain exactly when consistency matters most.

Distributed practice outperforms massed practice substantially for verbal recall — the effect synthesised by [Cepeda and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/) — so the short daily session is worth protecting even when it feels unproductive. Fifteen minutes daily during a plateau beats an ambitious schedule you abandon after a fortnight.
