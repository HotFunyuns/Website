A recording keeps everything; a transcript makes it usable. [Transcribe Audio to Text PRO](/apps/transcribe-audio-to-text/) sits at that hand-off, which is why it may interest three groups: **companies making creator tools**, **accessibility organisations** and **educators**. This page explains what the app does, what each group could build with it, and the accuracy commitments that any partnership would keep.

## What the app is built for

The listing frames transcription around four everyday uses:

- **Lectures**, reviewed alongside notes, with unfamiliar terms picked out to ask about.
- **Voice notes**, turned into a written start for a journal entry, plan or first draft.
- **Meetings**, used as a reference while writing up notes and follow-ups.
- **Interviews**, worked through before drafting an article or research.

The app's page on this site sets out a four-step workflow: start with recorded speech, transcribe it, **check the details** against the audio, then use the text. It is free with ads, and Google Play lists in-app purchases.

## The accuracy commitment

The listing is candid about limits, and partners would repeat it. **Accuracy depends on recording quality, background noise and how clearly people speak.** Names, numbers and important details should be checked against the original audio, **quotations especially, before anything is published**, and the original recording should be kept for tone and context. A partnership that described the app as error-free would contradict its own listing.

## For creator-tool companies

For podcasters, video makers and writers who work from recorded speech, a creator-tool company might discuss **integrations supported by the app's existing capabilities**, for example a hand-off from a recorder or editor; **cross-promotion** between complementary apps; **co-development** of features creators ask for; or **distribution**. Guidance that pairs transcription with a short checking routine would fit the listing's own advice.

## For accessibility organisations

Students and workers who find it hard to follow speech in real time may find it useful to read a recording afterwards. An accessibility organisation could explore **co-branded guidance** or an **educational and institutional partnership**. The framing matters: this is a way to review recorded speech at one's own pace, **not a live captioning service and not a substitute for professional captioning, note-taking or interpreting**. Organisations remain the experts on what their members need.

## For educators

Teachers and lecturers who allow recording can point students to transcription as a study aid. Ideas include study guides that turn a transcript into summaries, term lists and questions, and departmental recommendations. Recording permission stays with the institution and the lecturer.

## A worked example: a research interview workflow

A university research group records 12 one-hour interviews. If speech ran at 150 words a minute, the lower end of the 150–160 range Wikipedia gives as recommended for audiobooks, each hour would be about 9,000 words, so the set would be roughly 108,000 words of transcript; real interviews vary in pace. The group transcribes each file, then a researcher checks every quotation used in the paper against the audio and anonymises names. A partnership might package that routine as a guide; the researchers keep the recordings and their ethics approvals.

## Recorded speech is personal information

Every partner proposal in this area should start from consent. Recordings of lectures, meetings and interviews capture other people's voices and words, and recording laws differ between places; our guide to [transcribing an interview](/blog/how-to-transcribe-an-interview/) notes that some US states require every party's consent. Partner guidance would therefore tell users to get permission before recording, store recordings and transcripts somewhere access-controlled, and share only what a colleague needs. Reign Creative does not want recordings or transcripts sent to it as part of a partnership enquiry.

## Ownership changes on Google Play

Google Play moves apps between owners only through its **developer-account transfer**. Google's help explains that the original owner may want to **download bulk export, payout and earnings reports first**, because they do not transfer, while users, ratings, reviews and the store listing do. With in-app products in the app, the new account needs an active payments profile, and orders created before the transfer remain with the original account. Google's support team replies to transfer requests **within two business days** of the target developer's approval.
