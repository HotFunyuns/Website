A what-if scenario is worth doing under four rules: read the documented outcome first, change exactly one variable, stop after one step, and never cite the result as evidence. Follow those and a counterfactual becomes a decent test of whether a cause was doing real work. Ignore them and it becomes a memorable story that quietly replaces what you knew. If you want the definition and the scholarly debate about the genre itself, [counterfactual history and what-if scenarios](/blog/counterfactual-history-and-what-if-scenarios/) covers that ground; this article is the practice guide.

## Rule 1: The record goes first

Whatever you read first is what you are most likely to retain. So read the documented outcome before you play or write the alternative, and say it in your own words before you start.

This sounds fussy until you notice how often it is violated. Most games, videos and thought experiments present the branching choice first and the actual history afterwards, if at all. That ordering trains the wrong thing. Reverse it, and the alternative becomes something you are holding up against a known shape rather than something filling an empty space.

## Rule 2: Change one variable, and write it down

A counterfactual is only informative if you can attribute the difference to something. Change three things and you have written fiction, which is a legitimate activity but not an analytical one.

Write the changed variable as a sentence: "Suppose the harvest of that year had been average rather than catastrophic." Then ask what in the documented sequence depended on it and what did not. The interesting discovery is usually how much did *not* depend on it — most large outcomes are overdetermined, supported by several independent causes, and removing one leaves the outcome standing.

That is the real payoff. The exercise is a test for necessity, and most candidate causes fail it. The habit of asking which causes were necessary, which were merely contributing and which were background conditions is the same one used in ordinary causal analysis, and it is the subject of [cause and effect in history](/blog/cause-and-effect-in-history/).

## Rule 3: Stop after one step

Consequences of consequences are speculation compounded. The first step away from the record is disciplined by everything you know about the situation; the second is disciplined by the first step, which is invented. By the third, you are reasoning about a world with no sources at all.

This is the core of the professional objection to counterfactuals, and it is a fair one. It also marks exactly where the useful part ends. One step is analysis. Five steps is a novel.

## What the education research says

Tim Huijgen and Paul Holthuis make the positive case in *Historical Encounters*, an open-access journal published by the HERMES Research Network. They note that counterfactual historical reasoning is "very controversial" within the discipline itself, then argue from a classroom assignment developed at the University of Groningen that it can be used to "uncover and undermine assumptions, expand imagination, argue and reason from a historical context, ask historical questions and analyse sources."

Notice what is being claimed. The benefit is to reasoning, not to knowledge. A counterfactual does not tell you anything new about the past; it tells you something about the structure of your own explanation of the past, which is why it belongs in the method toolkit rather than in the evidence base.

The Historical Thinking Project's framework helps locate it. Among its six concepts are analysing cause and consequence — treating causation as "complex and multifaceted rather than simple or linear" — and taking historical perspectives, which means reconstructing what people at the time could know rather than judging from hindsight. A well-run counterfactual exercises both. A badly run one exercises neither, because hindsight is exactly what tells you which branch feels plausible.

## Rule 4: Never cite the result

The American Historical Association's Statement on Standards of Professional Conduct requires historians to "honor the integrity of the historical record" and to leave "a clear trail for subsequent historians to follow." A simulated outcome has no trail, because there is nothing behind it.

Practically, this means two things. Never quote a scenario outcome, a score or a game result as though it were a finding. And when writing, keep the invented branch and the documented record in separate sentences, each clearly labelled. "In the scenario, the fleet turns back" and "In the documented record, the fleet sailed" are two sentences on purpose.

If you want practice distinguishing record from interpretation on real material, The National Archives in the UK publishes free document-based lessons built around exactly that tension — asking, for instance, whether the Great Fire of London is better explained by "treachery or chance", and what Elizabethan and Jacobite propaganda was designed to achieve.

## How this maps onto the app we build

We should disclose the interest: World History Timeline Sim is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and its Time Travel mode is a large counterfactual engine with 2,497 scenarios, alongside 166 what-if matchups in a separate tournament mode.

The structure is what makes it usable under the rules above. Each scenario carries a separate "WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED" card, labels speculative results with a "SPECULATIVE OUTCOME" tag, and prints a footer reading "Alternate History Simulation • Speculative outcomes are educational, not factual". Read the actual-history card first — the app gives you the option, and Rule 1 says take it.

One feature needs an explicit warning. The Butterfly Effect Score that the mode returns is computed by counting keywords in the concatenated text of the scenario and the choice you picked. It is a gameplay and teaching device that responds to wording, not a historical measurement of anything, and the app's own result screens describe their scoring as a simple educational model based on your choices rather than a historical measurement. Never write down a Butterfly Effect Score as a result, and never treat a high one as evidence that a branch was plausible.

The wider limits apply here too. The app's content carries no citations and has had no external review, so scenarios are prompts rather than sources. And no app replaces a teacher, a scholarly book, an archive or a primary document; the app's own disclaimer screen says precisely that. ## A worked shape

Take a turning point you have read about properly — the [Bronze Age collapse](/blog/what-caused-the-bronze-age-collapse/) is a good candidate because the explanations genuinely compete. State the documented sequence. Change one variable, once. Ask which parts of the documented sequence still follow and which do not. Stop. Then write two sentences: what the record shows, and what your single change would have had to overcome.

If your answer is that almost everything still happens, you have learned that the variable was not load-bearing — and that is a real result about your explanation, arrived at without pretending to know anything about a world that never existed.
