**A "complete" protein contains all nine essential amino acids in useful proportions. An "incomplete" one is low in at least one.** That is the whole distinction, and most of the confusion around it comes from advice that was built on top of it and has since been relaxed.

## What "essential" means

Proteins are built from amino acids. The body can manufacture some of them itself; nine it cannot, and those have to come from food. Those nine are called **essential**.

"Essential" here is a statement about origin, not importance. All amino acids are needed; the essential ones are simply the ones your diet has to supply.

## The limiting amino acid

If a protein source is low in one essential amino acid relative to what the body needs, that amino acid **limits** how much of the protein can be used for building tissue. Having plenty of the other eight does not compensate.

That is why the distinction exists. A source that is low in one essential amino acid is less efficiently used for tissue building than one that is not, even if the total grams of protein are the same.

## Which foods are which

| Category | Typical status | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meat, poultry, fish | Complete | |
| Eggs, dairy | Complete | |
| Soya | Complete | Commonly cited plant exception |
| Quinoa | Complete | Also commonly cited |
| Most legumes | Lower in one essential amino acid | |
| Most grains | Lower in a different one | |
| Nuts and seeds | Varies | |

The pattern that made "protein combining" popular is visible in the last three rows: legumes and grains tend to be limited in *different* amino acids, so eating both covers the gap.

Exact amino acid composition for specific foods is available in the [USDA's FoodData Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/), which is the reference database for this kind of question.

## Why the combining rule was relaxed

For a period, the standard advice was to eat complementary plant proteins **at the same meal** — beans with rice, for example.

That specific requirement has been relaxed in mainstream guidance. The current emphasis is on the overall pattern of intake across a day rather than on pairing within a single sitting.

The practical effect for someone eating a varied plant-based diet with adequate total protein is that this stops being something to actively manage. Eating a range of protein sources over a day handles it.

## What this means if you are tracking

Three practical points:

**Total protein is still the main number.** The complete/incomplete distinction affects how efficiently a given amount is used; it does not replace the amount. [How much protein per day](/blog/how-much-protein-per-day/) covers what the published reference intakes say.

**Variety does the work.** A day containing several different protein sources is very likely to cover the essential amino acids without any calculation.

**Tracking apps count grams, not amino acids.** Almost no consumer tracker breaks protein down by amino acid, and that includes ours. What a tracker tells you is how much protein you ate, which is the more actionable number.

If you are eating entirely plant-based, or restricting your diet substantially, the amino acid question becomes more relevant — [plant versus animal protein](/blog/plant-vs-animal-protein/) covers that comparison, and it is a reasonable topic to raise with a registered dietitian rather than to resolve from articles.
