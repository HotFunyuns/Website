Most marks lost on a timed reasoning test are lost to time management rather than to ignorance. Three decisions decide the outcome: the seconds you allocate per item, the point at which you give up on an item, and what you do with the items you never solved. All three can be settled before the test starts, and two of them are arithmetic rather than judgement.

## Do the division before the first item

Every published test tells you the numbers. The UCAT allocates 44 Verbal Reasoning questions to 22 minutes, which is exactly 30 seconds per question; 36 Quantitative Reasoning questions to 26 minutes, about 43 seconds; 35 Decision Making questions to 37 minutes, about 63 seconds; and 69 Situational Judgement questions to 26 minutes, under 23 seconds each. Employer tests publish less, but the format screen almost always states the item count and the limit, and the division takes five seconds.

Two adjustments make the raw figure usable. First, data-interpretation sets share one table across several questions, so the first item in a set costs more than the rest — budget the reading time to the set rather than to the item. Second, keep a checkpoint rather than watching the clock continuously: at the halfway mark, compare items completed against items expected.

The recovery arithmetic matters more than the plan. Suppose a test has 30 items in 25 minutes, a budget of 50 seconds each. At the 14-minute mark you have finished 12, which is 70 seconds an item. The remaining 11 minutes is 660 seconds for 18 items — about 37 seconds each. That is the number that should change your behaviour, and noticing it at 14 minutes is worth far more than noticing it at 22.

## The trade-off is continuous, so pace is a dial

The instinct to "go faster" assumes accuracy holds until it suddenly collapses. Research on the speed-accuracy trade-off describes something smoother: "the tendency for decision speed to covary with decision accuracy seems an inescapable property of choice behavior", because "Faster responses entail less accumulated evidence, and hence less informed decisions."

Treating pace as a dial rather than a switch changes how you practise. The useful question is not "can I go faster" but "what does my error rate do when I move from 50 seconds to 40". That is measurable, and it differs by item type: on items where the work is arithmetic, speed costs relatively little; on items where the work is deciding what the question asks, speed is expensive, which is why [numerical reasoning tests](/blog/numerical-reasoning-tests-explained/) punish rushing so heavily.

University careers services frame the same point for employer tests: assessors look for both speed and accuracy, and not finishing is common rather than fatal. ETS gives a related warning for its own quantitative section — that most questions do not require difficult computation, so the on-screen calculator should not be used simply because it is there. Reaching for a tool has a time cost of its own.

## Set the abandonment rule in advance

An abandonment rule is a number you decide before the test and obey during it. Twice the per-question budget is a reasonable default: with a 45-second budget, anything unresolved at 90 seconds gets an answer entered and a flag for review.

The reason to fix it in advance is that the judgement is unreliable in the moment. Time already spent feels like an investment, and the item you have half-solved feels closer to solved than it is. Meanwhile the cost is invisible — it is the two or three straightforward items at the end you never see.

Flagging rather than skipping matters too. An item left blank with an intention to return is an item you may not return to; an item answered with a best guess and flagged is already worth something if you run out of time.

## What a guess is actually worth

This is pure expected value, and it takes one line.

**No penalty for wrong answers.** The UCAT states plainly that "There is no negative marking for incorrect answers." On a five-option item, a random guess is correct one time in five, so its expected value is 0.2 marks against 0 for a blank. Eight blank items filled at random are worth about 1.6 marks on average. Eliminating one option raises each guess to 1 in 4, or 0.25 marks. Under this scheme, leaving anything blank is strictly worse than guessing, and the only cost is the seconds spent clicking.

**A penalty scheme.** Some tests deduct a fraction of a mark for a wrong answer, classically a quarter mark on a five-option item. Then a random guess is worth (1/5 × 1) + (4/5 × −1/4) = 0.2 − 0.2 = **0** — exactly break-even, which is the point of choosing that fraction. Eliminate one option and it becomes (1/4 × 1) + (3/4 × −1/4) = 0.25 − 0.1875 = **+0.0625**, so informed guessing is worth something while blind guessing is not.

The rule that falls out is short: find out the marking scheme before the test. If there is no penalty, never leave a blank. If there is one, guess only when you have eliminated at least one option.

## Rehearse the conditions, not just the content

Content practice and condition practice are different things, and most people do only the first. Condition practice means working under a clock you cannot pause, tolerating the feeling of moving on from an unsolved item, and getting used to the fact that a run will contain errors.
