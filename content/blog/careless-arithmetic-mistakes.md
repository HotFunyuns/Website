**Most errors filed under "careless" are slips: the method was right and the execution failed.** That is a different kind of failure from getting the method wrong, and it needs a different response. Re-explaining a procedure to someone who already knows it will not reduce their slip rate, which is why "be more careful" is advice that has never worked for anyone.

This article separates the two, lists the small number of slips that arithmetic actually produces, and gives a checking routine short enough that you will run it.

## Slip or method error?

The distinction is borrowed from error research in fields where the consequences are serious. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality's [systems approach primer](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/primer/systems-approach) draws the line between schematic behaviour — things performed reflexively, as if on autopilot — and attentional behaviour, which involves conscious thought, analysis and planning. Slips are failures of the schematic kind, occurring under distraction, fatigue or stress. Mistakes reflect incorrect choices, and more often come from inexperience or insufficient training.

The primer's practical conclusion transfers cleanly. Organisations respond to all errors as though they were mistakes, reaching for remedial education and extra supervision, and that does nothing to stop the next person making the same slip. What works on slips is changing the task so the error is caught or cannot occur.

Applied to arithmetic, that means: if you knew to carry and did not, more explanation of carrying is wasted effort. Build a check that catches an uncarried digit instead.

## The five slips arithmetic actually produces

Almost every "silly" error in mental arithmetic is one of these.

**Place value and decimals.** The digits are right and the size is wrong. 0.4 × 0.6 becomes 2.4 instead of 0.24. Both factors are below 1, so the product must be smaller than either of them.

**Sign.** A negative gets absorbed or doubled. 15 − (−8) becomes 7 instead of 23. The countermeasure is deciding the sign of the answer before computing its size, as a separate step.

**Carry and borrow.** The commonest of all. 503 − 268 comes out as 245 or 345 rather than 235, because the borrow across the zero was skipped or applied twice.

**Transcription.** You compute correctly and record something else — reading 63 as 36, or copying an intermediate value wrong. This is invisible to any check applied to the numbers you wrote down, which is why it is worth re-reading the question rather than only the working.

**Dropped terms.** In a multi-step calculation, one part vanishes. 12 × 24 done as partial products is 12 × 20 + 12 × 4 = 240 + 48 = 288; drop the second part and you confidently produce 240.

That last category is where error research overlaps with the arithmetic literature. Han and colleagues, in [Memory Updating and Mental Arithmetic](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4735758/), found that numerical updating ability predicted multi-digit mental multiplication performance on harder problems specifically, consistent with the account that such problems require maintaining and updating intermediate results. Cragg and colleagues, in [When is working memory important for arithmetic?](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5724815/), found a working-memory load degraded performance across retrieval, decomposition and counting alike. Dropped terms are what running out of that capacity looks like from the outside, and they are not a failure of will.

## Two checks that take seconds

**Order of magnitude, first.** Before or immediately after calculating, decide roughly where the answer must land. A two-digit by two-digit product sits somewhere between the hundreds and the low thousands: 47 × 63 must be near 50 × 60 = 3,000. The true answer is 2,961 — computed as 47 × 60 + 47 × 3 = 2,820 + 141. An answer of 296 or 29,610 is rejected on sight, and every place-value and decimal slip in the list above is caught this way. The wider use of rough sizing is covered in [estimation techniques that make mental math useful](/blog/estimation-techniques-explained/).

**Last digit, second.** The final digit of a product depends only on the final digits of its factors. For 47 × 63, the last digits are 7 and 3, and 7 × 3 = 21, so the product must end in 1. It does. This costs one small multiplication and catches a large share of carry and transcription slips that magnitude checking cannot see, because a wrong digit in the middle usually changes the ending.

The two checks are complementary: magnitude catches errors of scale, last digit catches errors of detail. Running both on 19 × 21 takes about three seconds — near 20 × 20 = 400, and ending in 9 × 1 = 9 — and confirms 399 without redoing anything. Faster still, that particular product is a difference of squares, 20² − 1, one of the patterns in [multiplication shortcuts and why they work](/blog/multiplication-shortcuts-explained/).

## Build the routine into practice, not into resolutions

The reason checking fails is that it is treated as an act of virtue rather than a step in the procedure. Make it a step. Three rules are enough.

Decide the sign before the size. Estimate the magnitude before you commit. Check the last digit on any product before you move on. None of these requires the original calculation to be redone, which is why they survive contact with time pressure.

Then count your errors by type for a week. One category will dominate — it almost always does — and that is the category to build a countermeasure for. The What Works Clearinghouse [guide to mathematics intervention](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/26) lists systematic instruction and timed activities for fluency among its recommendations, and the order matters here: a check installed while the work is slow is a check that survives when the work is fast.

## Practising in conditions that surface errors

Errors are only fixable if you see them, and a score total hides them. What you want is per-question feedback while you are still thinking about the question, plus a record of what went wrong.
