A scouting combine is the stage of a football career that decides where a player is picked, and it is not the stage that decides how good the player is. Those two sentences are the whole argument, and the published research supports both halves of it more clearly than the annual coverage suggests.

This article covers what the event consists of, what the tests are, and what studies have found when they checked whether the tests do the job people assume.

## What the event actually is

NFL Football Operations describes the combine — formally the National Invitational Camp — as a four-day, invitation-only event at which scouts evaluate draft-eligible college players on medical, mental and physical criteria. Roughly 300 players are invited each year.

The public image is a stopwatch at the end of a 40-yard sprint. The schedule is mostly not that:

- **Medical examinations**, which are extensive and coordinated through a hospital partner. The scale is genuinely large; the president of the organisation that runs the event has described 365 MRIs being performed on 333 players across four days in 2013, each scan taking about an hour.
- **Interviews.** Each of the 32 clubs may conduct up to sixty fifteen-minute interviews with prospects. Sixty per club is a substantial share of the entire invited field.
- **Psychological and aptitude testing.**
- **Measurements** — height, weight, arm length, hand size — taken under standard conditions rather than reported by a college programme.
- **On-field testing**, the part that is televised.

For many players the medical is the single most consequential item, because an examination can reveal something that changes a club's valuation far more than a tenth of a second does. The event is often described as a four-day job interview, which is closer to the truth than "a track meet".

## The battery

The physical tests examined in the research literature on the event are:

| Test | What it is meant to capture |
| --- | --- |
| 10-, 20- and 40-yard dashes | Acceleration over short distance and straight-line speed |
| Bench press | Repetition strength at a fixed load |
| Vertical jump | Lower-body explosive power |
| Broad jump | Horizontal explosive power |
| 20- and 60-yard shuttles | Change of direction and short-area agility |
| Three-cone drill | Change of direction with a turn under control |
| Aptitude test | Cognitive processing under time constraint |

That table is the standard battery as recorded in the studies discussed below; the event has added and revised drills over time, including new movement drills trialled at international combine events before appearing at the main one.

Position-specific on-field work sits alongside the battery, which is where a quarterback throws, a receiver runs routes and a lineman is tested on footwork.

## What the research found

Two studies in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* are the ones worth knowing, because they ask the two different questions people conflate.

**Does the combine predict professional performance?** Kuzmits and Adams examined players drafted at three offensive positions — quarterback, running back and wide receiver — over the 1999 to 2004 period, correlating combine measures against ten performance criteria including draft order, three years each of salary and games played, and position-specific production. They report no consistent statistical relationship between combine tests and professional football performance, with the notable exception of sprint tests for running backs. The authors go on to question the overall usefulness of the event's physical component and suggest more rigorous psychological testing and the use of collegiate performance as a job sample.

**Does the combine predict where a player is drafted?** Robbins examined 1,155 players invited to the combine and drafted in the same year between 2005 and 2009, across 17 positions, testing raw data and two forms of normalisation. The primary finding was that performance in the physical test battery, normalised or not, had little association with draft success — and normalising provided no advantage over raw data. Of the eight measures examined, straight sprint time and jumping ability appeared to hold the most weight with the personnel responsible for draft decisions. The paper suggests the test battery could be revised to reflect the characteristics clubs actually value, and advises players to develop mental and technical skills alongside physical ones.

Read together, the picture is not "the combine is meaningless". It is narrower and more interesting: **the tests influence decisions more than they forecast careers**, and the specific tests that influence decisions are the sprint and the jumps.

## What that means for a prospect

If you are reasoning about the event — or about a simulated version of it — the implications are fairly direct.

**Train the tests that move the decision, not the ones that feel athletic.** Sprint time and jumping ability are the measures the evidence associates with decision-makers' weighting. The bench press is an inconsistent predictor of almost everything.

**Treat the medical as the main event.** It is the item most capable of moving a valuation sharply in either direction, and it is the one least amenable to preparation in the final month.

**Do not treat the interview as a formality.** Sixty interviews per club is a system designed to gather non-physical information, and the research keeps pointing at non-physical factors as the place the missing predictive power might live.

**Understand that stock and quality are separate variables.** A player can improve their draft position without improving as a player, and an evaluation system where that is possible will reward preparing specifically for the evaluation.
