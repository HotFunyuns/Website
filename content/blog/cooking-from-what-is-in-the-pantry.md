Half a cabbage, most of a bag of rice, three eggs and a jar of something. The question is not really "what can I make" — it is "which of the things I could make is still the thing it is supposed to be, given what is missing".

That distinction is the whole skill, and it is mostly a sorting exercise rather than a creative one.

## Sort the ingredients before you judge the gap

Every recipe's ingredient list contains three different kinds of item, and they fail differently.

**Structural.** These do a job. They bind, thicken, raise, set, emulsify, or make up the bulk of the dish. Eggs in a custard. Flour in a roux. Rice in a rice dish. Remove one and you are not making a compromised version of the recipe, you are making something else.

**Flavour.** These define the character. The spice blend, the herb, the acid, the aromatic. Remove one and you get a plainer version of the same dish, which is often perfectly good and occasionally a disappointment.

**Optional.** Garnishes, finishes, the fresh herb scattered on top for the photograph. Missing these costs nothing.

Once a list is sorted this way, "can I make this" becomes answerable in about five seconds. A missing optional item is not a gap. A missing flavour item is a decision. A missing structural item means pick a different recipe.

Most of the frustration with cook-from-what-you-have comes from treating all three categories as equally important, which makes almost every recipe look impossible.

## Why partial matches are the useful ones

An ingredient-matching tool that only shows you recipes you can make completely will show you very little, and it will hide its best answers.

The genuinely useful result is the recipe you are one item away from — because "one item away" is frequently solvable. You have a substitute. You can leave it out. It is the optional garnish. Or it is worth a five-minute walk to the shop, which is a decision you can only make if you know it is the only thing missing.

A complete-matches-only filter throws all of that away and gives you a list of four things you have made twice this week.

[Easy Recipes & Meal Planner](/apps/easy-recipes-meal-planner/) takes the other approach: the pantry tracker keeps a list of what is in your kitchen, surfaces recipe ideas based on ingredients you already have, and **shows what is missing before you commit to a meal**. The missing list is the feature, not the error state.

## The substitution rule we refused to automate

This is the part of the design we spent the longest on and shipped the least of.

Automatic substitution is an obvious feature. The app knows the recipe needs buttermilk, it knows you have milk and lemon juice, and it could simply say "use this instead" and mark the recipe as cookable. It looks intelligent and it demonstrates well.

We do not do it, for a reason that is easy to state and hard to engineer around: **a substitution is a change to what is in the dish, and the app cannot see who is eating it.**

The FDA recognises nine major food allergens — milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame, which was added as the ninth by the FASTER Act signed into law in 2021 — and packaged foods must declare them. Substitution routinely crosses those lines. Swapping dairy for a nut-based alternative, wheat flour for an almond-based one, soy sauce for something containing wheat: all are ordinary kitchen swaps, and all change the answer to "can this person eat it".

There are three further things the software cannot see. Whether the ingredient was structural or decorative in this particular recipe. Whether you have the technique to adjust for the difference. Whether the substitute is one you would actually enjoy.

So the app surfaces the gap and stops. You get told what is missing, clearly, and the decision about what to do with that stays with the person who knows the kitchen and the eaters. That is deliberately less clever, and it is much harder to get badly wrong.

The same reasoning drives the allergy log: it lets you record allergies and previous reactions and flags recipes when an ingredient may match something you recorded, and the flag is a prompt to check rather than an assurance about safety. Ingredients and labels still have to be confirmed for your own needs, which is what we say in the app and are saying here. How to keep a log that is actually useful is covered in [food allergy and reaction logs](/blog/tracking-food-allergies-and-reactions/).

## Substitutions that usually behave, and the ones that do not

Not advice about what to eat — just a note on which swaps tend to preserve a dish and which tend to redefine it.

**Usually fine:** one aromatic for another in the same family; one leafy green for another in a cooked dish; stock for water plus seasoning; one long-grain rice for another; dried herb for fresh at a reduced quantity.

**Changes the dish but still works:** one acid for another; one fat for another in a pan (though smoke points differ); one protein for another of similar cooking time.

**Rarely works on autopilot:** anything doing structural work. Flour substitutes in baking behave differently and are not one-for-one. Removing the fat from a pastry or the egg from a batter is not a reduction, it is a different recipe. Thickeners are not interchangeable by volume.

The general test: **ask what the ingredient is doing, not what it is.** If the answer is "providing structure", the swap needs a recipe written for it rather than a substitution.

## The rules that do not relax when you improvise

Cooking from what is around tends to involve more raw ingredients, more boards, and more improvisation at the counter — which is exactly when handling discipline slips.

The FDA's safe food handling guidance is worth keeping in view: wash hands with warm water and soap for at least 20 seconds before and after handling food; wash boards, dishes, utensils and counters with hot soapy water after preparing each food item; rinse fresh fruit and vegetables under running tap water, including those with skins and rinds you do not eat; keep raw meat, poultry, seafood and eggs separate from other foods in the trolley, the bags and the fridge; use one board for produce and a separate one for raw meat, poultry and seafood; never put cooked food back on a plate that held raw meat unless it has been washed in hot soapy water; and do not reuse a marinade used on raw food unless it is boiled first.

It also makes a point that matters more when you are improvising than when you are following a recipe: **colour and texture are unreliable indicators of safety**, and a food thermometer is the only way to be sure meat, poultry, seafood and egg products have reached a safe temperature.

## A workflow that takes five minutes

1. **Look, and record.** Actually open the cupboard. Working from memory is what produces the duplicate jar of paprika — keeping that list current is covered in [pantry inventory and grocery lists](/blog/pantry-inventory-and-grocery-lists/).
2. **Match, including near misses.** Shortlist anything you are one or two items away from.
3. **Sort the gaps.** Structural, flavour, optional. This eliminates most of the shortlist instantly.
4. **Decide substitutions yourself**, checking anything that affects who can eat it.
5. **Set the serving size before you start**, because scaling affects quantities and timings — [why doubling a recipe is not just doubling](/blog/recipe-scaling-and-serving-sizes/) covers what does not scale linearly.
6. **Cook, and update the list.** The inventory is only worth keeping if it stays roughly true.

Done regularly, this is also the cheapest form of planning there is: it works backwards from what would otherwise be thrown away.
