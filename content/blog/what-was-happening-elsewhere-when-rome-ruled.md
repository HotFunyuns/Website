While Rome dominated the Mediterranean, the Han dynasty held the central capitals of China and the overland routes running west from them, a city of monumental pyramids was rising in the Basin of Mexico, a kingdom on the Red Sea was minting its own power between the Roman and Persian worlds, geoglyphs were being cut into the Peruvian coastal plain, and Buddhist monasteries were being built into hillsides in the north-west of the Indian subcontinent. None of these were reacting to Rome. They were the other things that were happening.

## Setting the window

This article slices horizontally rather than vertically. Instead of following one state from beginning to end, it takes roughly the first five centuries CE and asks what was going on across the map at the same time.

That is a different exercise from the usual one, and it produces a different picture. Rome is a reasonable anchor because most readers already have its outline, and because the arguments about its trajectory — surveyed in [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/) — are the familiar reference points. But it is an anchor, not the subject.

## China: two capitals and the roads west

For much of this window the Han dynasty governed from Chang'an and Luoyang. UNESCO's World Heritage inscription for the Silk Roads: the Routes Network of Chang'an-Tianshan Corridor describes a 5,000 km section running from Chang'an and Luoyang, the central capitals of China under the Han and Tang dynasties, to the Zhetysu region of Central Asia. The inscription states that the network took shape between the second century BC and the first century AD and remained in use until the sixteenth century, linking multiple civilisations and carrying trade, religious belief, scientific knowledge, technological innovation, cultural practice and art.

Its thirty-three listed components give a sense of what such a network physically consisted of: capital cities and palace complexes of various empires and khanates, trading settlements, Buddhist cave temples, ancient paths, posthouses, passes, beacon towers, sections of the Great Wall, fortifications, tombs and religious buildings.

The Han did not last the whole window. China divided into competing states after it, and the political map of the third to sixth centuries is as complicated as the contemporaneous Mediterranean one. Our introduction to [the Silk Road](/blog/the-silk-road-for-beginners/) covers who actually moved goods along these routes and why the modern name is younger than the thing it names.

## Mesoamerica: Teotihuacan

About 50 km north-east of present-day Mexico City, UNESCO describes the city of Teotihuacan as built between the first and seventh centuries AD, characterised by the vast size of its monuments — in particular the Temple of Quetzalcoatl and the Pyramids of the Sun and the Moon — laid out on geometric and symbolic principles. It calls the city one of the most powerful cultural centres in Mesoamerica, one that extended its cultural and artistic influence throughout the region and beyond.

The chronology is worth sitting with. A planned monumental city was under construction in the Basin of Mexico across the same centuries in which Roman power peaked and then reorganised, with no contact between the two and no awareness on either side. Pre-Columbian studies is a substantial research field in its own right — Dumbarton Oaks has run a dedicated programme in it alongside its Byzantine one for decades — and treating it as a parallel subject rather than an appendix is the point of an exercise like this.

## The Horn of Africa: Aksum

UNESCO's inscription for Aksum, near Ethiopia's northern border, describes massive ruins dating from between the first and the thirteenth century AD: monolithic obelisks, giant stelae, royal tombs and the remains of ancient castles. It identifies the site as the heart of ancient Ethiopia, when the Kingdom of Aksum was the most powerful state between the Eastern Roman Empire and Persia — and notes that long after its political decline in the tenth century, Ethiopian emperors continued to be crowned there.

That phrasing does something useful. It positions Aksum by its own weight, as one of three major powers in a connected zone, rather than as a periphery of either of the others.

## The Andes: lines on the coastal plain

In the arid Peruvian coastal plain some 400 km south of Lima, the geoglyphs of Nasca and the pampas of Jumana cover about 450 square kilometres. UNESCO dates the lines to between 500 BC and AD 500 and describes them as depicting living creatures, stylised plants and imaginary beings, alongside geometric figures several kilometres long.

Note how the same source handles interpretation: the geoglyphs are described as being *believed* to have had ritual astronomical functions. That hedge is doing real work, and it should survive being quoted. The dating and the physical description are one kind of claim; the purpose is another, and much less settled.

## South Asia: monasteries built into the hills

UNESCO's inscription for the Buddhist Ruins of Takht-i-Bahi, in present-day Pakistan, records a monastic complex founded in the early first century, exceptionally well preserved because of its position on the crest of a high hill, with the ruins of a small fortified city, Sahr-i-Bahlol, nearby and dating from the same period.

Monastic architecture at that scale implies patronage, surplus and durable institutions. It is the same kind of evidence as a Roman aqueduct or a Mesoamerican pyramid — a society investing in permanent stone for something it expected to last.

The habit this article is really recommending is running a fixed date range across every region rather than down one.
