Thai vowels have two features that English speakers are not used to: **length**, where a short and a long vowel are different sounds that make different words, and **position**, where the vowel sign can sit on any side of its consonant. This guide covers both, along with the vowels that are not written at all.

## How many Thai vowels there are

Thai Wikipedia's article on vowel length gives the count used in standard Thai textbooks: **32 vowel sounds**, **18 short and 14 long**. They form **14 short–long pairs** (28 sounds) plus **4 unpaired short vowels**:

| Group | Pairs | Sounds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple vowels | 9 | 18 |
| Diphthongs: เอียะ–เอีย, เอือะ–เอือ, อัวะ–อัว | 3 | 6 |
| Extra vowels in pairs: ฤ–ฤๅ, ฦ–ฦๅ | 2 | 4 |
| Unpaired extra vowels: อำ, ไอ, ใอ, เอา | – | 4 |
| **Total** | **14** | **32** |

English Wikipedia adds that the script builds these from **21 traditionally named vowel symbol elements**, used alone or in combination, and that ฦ and ฦๅ are obsolete.

## The nine simple pairs

Each pair below is shown in its open-syllable form, with ◌ marking the consonant. The rough English comparisons come from Wikipedia's chart and are only approximations.

| Short | Sounds like | Long | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ◌ะ | u in "nut" | ◌า | a in "father" |
| ◌ิ | y in "greedy" | ◌ี | ee in "see" |
| ◌ึ | oo in "look", lips unrounded | ◌ือ | oo in "goose", lips unrounded |
| ◌ุ | oo in "shoot" | ◌ู | oo in "too" |
| เ◌ะ | e in "neck" | เ◌ | a in "lame" |
| แ◌ะ | a in "at" | แ◌ | a in "ham" |
| โ◌ะ | oa in "boat" | โ◌ | o in "go" |
| เ◌าะ | o in "not" | ◌อ | aw in "saw" |
| เ◌อะ | e in "the" | เ◌อ | u in "burn" |

Notice how often the short form simply adds ◌ะ to the long one. Notice too that the official romanisation writes both members of each pair the same way, *a* and *a*, *i* and *i*, which is why romanised Thai cannot show length.

## Where the signs go

Wikipedia describes vowel signs placed **above, below, left or right** of the consonant, or in combinations of those places. In practice:

- **After**: า, ะ, อ
- **Above**: ◌ิ, ◌ี, ◌ึ, ◌ื, ◌ั
- **Below**: ◌ุ, ◌ู
- **Before**: เ, แ, โ, ใ, ไ
- **Around**: เ◌าะ, เ◌ีย, เ◌ือ

A sign written **before** the consonant is still pronounced **after** it. And when a syllable starts with a consonant cluster, a vowel with parts on both sides **wraps the whole cluster**. An example is เฉพาะ, *chapho*, which Wiktionary glosses as "specific" or "particular" and pronounces ฉะ-เพาะ: the เ–าะ surrounds ฉพ, and the ฉ carries an unwritten *a*.

## Vowels that change shape

Several vowels look different when a final consonant follows. From Wikipedia's chart:

| Open syllable | With a final consonant |
| --- | --- |
| ◌ะ | ◌ั◌ (mai han akat) |
| เ◌ะ | เ◌็◌ |
| แ◌ะ | แ◌็◌ |
| โ◌ะ | no sign at all: ◌◌ |
| ◌ัว | ◌ว◌ |
| เ◌อ | เ◌ิ◌ |

## Hidden vowels

Some syllables have no written vowel. Wikipedia explains that the **inherent vowel** is *a* in open syllables and *o* in closed ones. Its example is ถนน, "road": three consonants, no vowel signs, read *thanon*, with *a* after ถ and *o* between the two น.

## ไ, ใ and the other extra vowels

Both ไ and ใ spell *ai*. Wikipedia notes that **mai malai (ไ)** is used in most words and **mai muan (ใ)** in only **twenty specific words**, so the ใ words are worth learning as a short list. The other extras are ◌ำ, *am*, and เ◌า, *ao*.

## When length is hard to see

Thai Wikipedia lists the forms that mislead learners. With a tone mark, the usually long ◌า, เ◌, แ◌ and ◌อ can be short, as in ช่าง, เก่ง, แท่น and ป้อม. The usually short ◌ำ, ไ◌, ใ◌ and เ◌า can be long, as in น้ำ, ไม้, ใต้ and เช้า. These are learned word by word.

## Why length matters

Length is one of the four inputs to tone. A syllable ending in a short vowel is **dead**, which changes its base tone. Compare two mid-class syllables: **กา**, long and live, has a **mid** tone; **กะ**, short and dead, has a **low** tone.
