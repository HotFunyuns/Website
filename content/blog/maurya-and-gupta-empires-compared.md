The Maurya and Gupta empires are routinely filed together as India's two great ancient states, and the pairing hides more than it explains. They are separated by roughly six centuries, they were assembled on different principles, and — the part that matters most for anyone trying to learn them — they survive in different kinds of evidence, so we can say confident things about one that we can only guess at for the other.

Columbia University's Asia for Educators timeline places the Mauryan empire at about 324–200 BCE, founded by Chandragupta and extended south by his grandson Ashoka, and the Gupta empire at about 320–500 CE, with successive invasions from Central Asia breaking Gupta power from the later fifth century. The similarity between the names Chandragupta Maurya and the Gupta founder Chandragupta I is a coincidence of naming, not a dynastic link.

## The structural difference: administration versus hegemony

The standard contrast is between a centralised administrative empire and a looser hegemony.

The Mauryan state is described in most accounts as governed outward from Pataliputra through appointed officials, provincial centres and a network of roads, with revenue assessment and a standing army. That picture comes partly from inscriptions and partly from texts such as the *Arthashastra*, whose date and relationship to actual Mauryan practice are themselves debated — it is a manual of statecraft, not a Mauryan government record, and reading it as a transcript of how the empire worked is a leap historians argue about.

The Gupta state looks different. Subordinate rulers appear to have kept their thrones, their titles and much of their revenue in exchange for acknowledgement, tribute and military support. That arrangement can produce an impressive map and a weak centre at the same time, and it helps explain why Gupta power fragmented quickly once sustained pressure arrived from the north-west.

Neither model is unique to India. The same tension between direct rule and delegated rule runs through the argument over [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/), where part of the disagreement is about whether the western empire collapsed or simply devolved into successor arrangements that had already been functioning for decades.

## The evidence difference, and why it changes the questions

Here is the sharper point. The two empires do not just differ in structure; they differ in what has survived.

For the Mauryas, the outstanding source is a body of inscriptions carved into rock faces and polished stone pillars across a wide area — the pillar at Prayagraj (Allahabad) has been studied as an Ashokan monument in the epigraphic literature for well over a century. These are royal texts, and they are propaganda in the plain sense that they present a ruler as he wished to be seen. They are also close to the event, fixed in place, and readable today, which is a rare combination for the third century BCE.

For the Guptas, the richest sources are coins, Sanskrit praise-inscriptions produced by court poets, religious architecture and sculpture, and literature written down or transmitted later. Praise-inscriptions are a genre with conventions: a list of conquered kings in a panegyric tells you what a court wanted claimed, and only indirectly what a court controlled.

This is not a ranking of reliability. Both bodies of evidence need the same handling, which is the subject of [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/): work out who produced a document, for whom, and what it was for, before deciding what it can support.

## What the monuments actually date to

Buildings are often used as shorthand for dynasties, and the dates rarely line up as neatly as the shorthand suggests.

UNESCO records that most of the Buddhist monuments at Sanchi date back to the 2nd and 1st centuries BCE, and describes the site as a major Buddhist centre in India until the 12th century CE — a span that runs straight through both empires and well past them. At Ajanta, UNESCO dates the first Buddhist cave monuments to the 2nd and 1st centuries BCE and records that many more richly decorated caves were added during the Gupta period in the 5th and 6th centuries CE. Nalanda, inscribed separately, comprises remains of a monastic and scholastic institution dating from the 3rd century BCE to the 13th century CE.

The pattern is worth internalising: religious sites accumulate. A cave, a stupa or a monastery is usually the work of several centuries and several patrons, so "Gupta-era Ajanta" means a specific phase of a much longer site, not the site itself.

## Why "golden age" is an argument

The Gupta period carries the golden-age label in most popular accounts, usually on the strength of Sanskrit drama and poetry, mathematical and astronomical work, and the sculpture and painting of the period.

The criticism of the label is not that the achievements are invented. It is that a courtly and elite output is being used to characterise an entire society, that the phrase implies the surrounding centuries were correspondingly dark, and that nineteenth- and twentieth-century writers had political reasons for wanting a classical Indian high point to point to. When you meet the phrase, the useful question is not "is it true?" but "achievement in what, for whom, and compared with what?"

The same caution applies to periodisation generally, which [the major eras of world history](/blog/major-eras-of-world-history/) treats as a set of arguments rather than a set of facts.

## The short version

Maurya: earlier, more centralised, unusually well attested by a ruler's own inscriptions, and a genuine administrative experiment.

Gupta: later, looser, richer in art and literature, and known mostly through evidence produced by people with a stake in how it would be remembered.

Calling either one a golden age tells you something about the historian and not much about the century.
