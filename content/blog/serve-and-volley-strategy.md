Serve and volley is usually discussed as a disposition — the bold, old-fashioned way to play. It is better understood as arithmetic. You are spending the time the ball takes to travel, buying position with it, and betting that the position is worth more than the risk of being passed. On some surfaces, at some scores, against some returners, it plainly is. On others it plainly is not, and no amount of conviction changes that.

## What the tactic actually buys

The mechanics are simple: the server moves quickly toward the net after serving, intending to hit a volley — a ball struck before it bounces — rather than staying back for a groundstroke. The aim is to put immediate pressure on the opponent and end points quickly.

Two things happen at once when you do it.

**You take time away from the returner.** They now have to produce a passing shot or a lob under time pressure, and they have to produce it while the geometry of the court is closing.

**You take time away from yourself.** You are volleying a ball that has travelled less distance, with less time to read it, from a position you reached at a sprint. Both sides of the exchange get harder, and the bet is that it gets harder faster for them than for you.

That framing explains why the tactic is surface-sensitive rather than fashion-sensitive. Anything that gives the returner more time — a higher bounce, a slower court, a deflated ball — shifts the arithmetic against you.

## Why it faded, honestly

The common story is that players stopped being brave. The better-supported story is about equipment and courts.

Serve and volley was common at the top of the sport through the twentieth century and diminished sharply in the twenty-first. The reason usually given is technological: advances in racquet and string technology allow players to generate a great deal of topspin on groundstrokes and passing shots, which made those shots far more competitive with volleys.

There is a second-order effect that is easy to miss. As equipment made the sport faster, court surfaces were slowed and balls deflated in order to promote longer rallies. That intervention was aimed at preventing matches becoming strings of aces. Its side effect was to devalue serve and volley further, because the extra time it created went to the returner.

So the tactic did not lose an argument. The conditions it depended on were deliberately changed, for reasons that had nothing to do with it.

## The three questions to ask before you go in

Treat it as a per-point decision. Three inputs decide most of the answer.

**1. Is the ball going to stay low and fast?** Grass and quick hard courts keep the ball low; clay slows it and kicks it up. Court surface is the single largest environmental variable in tennis, and it moves this decision more than anything else.

**2. Has the serve moved the returner?** A serve that pulls the returner wide creates the open court you are advancing into. A serve down the middle to a returner standing comfortably is an invitation for them to hit through you. The serve is not a signal to run; it is the thing that either does or does not create the opportunity.

**3. Where is the returner standing?** A returner standing deep has to cover ground before they can hit, and a dipping topspin pass gets harder from further back. A returner standing on the baseline taking the ball early is in the best possible position to punish you.

If two of the three are wrong, stay back. The tactic does not punish you for declining it.

## Chip and charge, and the returner's version

The mirror image is chip and charge: the returner slices a low return and follows it forward, forcing the server to hit up off a ball at their shoetops.

It carries the same logic and the same limits. It works when the return can be kept low, when the server is unprepared for a short ball, and when the surface does not hand the server time to reorganise. It works badly against a big first serve, because you never get the controlled slice the plan requires.

The general principle behind both is the same: the value of coming forward is decided by what the *previous* shot did, not by how committed you are to coming forward.

## Pricing the decision in code

Building this into [Tennis Career Simulator 2026](/apps/tennis-career-sim/) meant deciding what the serve-and-volley option should cost, and the first version got it wrong in a way that took a while to see.

Our initial model priced the decision mainly against the player's own net attributes. Good volleyer, good outcome. It produced a serve-and-volley specialist who was simply strong, and players quickly learned to take the forward option on every available point because there was no reason not to.

What shipped prices the decision against **two external factors first** — the surface, and the opponent's return quality — and only then against your own attributes. A serve-and-volley specialist on a fast surface against a deep returner gets a genuinely good option. The same player on a slow surface against a returner who takes the ball early gets an option that is available and bad. The style still opens the door; the situation decides whether walking through it is sensible.

The balance pass that changed this was uncomfortable, because it made the serve-and-volley style measurably harder to play than the baseline styles. That is the honest state of the tactic in the modern game, and reproducing the difficulty felt more truthful than flattening it. Players who want an easier ride are better served by one of the other five options in [tennis playing styles](/blog/tennis-playing-styles/).

**What the model does not represent:** the physical grind. A real serve-and-volley career carries a specific injury and fatigue profile from repeatedly sprinting forward and stopping. The simulation tracks general fatigue and form but does not model style-specific wear. It is a known gap rather than a design position.
