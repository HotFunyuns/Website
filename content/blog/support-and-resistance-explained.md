On many price charts you can see prices bouncing off certain levels more than once, falling to roughly the same low or stalling near the same high. Chart readers call these levels **support** and **resistance**. The idea is intuitive. It is also easy to overrate, because levels that look obvious afterwards were rarely obvious at the time.

## The definitions

StockCharts' ChartSchool describes support and resistance as key junctures where supply and demand meet:

- **Support** is the price level at which demand is thought to be strong enough to prevent the price from declining further. As the price falls toward it and gets cheaper, buyers become more inclined to buy and sellers less inclined to sell.
- **Resistance** is the price level at which selling is thought to be strong enough to prevent the price from rising further.

Note the hedged wording in the source: "thought to be", "it is believed". These are descriptions of how traders reason about levels, not physical barriers.

## Where the levels come from

ChartSchool's practical rule: support can be established with previous reaction lows, and resistance with previous reaction highs.

**A hypothetical example.** A stock falls to $40 in March, recovers to $48, falls back to $40.50 in May, and recovers again. Chart readers would mark a support zone around $40 to $40.50 and resistance near $48. If the price later approaches $40 a third time, they will watch whether buyers appear again.

Because prices often poke briefly through a level, ChartSchool notes it does not seem logical to call a level broken when the price closes a fraction beyond it, and that some traders establish **zones** rather than exact lines.

## Role reversal

ChartSchool describes another widely used principle: support can turn into resistance and vice versa. Once a price breaks below support, the broken level can act as resistance if the price returns to it; once it breaks above resistance, that level can act as support.

ChartSchool's explanation is about supply and demand. A break below support signals that the forces of supply have overcome demand, so if the price returns to that level, extra supply is likely to appear; a breakout above resistance shows demand has overwhelmed supply, so a return to the level is likely to find buyers.

## A hypothetical walk-through

Suppose a stock has turned down near $60 three times over several months, so chart readers mark resistance there.

1. **Breakout.** One day it closes at $61.50 on heavy volume. Readers who follow the role-reversal idea now treat $60 as possible support.
2. **Retest.** Over the next week it drifts back to $60.20 and turns up again. In hindsight, the old resistance "held" as support.
3. **Or a false breakout.** Alternatively, it slips back to $58 within two days. The breakout failed, and anyone who bought at $61.50 on the strength of it is now sitting on a loss.

Both outcomes are possible, and nothing on the chart at step 1 reliably tells you which will happen. That is the core limitation of the method.

## Why levels break

ChartSchool is clear that support does not always hold, and a break below it signals that sellers have overwhelmed buyers; resistance does not always hold either. Reasons levels fail include:

- **New information.** Earnings or news can carry a price straight through a level that held for months.
- **Hindsight.** Many levels are identified after the fact; in real time there are often several plausible levels.
- **Crowded stops.** If many traders place [stop orders](/blog/stop-order-vs-stop-limit-order/) just beyond an obvious level, a move through it can trigger them together. The SEC's investor bulletin warns that a stop order becomes a market order once triggered and can execute well away from its stop price, so a burst of triggered stops can make a move through a level sharper rather than softer.

ChartSchool's broader overview adds a general caution: not all technical signals and patterns work, and what works for one stock may not work for another.

## Using levels without over-trusting them

- **Describe, then interpret.** Note where prices have turned before, without assuming they will again.
- **Check the context:** the trend, [trading volume](/blog/trading-volume-explained/) and any upcoming news.
- **Combine with other views.** ChartSchool notes [moving averages](/blog/moving-averages-explained/) can be used to define potential support and resistance levels, with the same caveats.
- **Keep the business in mind.** A company's results can matter more than any line on a chart; [technical versus fundamental analysis](/blog/technical-vs-fundamental-analysis/) compares the two ways of thinking.
