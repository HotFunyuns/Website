**The demographic labels people use for anime — shōnen, shōjo, seinen — describe who a work was aimed at, not how it looks.** Visual conventions do cluster around them, because publications develop house tendencies, but treating them as style names produces confusion the first time you meet a work that breaks the pattern.

What actually varies is a shorter, more useful list.

## The five variables

### 1. Line weight

Whether lines are uniform or vary in thickness, and how heavy they are overall.

Heavy, varied linework reads as energetic and physical. Thin, uniform linework reads as delicate or clinical. This is the single fastest way to change the feel of a drawing without changing anything else.

### 2. Eye construction

Eyes carry most of the expression in the medium, which is why they are the most variable feature:

- **Size relative to the face.** Larger eyes read younger and more emotive.
- **Shape.** Rounded versus angular changes perceived personality substantially.
- **Amount of white shown.** More visible sclera reads as alert or startled.
- **Highlight placement.** The number, size and position of catchlights.
- **Iris detail.** From a flat block of colour to elaborate gradients and rings.

If you change nothing else and change the eyes, the style changes.

### 3. Facial proportion

Where the eyes sit vertically, how large the cranium is relative to the jaw, how much nose and mouth are simplified. Small shifts here move a design between "young", "mature" and "stylised" without any other change.

### 4. Colour treatment

Saturated versus muted, warm versus cool, high contrast versus low. This is largely independent of the linework, which is why the same drawing can be coloured to read completely differently.

The mechanics of specifying colour precisely — hue, saturation, lightness, and the various colour spaces — are defined in [the W3C's CSS Color Module](https://www.w3.org/TR/css-color-4/), which is a surprisingly readable reference for how colour is described numerically. [Colour palettes for anime art](/blog/color-palettes-for-anime-art/) covers choosing them.

### 5. Shading method

The one with the clearest production reason behind it.

**Cel shading** applies tone as hard-edged blocks rather than smooth gradients. It originates in animation practicality: flat regions of colour are far faster to reproduce consistently across thousands of frames than gradients are.

**Soft shading** uses gradients and blending. More common in illustration than in television animation, for the same reason.

Most anime-styled work uses cel shading or something close to it, which is why it reads as "anime" even in still images.

## Why the demographic labels persist

They are useful for finding things — knowing a work is shōnen tells you something real about its likely themes and pacing. They are just not descriptions of how it looks.

[Anime genres explained](/blog/anime-genres-explained/) covers what the categories actually denote and where they overlap. The [Anime News Network lexicon](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/encyclopedia/lexicon.php) is a reasonable reference for the terminology.

The practical version: **if someone says "draw in a shōnen style", ask which of the five variables they mean.** The answer is usually heavier linework and higher contrast, but it is worth confirming rather than guessing.

## What this changes if you are colouring

If you are working on existing line art rather than drawing it, three of the five variables are already decided. The two you control are colour treatment and shading method — and those are enough to change the piece substantially.

The first decision is not which colours to use. It is **hard shading or soft**:

| | Cel / hard shading | Soft shading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Look | Animation-like, graphic | Illustrative, painterly |
| Speed | Faster | Slower |
| Forgiveness | Less — edges are visible | More — errors blend |
| Suits | Bold line art, high contrast | Fine line art, subtle designs |

Deciding this before you start saves redoing the piece, which is the most common way a colouring session goes wrong.
