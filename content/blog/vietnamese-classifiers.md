To say "four chickens" in Vietnamese you say *bốn con gà*, four, a classifier, chicken. That middle word is not optional decoration: Wikipedia's Vietnamese grammar article says classifiers are required when a quantifier is present, with a few named exceptions. This guide covers the two classifiers you will use most, the shape-based ones, where they sit in the noun phrase, and which nouns go without. Vietnamese is written here in its standard alphabet with tone marks, which are part of each word; see [Vietnamese tones explained](/blog/vietnamese-tones-explained/) if the marks are new.

## What a classifier does

Wikipedia's article on classifiers defines them as words that accompany nouns and "classify" them by features of what they refer to, such as animacy or shape. In languages that have them, "three people" is expressed as something like "three *X* of people", the way English says "three blades of grass". The Vietnamese grammar article adds that Vietnamese uses a rich set of classifiers and measure words to introduce or stand in for nouns, similar to the system in Chinese.

## Cái and con: the two you meet first

**Cái** introduces most inanimate objects. **Con** generally introduces animate things, especially animals. Wikipedia notes that the most common classifiers typically do not translate into English, and gives this exchange:

- *Bán cho tôi bốn **con** gà.* "Sell me four chickens."
- *Bao nhiêu?* "How many?"
- *Bốn **con**.* "Four [of them]."

The last line shows a second job: once the noun is known, the classifier can stand in for it, so *bốn con* alone means "four of them".

The surprise is that con is not limited to living things. Wikipedia lists **dao** (knife), **đường** (road), **mắt** (eye), **sông** (river) and **vít** (screw) as nouns that take con, explaining that it conveys motion. Learn those as a set rather than trying to derive them.

## Classifiers by shape and kind

Beyond cái and con, Wikipedia lists these among the most common:

| Classifier | Used for |
| --- | --- |
| chiếc | much like cái, usually more connotative, for example for a cute object |
| người | people, except infants |
| quả / trái | round objects: fruit, the Earth |
| quyển / cuốn | book-like objects: books, journals |
| tờ | sheets of paper: newspapers, calendars |
| lá | smaller sheets: letters, playing cards |
| tấm | flat, rectangular objects: signs, panes of glass |
| cây | stick-like objects: plants, guns, canes |
| bài | compositions: songs, poems, essays |
| câu | sentences, verses, quotes |
| tòa | buildings of authority: courts, halls |
| chuyện | a topic or matter |
| việc | an event or ongoing process |

Wikipedia lists *quả* and *trái*, and *quyển* and *cuốn*, as alternatives for the same kind of noun, so you will meet both members of each pair. Everyday vocabulary also varies by region in other ways; [northern vs southern Vietnamese](/blog/northern-vs-southern-vietnamese/) covers the best-known differences.

## Where the classifier sits in the noun phrase

Wikipedia sets out the full order of a Vietnamese noun phrase:

**totality + article + quantifier + classifier + head noun + attributive modifiers + demonstrative + possessive phrase**

So numbers and plural words come before the classifier, while adjectives, "this" and "that", and possessors come after the noun. Plural articles work the same way: *các quả cam*, "(the) oranges", puts *các* before the classifier.

## A worked example: building one long noun phrase

Wikipedia's example is *cả hai cuốn từ điển Việt Anh này của nó*, "both of these Vietnamese-English dictionaries of his". Taken slot by slot:

1. **cả**: totality, "all".
2. **hai**: quantifier, "two".
3. **cuốn**: classifier for books.
4. **từ điển**: head noun, "dictionary".
5. **Việt Anh**: attributive modifier, "Vietnamese-English".
6. **này**: demonstrative, "this".
7. **của nó**: possessive phrase, "of his".

Seven slots, and the classifier is the hinge between the counting words on its left and the describing words on its right. Wikipedia's Classifier article gives a second count with a different classifier: *ba bộ áo dài*, three sets of áo dài.

## Nouns that skip the classifier

Wikipedia names three groups of "non-classified nouns" that can follow a number directly:

- **time units**, such as *phút*, "minute";
- **geographical and administrative units**, such as *tỉnh*, "province";
- **polysyllabic Sino-Vietnamese compound nouns**.

Two further notes from the same article: some linguists count as many as 200 classifiers, though only a few are used in conversation or informal writing, and the use of cái for inanimate objects has increased at the expense of some rarer classifiers. Cái can even introduce another classifier, as in *cái con*, though Wikipedia records that one linguist treats that as a different use of cái.

## Demonstratives come last

Because "this" and "that" follow the noun, classifier phrases often end with one. Wikipedia lists *này* "this", *đó* and *ấy* "that", *kia* "that yonder" and *nào* "which", with examples such as *bà này*, "this lady", and *người nào*, "which person". Note *người* there: the same word that counts people also means "person". [Vietnamese numbers](/blog/vietnamese-numbers/) covers the numbers that go in the quantifier slot.
