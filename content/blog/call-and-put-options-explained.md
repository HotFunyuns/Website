Options are a different kind of instrument from ordinary shares. FINRA describes an option as a derivative that conveys to its purchaser the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell a set quantity of a particular asset at a fixed price by a set date. That "right, not obligation" is what makes options flexible, and the obligations on the other side of the contract are what can make them dangerous.

## The vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Underlying | The asset the option is based on, such as a stock or ETF |
| Call | The right to **buy** the underlying at the strike price |
| Put | The right to **sell** the underlying at the strike price |
| Strike price | The fixed price at which the option can be exercised |
| Expiration date | The last day the contract is valid |
| Premium | The price the buyer pays for the option |
| Writer | The seller, who receives the premium and takes on the obligation |
| Contract size | FINRA notes a standard equity option equals 100 shares |

FINRA states the obligations precisely: the seller of a call must sell the underlying at the strike price if assigned; the seller of a put must buy it.

## A call, worked through

You buy one call on a stock at a $50 strike, expiring in two months, for a premium of $2.00 per share. Because the contract covers 100 shares, it costs **$200** before fees.

| Stock price at expiration | Option value | Profit or loss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $45 | $0 (expires worthless) | −$200 |
| $50 | $0 | −$200 |
| $52 | $2 × 100 = $200 | $0 (break-even) |
| $56 | $6 × 100 = $600 | +$400 |

The most the call buyer can lose is the premium. The break-even is the strike plus the premium. Notice the second row: the stock did not fall, yet the whole premium was lost, because it did not rise enough before expiry.

## A put, worked through

You buy one put at a $50 strike for $1.50 per share, $150 per contract.

| Stock price at expiration | Option value | Profit or loss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $52 | $0 | −$150 |
| $48.50 | $1.50 × 100 = $150 | $0 (break-even) |
| $44 | $6 × 100 = $600 | +$450 |

A put rises in value as the underlying falls. FINRA notes that options can play roles ranging from helping investors manage risk to increasing income from stock they already hold.

## Expiration and exercise

FINRA explains several rules about expiry and exercise:

- **Equity options are American-style:** the holder can exercise at any time before expiry. Index options are generally European-style and cash-settled.
- **In-the-money options are generally exercised automatically at expiration.** For a call, that means the money to buy 100 shares per contract becomes due.
- **Expiry schedules vary:** traditional monthly options expire on the third Friday of each month; weekly options usually list with at least a week to run; and LEAPS are long-term options expiring up to two years and eight months out. Some traders follow zero-days-to-expiration (0DTE) strategies, opening positions on a contract's last day.

Before expiry, FINRA notes, a holder has three choices: exercise (for American-style contracts), sell the contract to close the position, or let it expire worthless. A seller can be assigned at any time on an equity option, which FINRA lists as assignment risk, and the risk rises the day before a stock's ex-dividend date, because holders of in-the-money calls may exercise early to collect the dividend.

## The risks

FINRA's summary: options offer leverage, the ability to magnify the value of the premium you pay, but leverage can come with the risk of significant losses.

- **Buyers** can lose 100% of the premium, quickly. Time works against them: FINRA's glossary describes time decay, or theta, as the way an option's theoretical value erodes with the passage of time.
- **Sellers** collect a premium but take on obligations. Writing a call without owning the shares exposes the seller to the same open-ended risk as [short selling](/blog/short-selling-explained/); writing a put can oblige the seller to buy shares far above their market price.
- **Margin.** The SEC's margin bulletin notes brokers generally require a margin account to trade options, do not let you buy options with margin, but may allow margin to sell them, and that some option-selling strategies can lose more than the initial investment. See [cash account versus margin account](/blog/cash-account-vs-margin-account/).
- **Volatility** drives option prices; [volatility explained](/blog/what-is-volatility/) covers the measure.
- **Binary options** are all-or-nothing contracts that FINRA calls extremely risky, made riskier by fraudulent schemes, many based outside the US; see [investment fraud red flags](/blog/investment-fraud-red-flags/).

## Approval comes first

FINRA explains that your broker must approve your account for a specific level of options trading, through an options agreement, because some strategies involve substantial risk. FINRA also points to the official disclosure document *Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options* for the full picture.
