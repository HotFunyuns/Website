Attention exercises make you better at attention exercises. That is the finding, and everything else in this article is an elaboration of it. If what you want is to sit with a long document without reaching for your phone, a rapid-detection drill is not the intervention with the best evidence behind it — and being told otherwise is the most common way this category oversells itself.

## "Attention span" is at least three different things

Before asking whether it can be trained, it is worth separating what people mean by it, because the label hides several distinct abilities that fail in different ways.

**Sustained attention** is staying with one undemanding task over an extended period. It is measured with vigilance tasks where targets are rare and the main enemy is monotony.

**Selective attention** is holding onto a target while competing information is present. It is measured with interference tasks, and it fails when the distractor wins.

**Divided attention** is tracking two things that both need updating. It is the most fragile of the three and the one most people overestimate in themselves.

A fourth thing gets folded in colloquially and does not belong: the ability to *start* and *stay with* a self-directed task over an hour. That is closer to motivation, task structure and environment than to any laboratory attention measure, and it responds to different interventions entirely.

Advice that treats all of these as one dial is advice about nothing specific. The rest of this article keeps them apart.

## What an attention exercise reliably changes

One thing: performance on the exercise.

This is not a dismissal. Improving on a trained task is a real effect with a real mechanism — you learn the timing, the layout, the type of target, where errors come from, and how to trade speed against accuracy on this particular task. It is the same near-transfer effect that shows up across the practice literature, and we set the evidence out in [do brain training apps work](/blog/do-brain-training-apps-work/).

What it is not is proof that a general capacity has expanded. The 2014 consensus statement issued jointly by the Stanford Center on Longevity and the Max Planck Institute for Human Development holds that the scientific literature does not support claims that software-based brain games improve general cognitive performance in everyday life or prevent cognitive decline. That statement was signed by dozens of researchers who study exactly this, and it remains the fairest one-sentence summary of where the field sits.

## What the well-controlled trials found

Two studies are worth knowing about in detail, because they are the kind of evidence that distinguishes a real effect from an expectation effect.

**Thompson and colleagues** trained participants on an adaptive working-memory task for 20 sessions — about 40 minutes a day, five days a week, for four weeks — against an active control group doing a different demanding task on the same schedule. Both groups improved substantially and durably on what they had trained. Neither showed transfer to untrained working-memory measures or to fluid intelligence. When the comparison group is also doing something difficult, the extra benefit disappears.

**Cortese and colleagues** conducted a meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials of cognitive training, published in the *Journal of the American Academy of Child & Adolescent Psychiatry*. Training improved performance on trained working-memory measures substantially. Effects on symptom ratings shrank when the raters were blinded — from a standardised mean difference of 0.37 on unblinded measures to 0.20 on blinded ones — and on academic outcomes in reading and arithmetic there were no significant effects. Their conclusion was that despite improving working-memory performance, cognitive training had limited effects on symptoms according to blinded assessments.

That study concerns a clinical population and a clinical question, and we cite it only because it is one of the best-controlled tests of whether attention-related training reaches behaviour outside the training. It is not a statement about any app, it is not a recommendation, and nothing in this article is medical advice or a claim about any condition.

## A score is partly a strategy

One more finding reframes what an attention number even means. Vallesi and colleagues meta-analysed sustained-attention tasks across twelve studies comparing younger and older adults. Older adults were substantially slower on go trials — but they also produced *fewer* false alarms on no-go trials, and showed more post-error slowing. The authors read this as a more prudent strategy in the use of attentional resources rather than as an inhibitory deficit.

That is a general lesson about attention scores. Speed and accuracy trade against each other, and two people with identical underlying ability can produce very different numbers by sitting at different points on that trade-off. If your score on a timed drill improves, check whether your accuracy moved in the same direction before concluding anything.

## What our app has, and what it does not

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and the plainest useful thing we can say here is a negative. **It contains no attention test and no attention-training mode.** There is no vigilance task, no reaction measure, and nothing that produces an attention score.

What it does contain are drills that *demand* attention while asking about something else: rounds with a very brief exposure, rapid-recognition rounds, rounds that require you to hold two attributes of an item at once, and arithmetic sprints under a 30-, 60- or 75-second clock. Those are attention-demanding by construction. Demanding attention and training attention are different claims, and only the first one is ours to make.

## What to change instead

If the real problem is the fourth thing — starting a task and staying with it — the interventions with the best support are structural rather than internal.

- **Shorten the session and define its end.** A session with a boundary is easier to begin than an open-ended one, and it is the reason short drills survive a bad week where long ones do not. Our guide to [building a daily practice habit](/blog/building-a-daily-practice-habit/) covers the mechanics.
- **Remove the interruption rather than resisting it.** This is the single biggest lever available, and it is covered properly in our article on [the conditions that make concentration possible](/blog/how-to-improve-concentration/).
- **Specify the next action.** "Revise chapter four" is a project. "Work the six problems on page 81" is a task you can actually start, and a specified task competes much better against a notification than a vague one does.
- **Use retrieval, not rereading.** Attention wanders fastest during passive review. Active recall keeps you occupied by construction, the techniques are in [memory techniques that actually work](/blog/memory-techniques-that-work/), and the reason passive review feels so convincing is set out in [judging your own recall](/blog/judging-your-own-recall/).

## The honest position

Practise an attention-demanding drill because it is absorbing, because it gives a short session a clear shape, or because you enjoy watching a number move. Those are good reasons, and they are the reasons we offer.

Do not practise one expecting it to repair your relationship with long-form reading, and treat any product that promises that as having gone past what the evidence allows. Practice, entertainment, educational activity and standardised assessment are four different things. Attention exercises are comfortably in the first three and nowhere near the fourth.
