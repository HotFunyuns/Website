Russian makes you say something about a journey before you have said where it goes: whether you are in the middle of it, heading one way, or whether you are describing going as a general habit. That choice is built into the verb stem, and it is why the language has два глагола for what English covers with one.

**Я иду в школу** — I am on my way to school, now, in that direction. **Я хожу в школу** — I go to school, as a routine. Same destination, different verb, different sentence.

## The contrast, stated properly

The traditional terms are **unidirectional** (also determinate) and **multidirectional** (indeterminate).

- **Unidirectional** describes motion in progress in one direction. One trip, one heading, under way.
- **Multidirectional** describes motion in general — habitual, repeated, an ability, a round trip — without reference to a single direction or a single occasion.

Every pair sits on the same axis. What changes between pairs is only *how* you are moving.

| Unidirectional | Multidirectional | Means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| идти́ | ходи́ть | go on foot |
| е́хать | е́здить | go by vehicle |
| лете́ть | лета́ть | fly |
| плыть | пла́вать | swim, sail |
| бежа́ть | бе́гать | run |
| нести́ | носи́ть | carry (in hands) |
| везти́ | вози́ть | transport (by vehicle) |
| вести́ | води́ть | lead, take someone |

The walking-versus-vehicle split at the top of that table is the one to internalise first. Saying you walked from Moscow to Saint Petersburg because you reached for *идти* is the kind of error that stops a conversation.

## Four situations that decide the choice

Rather than memorise the definitions, learn the four cases where the decision is forced.

**1. Right now, on the way.** Unidirectional. *Куда ты идёшь?* — where are you going? You are visibly in motion.

**2. Habit or repetition.** Multidirectional. *Я хожу в спортзал три раза в неделю* — I go to the gym three times a week.

**3. Ability, with no occasion attached.** Multidirectional. *Он плавает* — he swims, meaning he can swim. *Он плывёт* would mean he is swimming now, in a direction.

**4. A completed round trip in the past.** Multidirectional — and this is the one English speakers miss. *Вчера я ходил в кино* does not mean "yesterday I was walking toward the cinema". It means I went to the cinema and came back. The return leg is baked into the verb. To say you set out and stayed, you would use a prefixed form instead.

That fourth case is worth dwelling on, because it is a meaning Russian expresses in one word and English has to spell out. A speaker hears *ходил* and knows the person is back.

## Prefixes change the question entirely

Here is where learners either understand the system or decide it is infinite.

When you put a prefix on a motion verb, **the unidirectional and multidirectional contrast disappears.** Prefixed motion verbs lose the directional distinction and gain a spatial or temporal meaning instead — arriving, leaving, entering, crossing, passing.

What happens to the two stems is elegant. They do not both survive as directional options. Instead:

- the prefix on the **unidirectional** stem produces the **perfective**;
- the same prefix on the **multidirectional** stem produces the **imperfective**.

So the pair that used to mean "one way" versus "generally" now means "completed" versus "in progress or repeated".

| Prefix | Meaning | Perfective | Imperfective |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| при- | arrive | прийти́ | приходи́ть |
| у- | leave, go away | уйти́ | уходи́ть |
| в-, во- | enter | войти́ | входи́ть |
| вы- | exit | вы́йти | выходи́ть |
| под- | approach | подойти́ | подходи́ть |
| от- | move away from | отойти́ | отходи́ть |
| пере- | cross | перейти́ | переходи́ть |
| за- | drop in, call round | зайти́ | заходи́ть |
| до- | reach as far as | дойти́ | доходи́ть |

*Он пришёл* — he arrived, it is done. *Он приходил* — he came round (and went away again). *Он приходит каждый день* — he comes every day.

One prefix worth separating out: **по-** on a unidirectional stem gives a perfective meaning "set off". *Он пошёл* — he set out walking. *Мы поехали* — we set off by vehicle. It is one of the highest-frequency verb forms in spoken Russian and it does not behave like the spatial prefixes above.

## The reason it looks bigger than it is

Count the moving parts. Eight or so root pairs, a dozen or so productive prefixes. Multiply those and you get a chart that genuinely is intimidating.

But you are not learning the product. You are learning the two lists and one rule connecting them:

- **the roots** tell you how the motion happens (foot, vehicle, air, water, carrying);
- **the prefixes** tell you what the motion does relative to a place (into, out of, up to, across, away);
- **which stem the prefix lands on** tells you whether it is perfective or imperfective.

A learner who tries to memorise the grid will be doing it for months. A learner who memorises two short lists and the rule can generate the grid on demand. This is the same economy that runs through Russian generally — the [cases article](/blog/russian-cases-explained-for-beginners/) makes the equivalent point about six endings doing six jobs rather than six tables needing memorisation.

Because the prefixed forms *are* aspect pairs, everything you know about choosing between perfective and imperfective applies directly. If that choice is not solid yet, [choosing Russian verb aspect](/blog/choosing-russian-verb-aspect/) is the prerequisite for the second half of this system rather than an optional extra.

## How we sequence this, and why we split it

[Regal - Learn Russian Language](/apps/learn-russian/) puts motion verbs in two separate places rather than one, and the split is deliberate.

The unprefixed pairs — идти/ходить, ехать/ездить — are taught early, with the travel and transport vocabulary, because a learner needs them the moment they want to say where they are going. They are introduced as a choice between two sentences that mean different things, not as a grammatical category with a name.

The prefixed set is held back and taught later, next to aspect. Teaching прийти and приходить before a learner has a working grip on perfective and imperfective means teaching a distinction they have nowhere to file. Waiting costs a few weeks and saves the much larger cost of a half-understood system that has to be relearned.

Throughout, the prefixed forms are introduced inside example sentences and roleplays rather than as a chart. The course's approach to grammar generally is that cases, gender, agreement and verb forms are added in stages, with exercises that explain why the correct answer is correct rather than only marking it — and motion verbs are the place where that pays off most, because the answer is almost never derivable from a rule in isolation. You need the situation.

An honest limit: no app gets you to the point of producing the right prefixed motion verb at conversational speed. Recognition comes from a course; production comes from use. What a course can do is make sure that when you hear *зашёл* you know someone dropped in, rather than hearing an unfamiliar word.

## A decision procedure

1. **How are you moving?** Pick the root pair: foot, vehicle, air, water, carrying.
2. **Is there a prefix on it?** If yes, stop thinking about direction. You are choosing aspect, and the prefix supplies the spatial meaning.
3. **If there is no prefix, is this one trip in progress in one direction?** Unidirectional.
4. **Is it a habit, an ability, or a there-and-back that is finished?** Multidirectional.
5. **Are you setting off?** по- plus the unidirectional stem.
