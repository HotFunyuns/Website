**A power play is a stretch of time during which one team has more skaters on the ice, because the other is serving a penalty.** The team with the advantage is "on the power play"; the team without is "on the penalty kill" or "short-handed".

Together these are called special teams, and they decide more hockey games than any other single factor.

## How a penalty produces a power play

When a player commits an infraction, they go to the penalty box and their team plays without them for the penalty's duration. The most common outcome is five skaters against four.

The two main categories:

| Type | Typical duration | Ends early on a goal? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minor | 2 minutes | Yes |
| Double minor | 4 minutes (two consecutive minors) | The first half ends on a goal |
| Major | 5 minutes | **No** |

That "ends early" column is the most important line in this article. A minor penalty is a two-minute *maximum*, and a quick goal returns the game to even strength. A major is five minutes of short-handed play whatever happens — a team can concede three goals during one and still have time left to serve.

The specific durations and categories vary between competitions. The [USA Hockey officiating rulebook](https://www.usahockeyrulebook.com/) is a freely readable example of one such framework.

## Why one extra skater matters so much

Five defenders can cover the offensive zone. Four cannot.

With five, a defending team can pressure the puck carrier while still marking every dangerous area. With four, pressuring the puck means leaving someone open, and not pressuring means allowing time to set up. There is no arrangement of four players that solves the zone, which is why the scoring rate during power plays is far above the even-strength rate.

The short-handed team's structure is usually a compromise: protect the most dangerous areas, force play to the outside, and accept that shots from low-value positions will happen.

## The five-on-three

When two players are in the box simultaneously, three skaters face five. This is the most extreme situation in the sport, and the defending structure essentially becomes damage limitation — cover the slot, block lanes, and survive.

Teams that draw a five-on-three at a decisive moment frequently win the game on it.

## The penalty kill's weapons

A short-handed team is not without options.

**Clearing the zone.** Icing is normally waved off for a short-handed team, which means firing the puck the length of the ice is a legal way to reset. [What is icing in hockey](/blog/what-is-icing-in-hockey/) covers why that exception exists and how consequential it is.

**Pressure at the right moment.** An aggressive kill can force a turnover, and short-handed goals — while rare — are among the most damaging events in a game, because the team that had the advantage ends up conceding.

**The clock.** Time is the penalty killers' ally. Every second used is a second the advantage does not exist.

## Why rosters carry specialists

Because special teams matter and the situations are structurally different, teams deploy different players in each:

- **Power-play units** favour puck movement, vision and shooting — skills that convert time and space into chances.
- **Penalty-kill units** favour positioning, defensive awareness and shot-blocking, plus the skating to pressure and recover.

Some players are on both. Many are on neither. A roster spot that contributes on one special team is worth meaningfully more than one that contributes on neither, which is a real consideration when building a squad — [the hockey draft and GM guide](/blog/hockey-draft-gm-guide/) covers valuing those roles during selection, and [hockey line combinations explained](/blog/hockey-line-combinations-explained/) covers how deployment is arranged.

## The statistical distortion

Special teams also distort individual numbers. Plus/minus, hockey's most-quoted individual statistic, usually excludes power-play goals for while including short-handed goals against. A player who kills penalties therefore absorbs downside without the corresponding upside.

That is one of several reasons to treat plus/minus carefully, covered properly in [plus/minus and hockey statistics explained](/blog/plus-minus-and-hockey-stats/).
