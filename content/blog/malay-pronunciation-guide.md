Malay pronunciation has an unusual property for a language English speakers take on: the spelling mostly tells you the answer. There is no tone system to encode, no vowel length distinction to memorise, no silent letters inherited from a dead spelling convention. Write a Malay word down and you have very nearly written down how to say it.

Which means this guide is short on rules and long on the two things that actually go wrong: one ambiguous vowel letter, and the English rhythm you bring with you without noticing.

## The alphabet is the one you already have

Standard Malay is written in **Rumi**, the 26-letter Latin alphabet, which is why a beginner can read a Malay sentence aloud on day one — badly, but recognisably. That is a genuine advantage and worth appreciating before we get to the corrections.

You will occasionally meet **Jawi**, the Arabic-derived script with additional letters for Malay sounds. It retains an official role in Brunei, appears in religious and cultural contexts, and turns up on signage in parts of Malaysia. You do not need it to begin, and knowing it exists saves the small shock of encountering it.

A few letters — **v**, **z**, and to a lesser extent **f** — appear almost exclusively in borrowed words. If you see one, you are usually looking at a loan from English, Arabic or elsewhere.

## Vowels: six sounds, and one letter doing two jobs

Standard Malay works with six vowel sounds: the values you would expect for **a**, **i**, **u**, **o**, plus **two** different vowels both written with the letter **e**.

That last point is the single largest pronunciation problem in the language, so it deserves the space.

### The e problem

One **e** is a **schwa** — the neutral, colourless vowel English uses in unstressed syllables. The other is a **clearer front vowel**, roughly the vowel of English *bed*. Ordinary Malay spelling writes both as a plain *e* and gives you no way to tell them apart on the page.

Words where **e** is a schwa:

- **besar** — big
- **sedap** — delicious
- **empat** — four
- **enam** — six

Words where **e** is the clearer vowel:

- **meja** — table
- **teh** — tea

Dictionaries and teaching materials sometimes distinguish the two with a diacritic, but standard writing does not, and you should not expect to see one in the wild. This is the one part of Malay pronunciation where **you genuinely have to hear the word**, and it is the reason a course with audio beats a phrasebook by more than the usual margin.

A useful pattern to notice, though not a law: schwa is very common in first syllables of two-syllable words, which is why *besar*, *sedap*, *beras*, *belajar* and *kereta* all begin the same way. Noticing that will get you most of them.

### Two vowels in a row are two syllables

**air** ("water") is not the English word *air*. It is two syllables — *a-ir*. Likewise **maaf** ("sorry", "excuse me") is *ma-af*, with the two vowels separated rather than run together.

This catches English speakers because English collapses adjacent vowels into diphthongs by reflex. Malay generally does not, outside its three genuine diphthongs.

### The three diphthongs

**ai**, **au** and **oi** are real diphthongs and behave as single vowel sounds. Everything else that looks like a vowel cluster is two syllables.

## Consonants: five rules and you are done

Most Malay consonants are pronounced as an English speaker would guess. These five are not.

**c is "ch".** Always. *cinta*, *cakap*, *cuba* all begin with the sound in English *church* — never a k, never an s. English speakers get this wrong more than any other consonant because English *c* is genuinely unpredictable and the habit is deep.

**j is "j" and y is "y".** *jalan*, *jam*, *ya*. No surprises, but worth stating because learners coming from Spanish, German or French bring different expectations for both letters.

**ng and ny are single sounds.** **ng** is the sound at the end of English *sing*, and crucially Malay allows it at the **start** of a word, which English does not. **ny** is a single palatal sound, roughly the *ny* of *canyon* — as in *nyanyi*, "to sing".

**ng versus ngg.** This is the distinction people miss. **bunga** ("flower") has the plain *ng* sound with no hard g after it. **tangga** ("stairs, ladder") has the *ng* sound **plus** a g. The extra letter is not decoration; it changes the word.

**A final k is a glottal stop.** In native Malay words, a word-final *k* is normally not a released k at all but a glottal stop — the catch in the middle of English *uh-oh*. *tidak*, *budak*, *masak*, *duduk* all end this way. Pronouncing a hard k there is one of the most audible beginner markers, and one of the easiest things to fix in an afternoon.

**A final h is pronounced.** Unlike English, where a final *h* is usually silent or decorative, Malay gives it real breath: *rumah*, *sudah*, *tujuh*.

### The loanword digraphs

Three more digraphs appear mainly in words borrowed from Arabic:

- **sy** — as in *syarikat* ("company"), a *sh* sound
- **kh** — as in *khabar* ("news"), a back-of-the-throat friction rather than a plain k
- **gh** — as in *maghrib*, the voiced counterpart of *kh*

Many speakers simplify *kh* toward a plain k and *gh* toward a plain g in casual speech. Knowing what the spelling is asking for lets you decide rather than guess.

### The letter r

Realisations vary regionally, from a tap to a trill, and it is generally pronounced more distinctly than the *r* of southern British English. This is one of the features that most marks a speaker's origin, and one where matching whoever you are learning from is more sensible than seeking a single "correct" version.

## Rhythm is where the actual work is

Get everything above right and you can still sound unmistakably foreign, because English speakers import a rhythm that Malay does not use.

English is aggressively unequal. Stressed syllables are long and clear; unstressed ones are compressed into schwas. Say *photograph*, *photographer* and *photographic* and you will hear the same vowels move around and change quality entirely.

Malay does not do this. **Stress does not distinguish words**, and syllables get much more even weight. The result is a flatter, steadier rhythm that English speakers instinctively "correct" by adding emphasis somewhere — which is why a learner can pronounce every individual sound correctly and still be hard to follow.

The fix is not a rule you can apply. It is imitation: listen to a phrase, say it back, and specifically resist the urge to lean on one syllable. Recording yourself and comparing is unusually effective here, because rhythm is something you can hear in a recording that you cannot hear while producing it.

This is also why **listening to real dialogue matters more than drilling isolated words**. Rhythm only exists across a phrase.

## The word-final a, and why you will hear two versions

In much of Peninsular Malaysia and in Singapore, a word-final **a** is reduced toward a schwa — so *apa*, *saya*, *nama* and *bila* all end in an indistinct vowel rather than a clear *ah*. Other conventions, including the one used across the strait in Indonesian, keep a fuller *a* in that position.

Neither is wrong. Both are established, and they are among the most reliable cues a listener uses to place a speaker. It is one of several differences worth understanding if you are choosing between the two national standards, and our [beginner's guide to Malay](/blog/learn-malay-beginners-guide/) sets out the rest.

Practical advice: match the convention used where you intend to speak, stay consistent, and recognise the other one when you hear it.

## Ten words to get exactly right

Small list, chosen because each one demonstrates something from above.

| Word | Meaning | What it demonstrates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| terima kasih | thank you | even syllable weight |
| selamat pagi | good morning | no stress reduction across four syllables |
| apa khabar | how are you | the kh digraph, and final-a reduction |
| saya | I, me | final a |
| besar | big | e as schwa |
| meja | table | e as the clearer vowel |
| tidak | no, not | final k as a glottal stop |
| air | water | two vowels, two syllables |
| bunga | flower | ng without a following g |
| tangga | stairs | ng plus g |

Ten words that between them cover the whole rule set. If you can say these accurately and evenly, the rest of Malay pronunciation is mostly application.

## How to practise, in order

**Fix the consonant rules first.** They are finite, they are rules rather than habits, and they take a single session. *c* as ch, final *k* as a glottal stop, *ng* versus *ngg*.

**Then work on rhythm, with whole phrases.** Not word lists. Rhythm does not exist in a word list.

**Then collect the e words as you meet them.** Do not try to learn the schwa/clear distinction as a rule. Learn it word by word, from audio, the way you would learn a gender or a tone. It accumulates faster than it sounds.

**Record and compare with a delay.** Listening back immediately, you hear what you meant to say. Listening back the next day, you hear what you actually said.

**Use dictation.** Writing down what you hear forces you to commit to a spelling, and committing to a spelling means committing to which vowel you heard — which is exactly the discrimination you are trying to build.

## Why this is a good place to start a language

Malay pronunciation is one of the few parts of learning a Southeast Asian language where the effort is small and the payoff is immediate. There is no script to decode, no tone to derive, and no long list of exceptions. A week of deliberate work gets most learners to a point where they are understood without repetition.

That matters more than it sounds, because the languages people abandon are the ones where nothing works for a month.
