Almost everything you have read about a pre-modern historical figure rests on a thinner evidential base than the confident prose suggests. For most famous individuals before the eighteenth century, what survives is a handful of documents they never wrote, a smaller handful they may have authorised, and narrative accounts composed by people who were not there — sometimes centuries later, usually with an argument to make. Reading biography well means keeping that structure visible instead of letting a fluent paragraph hide it.

## The evidence ladder

Claims about a person's life descend a ladder of directness. Each rung is useful; the rungs are not interchangeable.

**Documents the person produced or authorised.** Letters in their own hand, decrees issued under their name, inscriptions they commissioned, coins struck by their mint. This is the strongest rung, with one permanent caveat: it tells you what they wanted recorded, which is not the same as what happened.

**Administrative records generated about them.** Tax assessments, court rolls, census entries, muster lists, cargo manifests. These are often the most valuable material precisely because nobody was trying to impress posterity. The National Archives in the United Kingdom publishes several hundred research guides to exactly this kind of record — census returns, wills, court proceedings, military service files — and they are where a biography stops being anecdote and starts being checkable.

**Contemporary accounts by others.** Letters, chronicles and reports written during the subject's lifetime by people who may or may not have had access. Proximity in time is not the same as proximity to events.

**Later narrative accounts.** Histories and biographies written after the fact, frequently drawing on sources that no longer exist. This rung supplies most of what the public knows about antiquity.

**Material and archaeological evidence.** Buildings, burials, battlefield debris, isotopic analysis of remains. Slow to produce, hard to argue with, and usually silent on motive.

**Tradition and legend.** Stories transmitted orally or in devotional literature. These are genuine historical evidence about what people believed and when they came to believe it; they are weak evidence about the events themselves.

Our explainer on [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) sets out the general distinction; the ladder above is the version that applies specifically to claims about individuals.

## How long after the fact was it written?

This is the single most useful question, and it is the one popular accounts most often leave out.

Take Alexander of Macedon, who died in 323 BC. The main surviving narrative accounts of his campaigns were written by Diodorus in the first century BC, by Curtius Rufus in the first century AD, by Plutarch around AD 100, and by Arrian in the second century AD — a gap of roughly three to five hundred years. All of them drew on earlier accounts by people who had actually been present, including Ptolemy and Aristobulus. Every one of those contemporary accounts is lost. What we have is a reconstruction of a reconstruction, and the modern scholarly argument is largely about which later writer preserved which lost earlier writer most faithfully.

That is not an exotic case. It is the normal condition of ancient biography, and it applies with variations to figures across Eurasia and Africa whose records come through dynastic histories compiled by successor regimes. When a source's date is missing from an account, it is usually missing because including it would weaken the sentence.

## Who wrote it, and what did they want?

Once you know when, ask who and why. The US National Archives teaches document analysis as a four-step sequence — meet the document, observe its parts, try to make sense of it, then use it as historical evidence — and publishes a separate tool specifically for identifying perspective, on the principle that a writer's background, beliefs and experiences shape their point of view.

For biography the practical questions are: who commissioned or paid for this; who was the intended audience; what was the writer defending or attacking; what would they have had no way of knowing; and what did they stand to gain. A court history written under a founder's grandson has an obvious interest in the founder's legitimacy. A conqueror's own campaign memoir has an obvious interest in the necessity of the campaign. Neither is thereby useless.

## Hostile sources, partisan sources, and triangulation

The instinct to prefer the friendlier account is wrong in both directions. A panegyric and a hatchet job are both shaped, and the shaping is itself informative.

The workable technique is triangulation plus attention to concessions. Where two writers with opposed interests agree on a detail neither benefits from, that detail is relatively secure. Where a hostile writer concedes a competence, or a friendly one admits a defeat, the concession is worth more than the surrounding argument, because it runs against the grain of the text's purpose.

The limits deserve stating. Concessions can be rhetorical devices — admitting a small fault to make a larger defence credible is an ancient technique, not a modern one. And two sources agreeing proves nothing if one copied the other, which is why establishing the relationship between texts comes before comparing them.

The American Historical Association's standards statement puts the underlying obligation plainly: historians should "not misrepresent their sources," should "report their findings as accurately as possible and not omit evidence that runs counter to their own interpretation," and must leave "a clear trail for subsequent historians to follow." Trustworthiness, it says, is the historian's single most precious professional asset. A reader can apply the same test to anything they read: does this account show its trail?

## Why famous quotations are so often wrong

Quotations are the weakest link in almost every popular biography, because a good line travels without its paperwork.

A concrete case. Plutarch's *Life of Caesar*, available in translation through the Perseus Digital Library at Tufts University, records at chapter 50 that after the rapid defeat of Pharnaces, Caesar wrote to a friend at Rome named Amantius a message of three words — rendered in Greek as ἦλθον, εἶδον, ἐνίκησα, and familiar in Latin as *veni, vidi, vici*. Plutarch notes the rhetorical neatness of the three similar endings.

Now set that beside the dating. Caesar defeated Pharnaces in 47 BC and was killed in 44 BC; Plutarch was writing around AD 100. The line is not in Caesar's own surviving commentaries. It reaches us through a biographer working roughly a century and a half later, in a different language, in a genre that valued a well-turned anecdote. It may well be authentic. But the evidential status of "Caesar said this" and "Plutarch reports that Caesar wrote this" is not the same, and the difference is exactly what gets lost when the line is printed on a poster.

The same mechanism produces outright misattribution. Lines drift towards more famous mouths, get sharpened with each retelling, and first appear in print long after the supposed occasion. Where a quotation's earliest known source is not the person credited with it, the honest thing is to say so rather than repeat it — and a biography that offers no source for a quotation has given you no way to check.

## What a biography page in an app is, and is not

Anything that presents a life as a clean list — role, achievements, legacy — is showing you the output of the process described above with the evidence removed. That format is genuinely useful for orientation and genuinely misleading if mistaken for a finding.

We can be specific about our own product. [World History Timeline Sim](/apps/world-history-timeline-sim/) is our app, built by Reign Creative, and its people bank holds several hundred person records plus a leadership mode in which each campaign carries a short biography, a description of the historical situation and an explicit accuracy note. None of those records carries a citation, and none has been externally reviewed. An internal audit also found roughly ninety-five duplicated person entries among them, some giving differing details for the same individual. That is exactly the situation this article describes: a topic map, useful for finding out who existed and roughly when, and not evidence for anything.

Treat it that way and it does real work — it will tell you that a figure is worth looking up, and give you a rough chronological neighbourhood. Then go to an archive catalogue, a museum, a university press book or a translated primary text. Nothing in an app replaces teachers, scholarly books, archives or primary sources.
