An initial public offering, or IPO, is the moment a private company becomes a public one: it sells shares to the general public for the first time, and those shares then trade on a stock exchange. IPOs attract headlines, and sometimes big first-day price moves. The SEC's investor bulletin on investing in an IPO explains what actually happens and, importantly, why buying a newly public stock carries particular risks.

## How a company goes public

1. **Registration.** Under US securities law, a company may not offer or sell shares unless the transaction is registered with the SEC or an exemption applies. For an IPO, the company files a registration statement, typically on **Form S-1**. Its core is the **prospectus**, the document describing the company and the terms of the offering.
2. **SEC staff review.** The staff reviews the filing for compliance with disclosure requirements, often prompting revisions. The bulletin is explicit: the SEC declaring a registration statement effective **does not represent an approval of the merits** of the offering.
3. **Pricing.** The **underwriters**, investment banks that manage and sell the IPO, gather indications of interest from prospective investors and recommend a price; the company ultimately sets it.
4. **Listing and trading.** The company usually lists on an exchange such as the New York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq, and its shares begin trading. From then on it must file regular public reports, such as the quarterly and annual filings described in [how to read an earnings report](/blog/how-to-read-an-earnings-report/).

The money raised in the IPO goes to the company and any selling shareholders. After that, trading happens between investors, as explained in [how the stock market works](/blog/how-the-stock-market-works/).

## Who gets shares at the offering price

The SEC describes two ways the public can invest:

- **In the IPO itself,** at the offering price, if you are a client of an underwriter or dealer involved. The bulletin notes that underwriters and dealers often distribute most IPO shares to institutional and high-net-worth clients.
- **In the market afterwards,** buying shares when they are resold in the days after the IPO. This is the more common route for individuals, and the price can be very different from the offering price.

## Reading the prospectus

A new public company has no reporting history, so the SEC notes that information to inform a decision often can only be found in the prospectus. The final version usually appears on EDGAR as a 424B3 or 424B4 filing. Sections the bulletin highlights:

| Section | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Prospectus summary | The business, strategy, use of the money raised and the offering terms |
| Risk factors | What management thinks could significantly hurt the business or the investment |
| Dilution | The usually large gap between the IPO price and both the book value per share and what insiders paid |
| MD&A | Management's account of results and financial condition |
| Business | Products, markets, key suppliers and customers, competition |
| Financial statements | Audited figures, preceded by the auditor's opinion |

The bulletin also notes that many requirements are phased in over time for **emerging growth companies**, generally those with less than $1.235 billion in revenue, for up to five years after going public.

## Why newly public stocks can swing

The SEC explains a structural reason. On the first day, generally only the shares sold in the IPO can trade; shares held by founders, early investors and employees are often restricted or covered by **lock-up agreements**. Limited supply, especially for a highly sought-after IPO, can drive the price steeply up. Later, as restricted shares become available, the price may decline, and when lock-ups expire it may fall significantly if many shares come to market at once.

Add a short trading history and little public information, and IPO shares tend to be [volatile](/blog/what-is-volatility/). The SEC's bulletin calls IPOs potentially risky and speculative investments.

## Beware "pre-IPO" offers

The SEC has issued a separate alert about scams offering the chance to buy "pre-IPO" shares, often promoted on social media, by phone or email, with promises of high returns from getting in at the ground floor. Even legitimate pre-IPO investing carries the risk of losing everything: the company may never succeed, never go public, and its shares may be impossible to resell. [Investment fraud red flags](/blog/investment-fraud-red-flags/) lists the warning signs.
