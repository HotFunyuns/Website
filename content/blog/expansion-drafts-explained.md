**An expansion draft stocks a brand-new team by letting it select players that existing teams chose not to protect.** It is the only draft in professional sport where the talent comes from other rosters rather than from outside the league.

## The mechanism

The general shape is consistent across leagues that use it:

1. Each existing team submits a **protection list** — a set number of players who cannot be selected.
2. The expansion team selects a fixed number from each existing team, chosen from those left unprotected.
3. Rules typically require each existing team to expose a minimum quality or quantity of players, so nobody can protect everyone useful.

That is it. Everything interesting is in step one.

## The protection list is the whole design

The size of the protection list determines how good the new team can be, and leagues have set it very differently.

| Protection size | Effect on the new team | Effect on incumbents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Large (most of a roster) | Weak; scraps and prospects | Painless |
| Moderate | Mixed; some real contributors | Uncomfortable choices |
| Small | Genuinely competitive | Painful losses |

A league choosing this number is choosing how quickly it wants the new team to be watchable, against how much it wants to annoy its existing owners. Those pull in opposite directions, and where the number lands is a political decision as much as a competitive one.

## Why a team would expose a good player

This is the part that looks irrational and is not.

**Salary.** Under a cap, a large contract can be a liability rather than an asset. If a team is squeezed, having the new club take an expensive player is a benefit, not a loss. [Salary cap basics for GM games](/blog/salary-cap-basics-for-gm-games/) covers why cap space is itself an asset.

**Age and contract length.** A good player on a long deal that will age badly is a different proposition from the same player on a short one.

**Positional surplus.** A team with three excellent players at one position and none at another may protect the position it is thin at.

**Side deals.** Leagues often permit incumbents to trade with the expansion team — offering picks or prospects in exchange for a promise not to select a particular player. This turns the whole event into a negotiation.

## Strategy for the expansion team

The instinct is to take the best available player from each team. That is usually wrong.

**Breadth beats stars.** A new roster has holes at every position. One outstanding player and eleven poor ones is a worse team than twelve decent ones, and it is far worse than the star's quality suggests, because a roster is a system rather than a sum. The same reasoning drives ordinary roster construction — [how to build a balanced basketball roster](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-basketball-roster/) works through the fixed-minutes version of it.

**Assets can be worth more than players.** Incumbent teams will pay to protect specific players. Converting a selection you do not need into future picks builds the roster faster than the selection itself would.

**Contracts matter as much as ability.** An expansion team's greatest asset is usually flexibility. Absorbing a bad contract has a price, and that price should be paid in acquired assets rather than out of goodwill.

## Where this sits among draft types

An expansion draft is one of several answers to "how do players get onto a roster":

- **Entry drafts** distribute incoming talent, ordered by reverse standings or a lottery. [How draft order works](/blog/how-football-draft-order-works/) and [how draft lotteries work](/blog/how-draft-lotteries-work/) cover those.
- **Expansion drafts** redistribute existing talent, once.
- **Free agency** lets players choose, covered in [free agency explained](/blog/free-agency-explained/).

Each has a different design goal, and each produces a different kind of roster.
