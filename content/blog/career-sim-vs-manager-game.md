**A career sim asks whether one player can succeed. A manager game asks whether one squad can.** Every other difference between the two formats — session length, replay value, what the interface shows you, what kind of person enjoys it — follows from that.

We make both, so treat this as an interested comparison of our own two formats rather than a neutral survey of the genre. What follows is an honest account of what each is bad at as well as what it is good at.

## The core difference

| | Career sim | Manager game |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The question | Can this player succeed? | Can this squad succeed? |
| Your decisions | Situational: what to do now | Structural: who to have, and how to use them |
| Feedback speed | Immediate | Delayed by weeks of simulated time |
| Natural unit of play | One decision | One match, or one transfer window |
| What it produces | A story | A system you built |
| Failure feels like | Bad luck or a bad call | A design flaw in your plan |

That last row is the most telling. When a career sim goes badly, it feels like something happened to you. When a manager game goes badly, it feels like you made a mistake several months earlier. Different people find each of those more or less tolerable.

## What career sims are good at

**Narrative.** Following one player through school, a draft, a breakthrough and a decline produces a shape that a spreadsheet does not. The story is emergent rather than written, which is why two players' accounts of the same game are genuinely different.

**Teaching a sport.** A career sim introduces the vocabulary one perspective at a time. You learn what a closer is by being one, rather than by reading a roster screen with a closer on it.

**Short sessions.** A decision is a complete unit. You can open the game, resolve one plate appearance, and close it without leaving anything unfinished.

**Variety across replays.** Playing a different position is not a variation, it is a different game. [Hockey Career Sim 2026](/apps/hockey-career-sim/) as a goaltender bears very little resemblance to the same game as a winger.

## What career sims are bad at

**Control.** You are one player on a team you do not pick. A great season on a poor team is a real and frustrating outcome, and no amount of good decisions fixes it.

**Long-run agency.** Your influence over a season is bounded. If you want the outcome to be attributable to your choices, this is the wrong format.

**Statistical legibility.** Your numbers are shaped by your role and your teammates, which makes self-assessment harder than it looks.

## What manager games are good at

**Attributable outcomes.** The roster is yours. If it wins, that is your work. If it fails, the failure is diagnosable, and diagnosing it is much of the fun.

**Depth per save.** A squad can be rebuilt, retooled, aged out and replaced across many seasons without the game running out of decisions.

**Systems thinking.** Salary constraints, depth, positional scarcity and development timelines interact, and getting the interaction right is a genuine intellectual problem. [Salary cap basics for GM games](/blog/salary-cap-basics-for-gm-games/) covers the constraint that produces most of it.

## What manager games are bad at

**Immediacy.** Most decisions pay off in simulated weeks. The gap between action and feedback is the format's biggest cost.

**Session length.** The natural unit is large. Stopping mid-window or mid-match leaves state you have to reload mentally.

**Onboarding.** A roster screen assumes you already know what the positions are worth. That is a real barrier for someone new to the sport.

## Which to start with

If you are new to the sport, start with a career sim. If you already know the sport well and want the decisions to be yours, start with a manager game.
