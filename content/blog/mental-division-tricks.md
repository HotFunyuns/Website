Multiplication shortcuts are a party trick. Division is the one you actually use — splitting a bill, converting a unit, checking a price per kilo, working out whether a number somebody just told you is plausible — and it is the one most people abandon to their phone.

The reason is that school division is an algorithm for paper. Mental division is a different activity: you do not process digits left to right, you **make the divisor friendly and then handle what is left over.** Four methods cover almost everything.

## First, fix the magnitude

Before any method, spend two seconds on the size of the answer.

3,472 ÷ 8. Eight goes into 3,200 four hundred times, so the answer is in the four hundreds. You now cannot produce 43 or 4,340, and those are the errors that actually matter. A wrong digit is a small mistake; a wrong power of ten is a disaster, and it is the overwhelmingly common failure in mental arithmetic.

This habit costs nothing and catches nearly everything. It is also the one thing on this page that stays useful when you do reach for a calculator, because the calculator will happily return the answer to the question you mistyped. [Estimation techniques](/blog/estimation-techniques-explained/) goes further into building the magnitude reflex.

## Method 1: factor splitting

Any divisor that factorises can be applied in stages, and stages are far easier than one big step.

- **÷ 24** → ÷ 8, then ÷ 3. (Or ÷ 4 then ÷ 6, or ÷ 3 then ÷ 8.)
- **÷ 12** → ÷ 4, then ÷ 3.
- **÷ 18** → ÷ 2, then ÷ 9.
- **÷ 56** → ÷ 8, then ÷ 7.
- **÷ 35** → ÷ 5, then ÷ 7.

*1,728 ÷ 24.* Halve three times: 864, 432, 216. Then divide by 3: **72.**

The one piece of technique here is **choosing the order.** Take the factor that keeps you in whole numbers first. 1,728 ÷ 3 = 576 works fine, but if the first step had produced a decimal you would be carrying it through every subsequent step. Scan the number, decide which factor divides it cleanly, start there.

Factor splitting also explains why some divisors feel easy and others do not: 8 is three halvings, 16 is four. Divisors built from 2s and 5s are pleasant because our number system is built from 2s and 5s. The shortcuts for divisors that are factors of powers of ten — 5, 2.5, 25, 125 — are worked through in [mental math tricks that work](/blog/mental-math-tricks-that-work/); this article is about what to do when the divisor is not one of those.

## Method 2: partial quotients

This is the one that survives numbers chosen by reality rather than by a textbook, and it is badly under-taught.

Instead of processing digit by digit, **subtract chunks you recognise and keep a running total.**

*4,732 ÷ 7.*

- 600 × 7 = 4,200. Remaining: 532. Running total: 600.
- 70 × 7 = 490. Remaining: 42. Running total: 670.
- 6 × 7 = 42. Remaining: 0. Running total: **676.**

Nothing needed to be held in a particular position. Nothing had to be borrowed. If you had used 500 × 7 instead of 600 × 7, you would have taken an extra step and arrived at the same answer, which is the property that makes this robust: **there are no wrong chunks, only slower ones.**

Compare that with long division, where one misplaced digit propagates silently to the end. Partial quotients degrades gracefully. Under time pressure or distraction, that matters more than raw speed.

## Method 3: round the divisor and correct

For an awkward divisor, divide by the nearby round number and adjust by a percentage.

| Dividing by | Do this | Then |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 9 | ÷ 10 | add about 11% |
| 11 | ÷ 10 | subtract about 9% |
| 19 | ÷ 20 | add about 5% |
| 21 | ÷ 20 | subtract about 5% |
| 49 | ÷ 50 | add about 2% |
| 99 | ÷ 100 | add about 1% |

*3,600 ÷ 9.* Divide by 10: 360. Add 11%: about 400. Exactly 400.

The corrections are not arbitrary. Dividing by 9 rather than 10 means the answer must be larger by a factor of 10/9, which is about 1.111 — hence 11%. Dividing by 19 rather than 20 gives a factor of 20/19, about 1.053. Once you see where the numbers come from you can generate them for any divisor instead of memorising a table, which is the difference between a method and a trick.

This one is approximate unless you carry the correction precisely, so it belongs to estimation rather than exact arithmetic. That is usually fine — most real division questions want a number good to a couple of per cent. Percentage adjustments themselves, if that step is the slow one, are covered in [percentages in your head](/blog/percentages-in-your-head/).

## Method 4: divisibility rules, used correctly

Divisibility rules tell you **whether** a division will come out clean. They do not tell you the answer, and treating them as a division method is the commonest way to waste time with them.

The ones worth knowing:

- **2** — last digit even.
- **3** — digit sum divisible by 3.
- **4** — last two digits form a number divisible by 4.
- **5** — last digit 0 or 5.
- **6** — even **and** passes the test for 3.
- **8** — last three digits divisible by 8.
- **9** — digit sum divisible by 9.
- **11** — form the alternating sum of the digits. For 918,082: 9 − 1 + 8 − 0 + 8 − 2 = 22, which is divisible by 11, so the number is.
- **12** — passes the tests for 3 **and** 4.

Notice the composite pattern: 6 is 2 and 3, 12 is 3 and 4. Any divisor that factorises into coprime parts can be tested by testing the parts, which means you never need a separate rule for 15, 18, 24 or 36.

Seven is the notorious gap in this list. The workable test — split off the last digit, double it, subtract from the rest, repeat — is covered with worked examples in [mental math tricks that work](/blog/mental-math-tricks-that-work/), and the important caveat is the same one that applies to every rule here: **it answers yes or no and never produces a quotient.** Use these to decide whether a fraction will simplify, not to divide.

## Remainders, fractions and decimals

Decide what you want before you start, because the three want different work.

**A remainder** is what partial quotients leaves you naturally: keep subtracting until you cannot.

**A fraction** is the remainder over the divisor, simplified. 4,735 ÷ 7 is 676 remainder 3, so 676 and 3/7.

**A decimal** means treating the remainder as the beginning of the next stage: bring in a zero and carry on. Before you do, check whether it will terminate — **a fraction terminates as a decimal only when the simplified denominator has no prime factors other than 2 and 5.** Sevenths, ninths, elevenths and thirds never terminate, and knowing that in advance stops you chasing digits that will not end.

And for **dividing by a decimal**, shift both numbers by the same power of ten until the divisor is whole. 84 ÷ 0.25 becomes 8,400 ÷ 25, which is a multiply-by-4-and-shift problem: 33,600 ÷ 100 = **336.**

One thing we removed is worth describing, because it is a good illustration of that rule.

There is a genuine divisibility test for 7 that works by multiplying the digits, from right to left, by the repeating pattern 1, 3, 2, −1, −3, −2 and summing the result. It is a real, correct, rather elegant piece of number theory, and it demonstrated beautifully.

It also lost, consistently, to simply dividing by 7. By the time a user had recalled the weight pattern, aligned it with the digits and kept a running signed sum, partial quotients had produced the actual quotient rather than a yes-or-no answer. It looked like a shortcut and behaved like a detour, and it survived in the app only because it was impressive.

## The procedure

1. **Estimate the magnitude.** Always. Two seconds.
2. **Can the divisor be factored?** Split it, easiest factor first.
3. **Is it awkward but near a round number?** Round and correct by percentage.
4. **Is it none of those?** Partial quotients. Subtract chunks, keep a running total.
5. **Decide what you want the leftover to be** — remainder, fraction or decimal — before you produce it.
