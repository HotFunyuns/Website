**A forecheck is how a team pressures its opponent in the opponent's defensive zone to win the puck back or stop a clean breakout; forecheck systems differ in how many forwards go in to pressure and how many stay back.** The 1-2-2 sends one forechecker and keeps four players back. Two-man systems send two forwards in hard. The neutral-zone trap sends one forechecker and has the other four wait in the middle of the ice. Dump-and-chase is the offensive half of the idea: put the puck deep, then go and get it.

USA Hockey's coaching manual sums up the objective of forechecking as preventing "the opposition from clearing their zone" and gaining "possession of the puck in their defensive zone." It is also clear that "no one system is the best". Which is right depends on the players a team has.

## The principle under every system: take an angle

Before the patterns, one rule that every system shares. USA Hockey's tactical guide tells coaches that the forechecker should always "get an angle" on the puck carrier, so that at the very least he confines the carrier to one side of the ice — "never forecheck straight at the puck carrier."

The reason is geometry. A forechecker who skates straight at the puck can be beaten to either side. One who approaches on an angle takes half the ice away, and the systems below are mostly about what the other four players do with the half that is left. The same guide also wants forecheckers to force the puck carrier down the side of the rink rather than letting him come up the middle and choose which side to attack.

## The 1-2-2: one in, four back

In USA Hockey's description, one forward — the centre, in its diagram — forechecks the puck carrier. The two wingers stay wide so they can check the opponent's wingers along the boards, and the two defencemen play inside the wingers, with the puck-side defenceman holding the blue line as long as possible.

The manual calls it conservative and easy to teach, and lists what it does when it works:

- prevents three-on-two breakouts;
- controls the boards, taking the opponent's wingers away as a breakout option;
- encourages the opposing defenceman to carry the puck out himself;
- leaves one forward, usually the off-side wing, in position to help defensively.

What it concedes is stated just as plainly: a skilled forechecker will win the puck occasionally, but the opponent "should be able to beat one forechecker" by moving the puck to its three open players. The 1-2-2 accepts some clean exits in exchange for keeping enough players back to stop odd-man rushes.

## Two-man pressure

USA Hockey's two-man forecheck (its manual labels the alignment 1-1-3) sends two forwards — in its example, the centre and the puck-side wing — after the puck carrier, while the off-side wing stays high in the offensive zone, ready either to drop back as an extra defender or to move to the slot. The puck-side defenceman may pinch down the boards, and the other defenceman takes the middle.

The manual says it works best when the puck carrier is deep — below an imaginary line across the tops of the faceoff circles — and describes it as combining aggression with the caution of the 1-2-2: one forward plays back "to prevent a three-on-two breakout." When the play switches sides, the wingers swap jobs while the centre's role stays constant. Its payoff is a quick counter-attack, because the high forward is already near the slot when the puck is won.

Its cost is the mirror image of the 1-2-2's: more pressure on the puck, but more ice behind the forecheckers if both of them are beaten.

## The neutral-zone trap

The trap moves the battle out of the opponent's zone and into the middle of the ice. The Los Angeles Times laid out its mechanics in June 1995, during a Stanley Cup Final that New Jersey had reached while using it:

1. While the opponent has the puck in its own end, only one forward forechecks; the other two forwards and both defencemen wait in the neutral zone.
2. The forechecker, usually the centre, angles the puck carrier towards the boards.
3. The other four take up a box, or "four corners", formation and close the space around him.

The carrier is then left with three poor options. A pass risks interception. Shooting the puck into the zone before he crosses the red line risks icing. Carrying it means skating into players converging on him. The point, in the newspaper's words, was to stop rushes "before they gather speed."

It was controversial. ESPN analyst Mike Milbury argued it should be banned as a negative tactic that kept scores down, while the Devils' players pointed to the scoring chances they still created. The same report noted that the system was not new: Viacheslav Fetisov recalled teams in Sweden, Finland and Czechoslovakia playing it long before, and the paper reported that Scotty Bowman had seen Swedish teams use it and adapted it while coaching Montreal in the 1970s.

The rule that made the trap bite is still in the book. Under the NHL's 2026-27 Rule 81, a team at equal or greater strength that shoots the puck from its own half beyond the opponent's goal line is called for icing — so a trapped team still has to gain the centre red line before it can safely dump the puck in.

## Dump-and-chase

Dump-and-chase is where a team's offence turns into its forecheck. Instead of carrying the puck over the blue line, a player shoots it into the corner and forwards go after it, so that the forecheck starts in the opponent's zone.

USA Hockey's tactical guide is notably sceptical of doing this by habit. It says dumping the puck "should be used only as a last resort or as a pre-determined play," and adds a condition: before the puck is dumped, a forward "must be in an advantaged position" to reach it before the opposing defencemen. Its blunter line is that working hard to win the puck and then shooting it away to chase it again "doesn't make much sense."

Read together, those lines describe the good version of dump-and-chase: a deliberate, timed play with a forward already winning the race, rather than a way of getting rid of the puck.

## The rules every system plays under

Three rules from the NHL's 2026-27 rulebook shape all of the above:

- **The neutral zone is the space between the blue lines** (Rule 1.6), which is where the trap lives and where every breakout has to pass.
- **The attacking blue line is the only offside line** (Rule 83). A long pass from deep in a team's own end to a teammate at the far blue line is legal, which gives a team one way to get past a forecheck before it sets.
- **Icing is judged from the centre red line** (Rule 81). A dump-in from a team's own half is icing unless that team is short-handed — the reason dump-ins come after the red line.

## Choosing a system is a roster decision

USA Hockey's manual says the quality of a team's personnel is "an important factor" in choosing a system, and its youth skill-progression guide tells coaches to teach the concepts behind each system — why a player does what he does, and what the opponent is being forced to do — rather than drilling patterns alone. That makes system choice a question about players as much as about tactics.

| System | What it asks of the first forechecker | What the roster needs | What it gives up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1-2-2 | Angle the carrier to one side | Wingers who hold the boards; a defenceman who can hold the line | Clean exits when the opponent moves the puck quickly |
| Two-man pressure | Pressure deep, below the circles | Fast, persistent forwards; a reliable high forward; a mobile defenceman who can pinch | Space behind the forecheckers if both are beaten |
| Neutral-zone trap | Steer the carrier to the boards in the neutral zone | Positional discipline and patience across all five | Pressure in the offensive zone |
| Dump-and-chase | Win the race to the corner | Speed and players who win puck battles | Possession, deliberately, in exchange for territory |

The right-hand column matters most. Each system is a trade, and a coach chooses which risk his players are best equipped to carry.
