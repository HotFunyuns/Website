The Punic Wars were three wars between the Roman Republic and Carthage, fought between 264 and 146 BC. Wikipedia describes them as forty-three years of warfare on land and sea across the western Mediterranean, plus a four-year revolt against Carthage. At the start, Carthage was the dominant power in the western Mediterranean with an extensive maritime empire, while Rome had a strong army but no navy. At the end, Carthage did not exist. This guide takes each war in turn, with the treaty terms that linked them.

## Two powers that had been allies

The wars did not grow out of a long rivalry. Wikipedia notes that Rome and Carthage had declared mutual friendship in formal alliances in 509 BC, 348 BC and around 279 BC, with strong commercial links. By 270 BC Rome had conquered all of peninsular Italy south of the Arno. Carthage, from its capital in what is now Tunisia, dominated southern Iberia, much of coastal North Africa, the Balearic Islands, Corsica, Sardinia and western Sicily. Sicily sat between them.

The EBSCO Research Starter by Jeremiah B. McCall puts the trigger plainly: Messina, in north-east Sicily, appealed to Rome for help against the Carthaginians, and the Romans, who "seem to have feared" Carthaginian control so near Italy, crossed over.

## The First Punic War, 264–241 BC

The fighting took place mainly on Sicily and in its waters, with campaigns in North Africa, Corsica and Sardinia. Rome built a navy during the war; in 243 BC, Wikipedia says, the Senate asked Rome's wealthiest citizens to finance one ship each, repayable from future Carthaginian reparations.

Carthage was defeated in 241 BC. Under the Treaty of Lutatius it paid 3,200 talents of silver and Sicily was annexed as the first Roman province. The peace then triggered the Mercenary War inside Carthaginian territory, and while Carthage was weakened, Rome demanded Sardinia and Corsica and a further 1,200-talent indemnity. McCall's entry dates that surrender of the islands to 238 BCE and calls it "adding insult to injury". Carthage then built a new empire in Iberia.

## Hannibal's war, 218–201 BC

The Second Punic War began in spring 218 BC, when Rome declared war after Hannibal besieged and sacked Saguntum, a Spanish town allied with Rome, in 219 BC. Wikipedia notes scholarly debate over whether Hannibal deliberately provoked Rome. Rather than wait in Spain, he marched through southern France and over the Alps into Italy — the route traced on the 1874 map of the wars that the University of South Florida's Educational Technology Clearinghouse reproduces.

Three battles in three years followed; Wikipedia quotes the historian Toni Ñaco del Hoyo calling them the three "great military calamities" Rome suffered in the war's first three years:

| Battle | Year | Outcome reported by Wikipedia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trebia | 218 BC | Roman army encircled; some 10,000 of 42,000 escaped |
| Lake Trasimene | 217 BC | 15,000 Romans killed and 15,000 captured |
| Cannae | 216 BC | At least 67,500 Romans killed or captured |

Rome had authorised a force of 86,000 for 216 BC, the largest in its history to that point. After Trasimene it had also tried the opposite approach under the dictator Fabius Maximus, avoiding pitched battle — a strategy, Wikipedia notes, that was unpopular while Italy was being devastated.

Hannibal stayed in Italy for fourteen years but could not win the war there. McCall's entry credits Rome's "great reserves of manpower and the steadfast loyalty of many of their central Italian allies". Rome took Iberia, and in 204 BC Scipio landed in Africa. Carthage recalled Hannibal, and at Zama in October 202 BC the Roman and Numidian cavalry drove off the Carthaginian horse and returned to attack the rear of Hannibal's infantry.

The treaty, accepted in spring 201 BC, stripped Carthage of its overseas territories and some African ones, imposed an indemnity of 10,000 silver talents payable over 50 years, limited its fleet to 10 warships, banned war elephants and prohibited war outside Africa, and inside Africa without Rome's permission. Scipio took the name Africanus.

## Counting the years and the payments

The headline span is 264 to 146 BC, but the fighting was concentrated. A worked example using Wikipedia's dates:

- First war: 264 − 241 = 23 years
- Second war: 218 − 201 = 17 years
- Third war: 149 − 146 = 3 years
- Total: 23 + 17 + 3 = 43 years, matching Wikipedia's "forty-three years of warfare"

The 201 BC indemnity, spread evenly, works out at 10,000 ÷ 50 = 200 talents a year. The first war's demands, including the later Sardinian levy, came to 3,200 + 1,200 = 4,400 talents. Wikipedia notes that several different "talents" existed in antiquity and that its figures are Euboic talents, so these totals should not be compared directly with talents mentioned in other contexts, such as the ransom Darius offered [Alexander the Great](/blog/alexander-the-great-explained/).

## The Third Punic War and the end of Carthage, 149–146 BC

Over the following decades the Numidian king Masinissa repeatedly encroached on Carthaginian land. When Carthage defended itself in 151 BC, Rome treated it as justification and declared war in 149 BC. Wikipedia describes the Romans first demanding Carthage's armaments — 200,000 sets of armour and 2,000 catapults were handed over — and then demanding the city be abandoned and rebuilt away from the sea, at which point Carthage refused.

The siege lasted into 146 BC. Under Scipio Aemilianus the Romans broke in and spent six days clearing the city. There were 50,000 Carthaginian prisoners, a small proportion of the pre-war population, who were sold into slavery. The territory became the Roman province of Africa, with Utica as its capital.

One popular detail is wrong. Wikipedia states that the tradition of the Romans sowing the city with salt "has been shown to have been a 19th-century invention". Some reference works still repeat it, including McCall's EBSCO entry, which says the Romans were "sowing salt in the surrounding fields". It is a good case for [common history myths](/blog/common-history-myths-corrected/).

## Who told the story

No primary sources written from a Carthaginian point of view survive. Wikipedia names Polybius, a Greek taken to Rome as a hostage in 167 BC, as the most reliable source; he accompanied Scipio Aemilianus at Carthage, and modern historians think he treated Scipio's family too favourably while accepting his account largely at face value. Livy is openly pro-Roman and his battle accounts are "often demonstrably inaccurate". The method for weighing writers like these is in [how to spot bias in history writing](/blog/how-to-spot-bias-in-history-writing/).
