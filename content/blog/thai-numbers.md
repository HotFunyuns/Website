Thai numbers are among the most regular parts of the language: learn ten words, three special cases and a handful of place-value words, and you can say any price in the country. This reference covers counting, the Thai digits, big numbers and money. Romanisations follow RTGS, which Wikipedia's article on Thai lists as an official romanisation standard; it does not mark tone, so pair each word with audio.

## Zero to ten

| Number | Thai digit | Thai word | RTGS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | ๐ | ศูนย์ | sun |
| 1 | ๑ | หนึ่ง | nueng |
| 2 | ๒ | สอง | song |
| 3 | ๓ | สาม | sam |
| 4 | ๔ | สี่ | si |
| 5 | ๕ | ห้า | ha |
| 6 | ๖ | หก | hok |
| 7 | ๗ | เจ็ด | chet |
| 8 | ๘ | แปด | paet |
| 9 | ๙ | เก้า | kao |
| 10 | ๑๐ | สิบ | sip |

Wikipedia notes that several of these words resemble the numbers of southern Chinese varieties such as Cantonese and Min Nan, and that Thai, Lao and Khmer numbers are quite similar, though the numerals vary somewhat in shape and pronunciation.

## The two special cases: et and yi

**One becomes et.** Used alone, one is *nueng*. At the end of a larger number it becomes *et* (เอ็ด): 11 is *sip et*, 21 is *yi sip et*, 101 is *roi et*. Wikipedia notes that the Royal Thai Navy mandated *nueng* in every case, as in *sip nueng*, but everyday Thai uses *et*.

**Two becomes yi in the twenties.** As the tens digit, two is *yi* (ยี่), so 20 is *yi sip* and 22 is *yi sip song*. Everywhere else it is *song*: 32 is *sam sip song*, 200 is *song roi*. In casual speech, Wikipedia says, *yi sip* can be shortened to *yip* from 21 to 29, as in *yip et* for 21.

## Tens to a million

| Number | Thai word | RTGS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | สิบ | sip |
| 100 | ร้อย | roi |
| 1,000 | พัน | phan |
| 10,000 | หมื่น | muen |
| 100,000 | แสน | saen |
| 1,000,000 | ล้าน | lan |

Thai has single words for **10,000** and **100,000**, so large numbers group differently from English. Above a million, put a multiplier before *lan*: ten million is *sip lan*.

Wikipedia gives three rules for building numbers. *Roi*, *phan*, *muen* and *saen* take a number in front, though *nueng* is optional, so 100 is *roi* or *nueng roi*. *Nueng* never goes before *sip*: 210 is *song roi sip*. And the parts simply run in order, so 232 is *song roi sam sip song*.

## A worked example: three numbers

1. **45,250**: 4 × 10,000, 5 × 1,000, 2 × 100 and 5 × 10, so *si muen ha phan song roi ha sip*.
2. **1,280,000**: one million plus 2 × 100,000 and 8 × 10,000, so *nueng lan song saen paet muen*.
3. **121**: *roi yi sip et*, with both special cases at once.

## Thai digits

Thailand has its own digits, ๐ ๑ ๒ ๓ ๔ ๕ ๖ ๗ ๘ ๙, which follow the same positional system as Arabic numerals: ๒๕ is 25. Wikipedia notes that **Arabic numerals are more common** today, so the Thai digits are worth learning to recognise rather than to write.

## Decimals, fractions and order

- **Decimals** use *chut*, "dot": 1.01 is *nueng chut sun nueng*.
- **Fractions** use *nai*, "in": ⅓ is *nueng nai sam*.
- **Half** is *khrueng*; one and a half sheets of paper is *kradat nueng phaen khrueng*.
- **Negatives** use *lop*, "minus": −11 is *lop sip et*.
- **Ordinals** put *thi* first: *thi nueng*, first; *thi song*, second.

## Counting things

Thai nouns do not change for plural. Wikipedia explains that a count is usually **noun, then number, then a classifier**: "five teachers" is *khru ha khon*, literally "teacher five person". [Thai classifiers](/blog/thai-classifiers/) covers the common ones.

## Prices in baht

The **baht** (บาท, sign ฿) is divided into **100 satang**. Wikipedia lists banknotes of **฿20, ฿50, ฿100, ฿500 and ฿1,000** in use, and coins of 25 and 50 satang and ฿1, ฿2, ฿5 and ฿10. To ask a price, use **thao rai** (เท่าไร), "how much?", spelled เท่าไหร่ in casual writing. A price of ฿350 is *sam roi ha sip baht*, and ฿1,250 is *phan song roi ha sip baht*.
