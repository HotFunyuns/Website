[Learn to Trade Stocks Beginner](/apps/learn-to-trade-stocks/) is a stock trading simulator built for learning. It combines lessons on how markets work with a practice account funded by a virtual balance, so you can place trades, watch what happens and review your reasoning without any money at risk. Its Google Play listing is explicit about the limits: the app is for education and simulated trading, with no real-money brokerage transactions, no personalised financial advice and no guaranteed profits. It is free, supported by ads, and Google Play lists no in-app purchases for it.

This guide explains what is in the app and a sensible order to use it in.

## What the app contains

| Area | What it offers |
| --- | --- |
| Lessons | From what a stock is to order types, portfolios, risk, options and technical indicators; the store screenshots describe an academy organised by topic, including chart reading and candlestick patterns |
| Paper trading | A virtual starting balance, a guided first trade, and buy and sell practice |
| Orders | Market, limit and stop orders, with positions, open orders and profit and loss tracked |
| Chart replay | Simulated price action you can play, step through and speed up |
| Practice | Simulated scenarios and daily challenges |
| Journal | A trading journal for reviewing decisions |

The listing frames the harder topics carefully. Options are described as contracts with their own risks, including leverage and time decay; technical indicators as summaries of price or volume information that cannot confirm a future outcome or remove uncertainty. That framing matches how this site's [investing basics guides](/blog/topics/investing-basics/) treat them.

## A sensible first week

**Day 1: what you are buying.** Start with the lessons on ownership. A share is a small piece of a company, not a lottery ticket; [what is a stock](/blog/what-is-a-stock/) covers the basics in more depth.

**Day 2: the guided first trade.** Place the guided trade and look at everything the app records: the price you paid, the position it created and how profit and loss is calculated as the price moves.

**Day 3: order types.** Practise the same trade three ways, as a market order, a limit order and a stop order, and note when each one fills. The SEC's Investor.gov explains the trade-off precisely: a market order is executed but its price is not guaranteed, while a limit order controls the price but may never be filled. The [market versus limit order guide](/blog/market-order-vs-limit-order/) goes further.

**Days 4–5: chart replay.** Step through a replayed chart one bar at a time. Before each step, write down what you expect and why, then see what happened. This trains the habit of separating a reason from a result, which the listing puts at the centre: when you sell, compare the outcome with your reason for buying. The [candlestick chart guide](/blog/how-to-read-candlestick-charts/) explains what each bar shows.

**Days 6–7: the journal.** Record every practice trade: the reason, the plan for exit, and what happened. The [trading journal guide](/blog/trading-journal-how-to/) suggests what to write.

## Using the practice account well

- **Keep position sizes realistic.** A virtual balance makes it easy to bet the lot on one idea. Practise with the amounts you might really use, and look at how one position changes the whole portfolio.
- **Practise stop orders.** Stop orders are among the order types the app lets you practise. Learn what a stop does in a fast-moving market before you rely on one.
- **Review losses as carefully as gains.** The app's own routine is to compare each result with your reason for buying or selling, and that works for trades that went against you as well as those that worked.
- **Change one thing at a time.** If you test an idea, keep the rest of your approach the same so you can tell what made the difference.

## What a simulator cannot teach

Paper trading removes the cost of mistakes, which is its value, but it also removes things that matter with real money: commissions and fees, taxes on gains, orders that fill at a worse price than the quote, and the emotional pull of watching your own money fall. The app's listing says it directly: simulated results cannot predict what will happen in the real market. [What is paper trading?](/blog/what-is-paper-trading/) covers the gap in detail.
