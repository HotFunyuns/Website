**Listening is harder than reading because speech removes every support reading gives you.** No spaces between words. No punctuation. No rereading. No control over pace. A reader who does not understand a sentence can stop and work on it; a listener has already moved on.

That is why comprehension in a new language is almost always ahead in reading and behind in listening, and why the gap does not close on its own.

## The skill underneath: segmentation

The thing that makes speech incomprehensible at first is not speed. It is **segmentation** — finding where one word ends and the next begins.

Speech has no gaps between words. The impression of gaps in your own language is produced by your brain, which already knows where the boundaries are. In a language you do not know, the stream is genuinely continuous.

That has two consequences worth internalising:

**"They speak so fast" is usually a segmentation problem.** Native speakers of your own language speak at a similar rate. The difference is that you can segment one and not the other.

**Segmentation improves with exposure, not with study.** No amount of grammar explains where boundaries fall. Only hearing a great deal of the language does.

The problem is most acute in languages written without word spaces — [the Lao alphabet explained](/blog/lao-alphabet-explained/) covers a script that makes the same demand visually — but it applies everywhere.

## Passive listening: the honest verdict

Playing audio in the background while doing something else is popular advice and mostly ineffective.

The reason is the same one that makes [comprehensible input](/blog/comprehensible-input-explained/) work: material has to be *mostly understandable* for exposure to build anything. Audio you cannot follow is noise, and hours of noise produce familiarity with the sound of the language and very little else.

Familiarity is not nothing — it helps with rhythm and with reducing the intimidation factor. It is just far less than the hours suggest.

**Active listening to comprehensible material beats passive listening to difficult material by a wide margin, per hour.**

## A routine that works

**1. Choose short material you mostly understand.** One to three minutes. Understanding roughly most of it is the target; if you understand almost none, it is too hard.

**2. Listen once without any text.** Get the shape. Do not worry about detail.

**3. Listen again, several times.** Each pass extracts more. This is where the value is, and it is the step people skip in favour of new material.

**4. Read the transcript.** Find out what you missed. The gap between what you heard and what was said is the information you are after.

**5. Listen again with the transcript.** Attach the sounds to the words.

**6. Listen once more without it.** You will hear things that were inaudible twenty minutes ago. That experience — the same audio suddenly becoming clear — is the mechanism working, and it is worth seeking out because it is motivating.

**7. Do dictation on a short section.** Write down what you hear. This is the highest-yield step and the least pleasant.

## Why dictation is worth the discomfort

Ordinary listening lets you skate over a word you did not catch. The meaning arrives roughly intact and you move on, and the word you missed stays missed.

Dictation makes that impossible. Writing down what you heard forces you to resolve every sound, including the ones you had been quietly skipping.

It is also retrieval practice, which is one of the better-supported techniques in the [Dunlosky review of effective learning methods](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/) — you are producing from memory rather than recognising.

## Repetition beats variety, early on

The instinct is to listen to many different things. Early on, the opposite is more efficient.

Familiarity lowers the effective difficulty of a recording without changing the recording. A piece that was too hard on the first pass is often ideal on the fourth — and you have created appropriate-level material out of material that was not, without needing to find anything new.

This matters most for under-resourced languages, where there is not much material to find. [Learn Lao for beginners](/blog/learn-lao-beginners-guide/) covers studying when resources are scarce.

Once segmentation is reasonably established, variety becomes more valuable, because you need exposure to different voices, speeds and registers.

## What listening will not fix

**Production.** Understanding a distinction does not mean you can produce it. This is starkest in tonal languages — see [what is a tonal language](/blog/what-is-a-tonal-language/) — but it is true generally. [The shadowing technique explained](/blog/shadowing-technique-explained/) covers a production-side method that pairs well with listening work.

**Accuracy.** You can follow a sentence perfectly without noticing the grammatical detail you get wrong yourself. That needs feedback.
