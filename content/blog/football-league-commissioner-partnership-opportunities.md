Rules, owners, schedules, votes and the occasional crisis: Football League Commissioner makes the commissioner's in-tray the game. Reign Creative LLC developed it as an American football league simulation, and we think its most natural partners are people who already think about how leagues are governed — sports-management educators — and publishers who specialise in simulation games. Two scenarios below show how each might use it, followed by what a licence could and could not cover.

## The office, as the listing describes it

From the Google Play listing: you customise rules, explore expansion and relocation, and give the league its own identity, while simulation shows the consequences. You can advance a single game, a week, a season or the playoffs, then read standings, schedules and stats. An observer mode follows favourite teams and players as storylines develop. The store screenshots add the administrative layer — owners, media deals and league health, inbox proposals, votes and crises, schedule creation, transaction approvals and salary-cap settings. A Yearbook, timelines, records and awards build a past, and multiple saves hold separate worlds. Sandbox and Unlimited Draft modes sit alongside.

The app shows ads, and Google Play lists no in-app purchases. Its own screenshots say the league uses real players from across football's eras, with ratings and results that are the simulation's estimates, and the app's page on this site adds that the game is not affiliated with, endorsed by or licensed by any league, team, players' association or athlete. For players, the [Football League Commissioner guide](/blog/football-league-commissioner-guide/) explains each mode, and the app page is [/apps/football-league-commissioner/](/apps/football-league-commissioner/).

## Scenario A: a sports-governance seminar

Imagine a lecturer running a seminar on how leagues are run. The site's explainer on [what a sports commissioner does](/blog/what-does-a-sports-commissioner-do/) covers the real role; the app gives students a sandbox in which to make those calls. A group could propose an expansion team, steer it through a vote, adjust the salary-cap settings, and then simulate several seasons to see what the league's history records. Another group might build a schedule and compare it with the principles in [how sports schedules are made](/blog/how-sports-schedules-are-made/).

The caveat matters as much as the idea. The app's page says simulated results are the game's own and not predictions of any real season, so the output is a prompt for argument, not evidence. An institutional partnership would be scoped with the educator, and we make no claim about learning outcomes.

As a worked example, a ten-week seminar in which each of five groups runs its own save, simulating one season per week, would produce 5 × 10 = 50 simulated seasons in total, and each group's Yearbook would hold ten. The numbers simply show how the multiple-save feature scales to a class.

## Scenario B: a simulation publisher's catalogue

A publisher focused on management and simulation games might see three routes. It could **publish or distribute** the game in regions where it wants a football title. It could **license** it as a branded or white-label edition where that is technically feasible, swapping presentation while the simulation stays the same. Or it could **co-develop** additions, for instance deeper owner negotiations or new crisis types, building on systems the screenshots already show. Localisation into new languages would be a project, since the listing does not describe language options.

## What a licence could not include

We can discuss licensing what we own: the software, its design and its presentation. We cannot license league names, team marks or player rights, because we hold none, and a partner wanting them would need agreements with the rights holders. We also do not publish financial details, user figures or prices on this site, and nothing here is a binding offer.

## How talks begin, and how an ownership change works

Use the inquiry form on the [partnerships page](/partnerships/) or write to ReignCreativeSupport@gmail.com. Tell us who you are, the app, and the idea in a few sentences; please keep confidential, financial or personal information out of that first email. A call follows when there is a fit, with non-public material shared later under NDA where appropriate. Everything is nonbinding until due diligence is complete and both parties sign.

If a publisher would rather own the app, Google's [help page on app transfers](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/6230247) sets the route. The request is submitted from Play Console, then reviewed and approved by the receiving developer; Google states that its support team reviews and answers transfer requests within two business days. The app's users, statistics, ratings, reviews, content ratings and store listing travel with it, while orders made before the move remain with the original account. Linkage settings for integrated services such as Google Analytics do not travel, so permissions are re-granted, and ad integrations need updating for the new account.
