A colourful mood grid, filled in eagerly for ten days and then forgotten, tells you very little. A tracker that lasts is small enough to fill in every day and useful enough to look back at. This guide covers what to record, how to read the results without fooling yourself, and when a mood log is a sign to talk to someone.

## What a mood tracker is for

A mood tracker turns a vague sense of "I've been off lately" into something you can look at. The NHS notes that if you know **what is causing a low mood, it might be easier to find ways to manage it**, and gives examples of things that may cause one: pressure at **work**, **family** and relationship difficulties, **money** problems, **health** and **bereavement**. A tracker is a way to notice those links in your own life, over weeks rather than in the moment.

## What to record each day

Keep the daily entry under a minute, so it is easy to repeat:

1. **A rating on a fixed scale.** 1 to 5 is enough. Use the same scale every day, and rate at roughly the same time, such as before bed.
2. **One or two tags.** Pick things you suspect matter: sleep, exercise, time outside, seeing friends, a hard day at work.
3. **One line of words.** "Tired after the late shift" tells you more in a month than any colour.

Resist adding ten categories at the start. You can add a tag later, and a short entry is easier to keep up than a long one.

## Why the words matter

Naming a feeling is more than record-keeping. Wikipedia's article on **affect labelling**, "putting feelings into words", notes that labelling a negative emotional state has been found to reduce the experience of it, and gives the example that **writing about a negative experience in a journal may improve mood**. It is also candid that the research is at an **early stage**, and that whether laboratory findings apply to real life is still an open question. Treat the one-line note as a habit that may help, not a treatment.

## A worked example: two weeks of entries

Fourteen days of ratings, tagged "walked outside" and "slept under 7 hours":

| Group | Days | Average mood |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All days | 14 | 3.29 |
| Walked outside | 7 | 3.86 |
| Did not walk | 7 | 2.71 |
| Slept under 7 hours | 6 | 2.50 |
| Slept 7 hours or more | 8 | 3.88 |

Both tags look important. But check the overlap: only **2 of the 6** short-sleep days included a walk, against **5 of the 8** other days. The two habits travel together, so the walking gap is partly a sleep gap. Splitting them helps a little. On days with enough sleep, walking days averaged **4.20** against **3.33** without, and on short-sleep days **3.00** against **2.25**, but some of those groups contain only two to five days. The honest conclusion after two weeks is "keep tracking both", not "walking fixes my mood".

## How to read trends without overreading them

- **Look at weeks, not days.** One bad Tuesday is noise; four bad Tuesdays is a pattern worth asking about.
- **Compare with and without.** Average the rating on days with a tag against days without it.
- **Check for overlap**, as in the example above.
- **Mark one-off events**, such as illness or travel, so they do not distort the picture.
- **Remember the arrow can point both ways**: low mood can cause skipped walks as well as the reverse.

## Three habits that distort a mood log

- **Logging only bad days**, which makes every month look worse than it was.
- **Changing the scale** halfway through, which makes old and new entries impossible to compare.
- **Judging the entry**: the tracker records the day; it does not grade it.

## When to talk to someone

A mood tracker is not a diagnosis. The NHS says a low mood that lasts **2 weeks or more** could be a sign of depression, and advises seeing a GP if you have had a low mood for more than 2 weeks, are struggling to cope, or what you are trying is not helping. NIMH notes that sad feelings usually pass with time, but depression is different, and that anyone struggling or having thoughts of suicide in the US can **call or text 988**, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. In the UK, the NHS lists **Samaritans on 116 123**. A tracker can make that conversation easier, because it shows how long things have been hard.
