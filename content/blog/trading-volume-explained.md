Below many price charts sits a row of bars: volume. It is a simple market statistic, the number of shares that changed hands, and it is easy to read more into it than it contains. Knowing what it can and cannot tell you makes the rest of the chart easier to read.

## What volume measures

Volume is a count of shares traded in a period: a minute, a day, a week. A stock that traded 2 million shares today had a daily volume of 2 million. StockCharts' ChartSchool notes that on most stock charts, volume bars are displayed at the bottom, so the price history and the trading activity can be read together.

The single most important fact about volume: **every share traded had a buyer and a seller.** When 2 million shares change hands, 2 million shares were bought and 2 million were sold. Volume therefore measures how much trading happened, not which side "won". Phrases such as "heavy buying pushed the stock up" describe the price change; the volume alone cannot.

## Reading volume against its own average

Raw volume is hard to interpret on its own: 500,000 shares is enormous for one company and negligible for another. Two comparisons help:

- **Average daily volume:** the typical volume over recent weeks or months, which a quote screen may show.
- **Relative volume:** today's volume divided by the average. A value of 3 means three times the usual activity.

**Example.** A stock that usually trades 1.5 million shares a day trades 4.5 million today: relative volume is 4.5 ÷ 1.5 = 3. Something drew three times the usual activity, perhaps news such as earnings, which FINRA notes are normally released outside regular hours. A spike tells you something happened; it does not tell you whether it was good or bad.

## Volume and the price you pay

ChartSchool's overview of technical analysis notes that heavily traded stocks allow investors to trade quickly because there are many buyers and sellers, while in a thinly traded stock you may have to change your entry or exit price considerably to make a trade, and low-liquidity stocks' prices are easier to manipulate. Lower volume is also one reason a [bid–ask spread](/blog/bid-ask-spread-explained/) can be wider.

The same applies across the trading day. FINRA's guide to extended-hours trading notes that activity outside regular hours is dwarfed by the tens of millions of transactions in the regular session, so orders may be filled partly or not at all, and prices can be less competitive. [Stock market hours](/blog/stock-market-hours-explained/) covers those sessions.

## Volume with price

Chart readers combine volume with the price move in the same period, for example when judging a move through a level in [support and resistance](/blog/support-and-resistance-explained/). ChartSchool's overview describes volume-based indicators as a way to measure buying or selling pressure. These are interpretations, not rules, and like other chart readings they can fail.

## On Balance Volume

ChartSchool describes **On Balance Volume (OBV)**, developed by Joe Granville and first explained in his 1963 book, as one of the earliest measures to track the inflow and outflow of volume. Its rule is simple:

- If today's close is above yesterday's, add today's volume to the running total.
- If it is below, subtract it.

**Example.** Day 1 volume 8,200 (the starting value). Day 2 closes higher on 8,100: OBV = 16,300. Day 3 closes lower on 8,300: OBV = 8,000. ChartSchool stresses that the OBV's scale is not meaningful; analysts look at whether the line is rising or falling compared with previous periods.

ChartSchool records that Granville theorised that volume precedes price: a rising OBV reflects positive volume pressure that can lead to higher prices, and a falling OBV negative pressure. That is a theory, not a rule. OBV also assigns a whole day's volume to one side based only on the close, which is a crude simplification of thousands of trades. Like other indicators, it summarises the past.

## A warning about volume and fraud

The SEC describes pump-and-dump schemes as touting a small microcap stock with false or misleading claims, urging people to buy quickly, then selling into the buying frenzy that creates. Heavy activity in a little-known stock can therefore make it look popular for exactly the wrong reasons. [Investment fraud red flags](/blog/investment-fraud-red-flags/) explains the pattern.
