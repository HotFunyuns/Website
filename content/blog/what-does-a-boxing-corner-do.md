Between every round of a boxing match there is a minute of controlled chaos in each corner: water, ice, advice, a cut being closed, a decision about whether to go on. The people doing it are the boxer's **corner**, officially called **seconds**. Wikipedia describes a cornerman as a coach or trainer assisting a fighter during a bout, who must stay outside the combat area during the round and may enter the ring in the break. This guide sets out what corners may do under two rulebooks: Minnesota's professional boxing rules, as an example of a US state regulator, and World Boxing's competition rules for amateur boxing, in the edition in force since November 2024.

## How many in the corner

| | Professional (Minnesota rules) | Amateur (World Boxing rules) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seconds allowed | Up to 3 in a non-title contest; up to 4 in a championship | Up to 3 |
| In the ring between rounds | 1 | 1 |
| On the ring apron | 2 | Up to 2 may mount the apron |
| Elsewhere | In championships, the fourth stays at ground level | |

Minnesota's rules also require each chief second and corner member to be identified to the inspector and referee before the bout, and seconds must be licensed where the supervising commission licenses them.

## During the round

The rules keep the corner out of the fight while it is happening. Under Minnesota's rules, seconds **must remain seated**, must not mount the stairs or apron or enter the ring until the bell ends the round, and must **refrain from excessive coaching**. World Boxing's rules similarly bar seconds from entering the ring outside rest periods and from encouraging or inciting spectators. Even after a knockdown, Minnesota's rules say seconds may not enter the ring to help the boxer until medical personnel instruct them to.

## The one-minute break

Minnesota's rules list what a second may do in the one-minute rest period:

1. **coach** the boxer;
2. **treat cuts, abrasions and swelling**;
3. provide **water, ice, approved sports drinks or other cooling-down** techniques.

Then the clock takes over. Seconds must leave the ring at the timekeeper's whistle, **ten seconds before** the round begins, and remove everything from the ring and platform before the bell. Boxers themselves may not leave the ring during the break.

That leaves roughly 50 seconds of useful work: a sip of water, a cold sponge, an instruction and any urgent treatment. Before the bout, the corner has administrative jobs too: Minnesota's rules require the boxer's wrapped hands to be presented for inspection by the commission before the gloves go on, sealed sports drinks to be presented for examination, and the corner to present the boxer ready to box when the referee calls both fighters for final instructions.

## Cuts and swelling

Wikipedia notes cornermen may perform cutman duties, applying ice or adrenaline to reduce swelling and stop bleeding. World Boxing's rules spell out what is allowed in amateur bouts: seconds may carry ice bags, an **Enswell**, gauze pads and swabs, and for cuts may use only adrenaline 1/1000 and non-petroleum skin-protective jelly. No other drugs are allowed. Wikipedia's article on cutmen explains the Enswell: the cutman presses the chilled tool against the skin to cool and reduce swelling, especially around the eyes, where swelling can impair vision.

## Stopping the fight

Here the two rulebooks differ sharply:

- **Amateur (World Boxing):** a second may retire the boxer by mounting the apron and **throwing the towel into the ring**, except while the referee is counting. The opponent then wins by abandonment.
- **Professional (Minnesota):** seconds must signal the referee to stop the fight **by mounting the ring apron rather than throwing towels**, sponges or anything else into the ring. Only the referee can stop the contest, and may consult the ringside physician at any time.

Corners also carry responsibility: Minnesota's rules state that a boxer may lose points or be **disqualified** for the conduct of their chief second or corner. [KO versus TKO](/blog/ko-vs-tko-how-fights-end/) explains how stoppages are recorded.

## Eleven breaks in a twelve-round fight

Professional bouts last up to twelve rounds, and Wikipedia's article on professional boxing notes that boxers are generally allowed to take substantial punishment before a fight is halted. A twelve-round fight has eleven one-minute breaks, against two in a three-round amateur bout, and each one is another chance for coaching, cut work and the decision whether to signal a stop; [amateur versus professional boxing](/blog/amateur-vs-professional-boxing/) compares the two codes.
