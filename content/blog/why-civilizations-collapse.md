Civilizations rarely collapse in the way the word implies. What the archaeological record usually shows is a political order failing while the population, the language and much of the culture continue under different arrangements. "Collapse" bundles four separable outcomes — the end of central authority, the abandonment of cities, a fall in population, and cultural discontinuity — and the interesting cases are the ones where only some of them happen.

This article is the comparative view. Two specific cases are handled elsewhere: [what caused the Bronze Age collapse](/blog/what-caused-the-bronze-age-collapse/) works through the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BC, and [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/) sets out the competing explanations for the western empire. Neither is re-argued here.

## Four things that get filed under one word

Separating the outcomes does most of the analytical work.

**Political fragmentation** is the loss of a centre that could tax, conscript and adjudicate over a wide area. It is the most commonly observed of the four and the least dramatic in its effects on daily life, because regional authorities usually take over.

**Urban abandonment** is a settlement-pattern change: cities shrink or empty while the countryside stays occupied. It is highly visible archaeologically, which is one reason it dominates popular accounts.

**Demographic decline** is an actual fall in the number of people, through mortality or emigration, and it is much harder to demonstrate than either of the above. Radiocarbon-date frequencies and settlement surveys are the usual proxies, and both have known biases.

**Cultural discontinuity** is the end of a tradition — a script that stops being written, a religious system that stops being practised. It is the rarest of the four, and claims of it are frequently overstated by observers who were looking only at elite production.

A society can lose its state and keep its cities. It can lose its cities and keep its population. Asking which of these actually happened turns an unanswerable question into a tractable one.

## The Maya case, and why it changed the vocabulary

The Classic Maya are the standard textbook example and the clearest case of a reassessment. Over roughly the eighth to tenth centuries, many southern lowland cities stopped raising dated monuments, dynastic inscriptions ceased, and major centres were depopulated. That much is not in dispute.

What has changed is the interpretation. A 2023 paper in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, written by fifteen specialists in Mesoamerican archaeology, argues directly against the dominant framing, concluding that "Mesoamerican urbanism was as much a story about resilience and adaptation to environmental change as it was about collapse," and noting that many centres persisted for centuries. Maya societies continued in the northern lowlands and the highlands. Maya languages are spoken today by millions of people in Mexico, Guatemala, Belize and Honduras. The Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia, Mexico's federal body for archaeology and history, manages the major sites and the research programmes around them as part of a living national heritage rather than a closed chapter.

This is not a semantic quibble. Describing a civilisation as having "disappeared" when its descendants are present is a factual error about the present as well as the past, and it has consequences for how living communities are treated.

## How climate evidence actually enters the argument

Palaeoclimate data has transformed this field, and it is worth understanding precisely what it can show. NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information maintains a very large archive of proxy records — tree rings, ice cores, corals, stalagmites, and ocean and lake sediments — that extend the climate record back well beyond instrumental measurement. Those proxies can date a drought or a cold interval independently of any human source.

What they cannot do is establish causation on their own. A precisely dated drought sitting near a precisely dated abandonment is a correlation, and societies have survived comparable droughts elsewhere. The strongest studies therefore connect the climate record to independent evidence of human consequences. A 2022 study in *Nature Communications* examined Mayapan, a Postclassic Maya capital occupied from roughly 1200 to 1450 CE, and found that increases in traumatic injuries in the skeletal record correlated strongly with drought conditions between about 1400 and 1450 CE, with mass burials showing evidence of violence. The authors argue that prolonged drought escalated factional conflict among rival elite families, contributing to the city's abandonment around 1450.

That is a chain with physical links at each stage: dated climate proxy, dated skeletal trauma, dated abandonment. It is a much stronger form of argument than "there was a drought, and then things went wrong."

## What makes societies absorb shocks

The more productive version of the question is not why societies fail but why most of them do not. Research published in *Nature* in 2024 analysed 154 periods of population decline across 16 global regions and some 30,000 years of radiocarbon evidence, and found that populations subject to more frequent disturbances were better at resisting subsequent shocks and recovered faster from them.

The finding also carries an uncomfortable corollary. Agricultural and pastoral societies experienced downturns more often than hunter-gatherer populations, so the resilience they developed was a response to a vulnerability they had created. Complexity is not simply protective or simply fragile; it changes the shape of the risk.

That is why single-mechanism theories — diminishing returns on complexity, elite overproduction, resource exhaustion — each explain some cases well and others badly. They are hypotheses to test against a record, not laws.

## Using the comparison without flattening it

Comparative collapse studies are valuable and easy to abuse. The abuse is to strip a case of its specifics until it becomes an illustration of a general theory, at which point the theory is being confirmed by its own examples. The discipline is to keep each case's evidence attached to it: what exactly was abandoned, over what period, on what dating, with what continuing afterwards.
