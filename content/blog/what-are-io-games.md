The "io" in io games is a web address. That is the whole etymology: .io is an internet country-code top-level domain, nominally assigned to the British Indian Ocean Territory, which became popular with technology organisations and websites because the two letters look like the computer-science term *input/output*. A run of browser games registered addresses there, the suffix stuck to the format, and a piece of domain-registry trivia turned into a genre label.

Which means the name is telling you something real, but not the thing most people assume it is telling you.

## From one game to a category

The label is traced to Agar.io, released in 2015. You control circular cells on a map presented as a Petri dish, growing by swallowing randomly generated matter and smaller player cells while staying clear of anything larger. The name comes from agar, the substance used to culture bacteria, which is a better joke than most genres get.

It was a fast, word-of-mouth success and it inspired a family of similar web games that came to be called ".io games" — some with the same objective and different characters, others importing mechanics from other genres entirely. Slither.io, which arrived the following year with a snake instead of a cell, is the other title most people can name, and it is explicitly described as similar in concept to Agar.io and reminiscent of the classic arcade Snake.

Snake itself is much older than either: a genre in which you steer the head of a growing line and must avoid colliding with obstacles and with yourself, originating in a 1976 arcade game and getting harder as the line lengthens. The io wave did not invent that. It put it in an arena with other people in it and made it startable in one tap.

## The four things the format actually shares

Because the label came from an address rather than a design document, there is no formal definition. What there is, is a recognisable shape.

**Instant entry.** No lobby, frequently no account, no loading screen you have time to read. The run starts when you tap.

**One rule.** Get bigger by consuming; avoid anything bigger. Or: claim territory; avoid your own trail. Whatever it is, you can explain it in a sentence and learn it in ten seconds.

**A shared arena.** Everyone is in the same space rather than a matched lobby of fixed size, and the space is already populated when you arrive. You are joining something in progress, and it continues after you die.

**No finish line.** There is no level to complete. You play until you are eaten, and the unit of play is the run.

That last point is the one that makes the format work on a phone. A run that ends by itself is a run you can start in a queue, and the emotional shape is much closer to an arcade high-score game than to a session-based multiplayer match.

The same "the run is the unit" logic shows up in other genres, which is why these games feel familiar to roguelite players even though almost nothing else is shared — [what actually makes a roguelike](/blog/what-makes-a-roguelike/) is worth reading if you want to see how differently two run-based formats can be built.

## What the label does not tell you

Here is the useful correction: **io does not mean live multiplayer.**

It is an easy assumption, because the format was born in browsers where a shared server was the whole novelty. But the suffix describes how the game plays, not how it is wired. Plenty of io-styled games use AI opponents for some or all of their modes — Agar.io's own mobile version includes a Relax mode in which all of the other players are bots.

Once you know that, the honest question to ask about any game in this shelf is not "is it io" but "who am I playing against, and does the game say so plainly".

## Ours says so plainly

[Jellyfish Arena Survivor io](/apps/jellyfish-arena-survivor-io/) is an eat-and-grow arena with the shape of an io title and the movement of a snake game. You start small, collect glowing food to grow longer, and avoid whatever is currently bigger than you. A live leaderboard tracks the ten largest rivals, the top spot carries a crown, and a minimap shows you where the food, the danger and the current leader are.

The arena fills with up to 100 AI-controlled rivals. They are not other people.

We could have been vaguer about that. The genre convention gives you plenty of cover — "arena", "rivals", "leaderboard" are all words that let a reader assume live players without you ever claiming it. We decided that describing an AI-filled arena in language designed to be mistaken for multiplayer is a small dishonesty that a player discovers within one run and resents for longer than that. So the store listing says the opposition is AI, and so does this page.

There is a genuine design consequence, and it is not all cost. A run starts the moment you tap, because there is no lobby to fill and no server to match you into. Nothing waits. The arena is populated the instant you arrive, at three in the morning as reliably as at eight in the evening, and it plays the same on a train with no signal as it does at home.

What it changes is pacing. AI rivals are more consistent than people: they do not rage-chase you across the map for two minutes, and they do not suddenly disconnect. That is more predictable and less chaotic, and whether that is a feature depends entirely on what you wanted. If the thing you love about io games is the unpredictability of real opponents, we are honestly not the game for that. The trade-offs of the bot-filled approach in general are covered in [io games without a connection](/blog/io-games-without-a-connection/).

## Is it a genre at all?

Strictly, no. It is a format label that escaped from a domain registry, and it now covers games with almost nothing mechanically in common — growth arenas, territory painters, shooters, snake variants. Calling it a genre is roughly as precise as calling everything on a particular publisher's website a genre.

But it is a useful label anyway, because it reliably predicts three things a player actually cares about: how long it takes to start, how long a session lasts, and whether you can stop mid-anything. Those are the questions that decide whether a game fits into five spare minutes, and very few genre labels answer them at all.

What it does not predict is difficulty, depth or opposition, and that is where the reading has to start.
