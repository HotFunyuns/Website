**Surviving longer and scoring higher are different goals, and a game's scoring system decides how different.** A player optimising for survival and one optimising for score make opposite decisions in the same situation, and both are correct for what they are doing.

## What the scoring system is asking for

Read the scoring rules before deciding how to play. Three common shapes, with very different implications:

**Time or distance based.** Points accrue for staying alive. Survival and scoring converge, and the optimal play is conservative.

**Kill based.** Points per enemy destroyed. Now aggression pays, and there is a real tension: staying alive is necessary but not sufficient.

**Multiplier or chain based.** Points scale with a chain that breaks on a mistake or a pause. This creates the sharpest strategy, because a single error costs everything accumulated rather than a fixed amount.

Most games combine these. The one that dominates is the one your strategy should follow.

## Multipliers make consistency the priority

When a chain multiplies your score and breaks on a miss, the arithmetic changes completely.

A large multiplier makes every subsequent point worth several. Breaking it does not cost you the points you missed — it costs you the amplification on everything that follows.

**The consequence: the safe play that maintains the chain usually beats the aggressive play that risks it**, even when the aggressive play would score more in isolation.

This is why high-level play in chain-scoring games often looks conservative in moments where a casual player would push. The chain is the asset; the individual kill is not.

## Risk is a resource with a variable price

The same risky action is correct in one moment and wrong in another. What changes is the cost of failure.

| Situation | Cost of failure | Risk pricing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early run, resources in hand | Low | Risk is cheap — take it |
| Mid run, score building | Moderate | Selective |
| Late run, score above your best | High | Expensive — protect it |
| Late run, score below your best | Low | Cheap again — nothing to protect |

That last row is the one people miss. **If your run is already worse than your record, there is nothing to protect** — the conservative play produces a score you do not want anyway, so risk is free.

This is the same principle that governs [when to take an aggressive line in golf](/blog/golf-course-management-basics/), and the same one that decides [when a quarterback should throw into coverage](/blog/reading-a-defense-basics/). Risk is priced by what failure costs right now, not by how good the outcome would be.

## Routing beats reacting

The biggest available improvement is usually not faster reactions. It is **deciding in advance how to handle sections you have seen before.**

Reactions have a ceiling. A plan does not.

Practically:

- **Identify the sections that cost you runs.** These are usually few.
- **Decide in advance** where you will be positioned, which resources you will spend, and what you will not attempt.
- **Execute the plan** rather than improvising, even when improvising looks tempting.

A routed section is executed almost automatically, which frees attention for the parts that genuinely require it. [Boss pattern recognition](/blog/boss-pattern-recognition/) covers the same idea applied to a single fight.

## Resource timing

Games with limited-use abilities — a shield, a bomb, a charged attack — turn scoring into a resource-allocation problem.

The common error is **spending defensively at the first sign of pressure.** A resource used to escape a situation you could have positioned out of is a resource unavailable for the section that genuinely needs it.

The habit worth building: before spending, ask whether positioning would have solved it. If yes, save the resource and fix the positioning.

## Endless modes

An endless mode makes the risk calculation continuous rather than staged. There is no finish line, so the run ends when you make a mistake — and the question is how much risk you are willing to carry indefinitely.

The equilibrium most players settle on: **conservative baseline, with selective aggression in the sections you have routed.** Uniform aggression ends runs early; uniform caution has a ceiling.

This open-ended score contract is the oldest one in the genre — [Space Invaders](https://www.museumofplay.org/games/space-invaders/), documented by the Strong National Museum of Play, established it — and [the history of shoot 'em up games](/blog/history-of-shoot-em-up-games/) traces how later designs elaborated on it.
