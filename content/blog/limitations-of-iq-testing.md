Every limitation below applies to a well-constructed instrument, administered correctly by a qualified person, with current norms. This is not the case against bad web quizzes — that argument is made in [are online IQ tests accurate](/blog/are-online-iq-tests-accurate/). It is the harder and more useful question: what remains true when everything is done properly.

Five limits survive good practice, and each of them changes how a score should be read.

## The instrument reports only on what it sampled

A battery is a fixed set of short tasks. Whatever it did not sample cannot appear in the result, no matter how important that ability is to the person's life.

This sounds obvious and is routinely ignored, because the reported number has no visible boundary on it. A composite score looks like a statement about a person. It is a summary of performance on seven or so subtests, chosen years earlier by a publisher for reasons of practicality as much as theory.

The same compression problem applies inside the test. A composite averages away a profile. Two people with identical composites can have opposite strengths across the domains that built them, and the profile is frequently the more informative object. Anyone reading a single number is reading the least detailed thing the assessment produced.

## The number is an estimate, not a reading

Measurement error is not an occasional failure. It is a permanent property of testing, quantified rather than eliminated.

A score reported as a single integer implies precision the instrument does not claim, which is why a competent report gives a confidence interval. The consequence is sharpest at boundaries: a result near a cut point can sit on either side of it on a second administration without anything about the person changing, as discussed in [percentile, standard score and raw score](/blog/percentiles-and-standard-scores/).

## Bias is tested, and the testing is more interesting than the argument

"Are IQ tests biased?" is usually debated as though it were a matter of opinion. In psychometrics it is a technical property with an established method.

The method is **differential item functioning**. It asks whether test takers from different groups who are at the *same measured ability level* respond differently to a particular item. That qualifier is what makes it useful: comparing group averages tells you nothing about the item, because the groups may genuinely differ in the ability the item measures. Hope and colleagues make the methodological point directly in their 2018 study in *BMC Medical Education* — DIF compares performance at every level of the ability curve, where a simple average can conceal differences at the lower end, where candidates are competing to pass, and at the upper end.

Their result is worth knowing. Across **2,773 questions answered by 13,694 candidates**, only eight items — 0.29 per cent — showed notable differential item functioning, and a blinded panel of clinician reviewers could identify no plausible explanation for the differences that were found. The authors concluded that group-level differences in performance could not be explained by unfairness at the question level.

Read that carefully, because it is easy to over-claim in both directions. It does not show that the assessment was fair in every sense; it shows that *item-level* unfairness was not the explanation for the group differences observed. Fairness at the item level and fairness of the whole enterprise are different questions, and a finding about one does not settle the other.

Test publishers treat this as process rather than argument. ETS states that it is committed to ensuring its tests and products are as free of bias as possible, that every product is formally evaluated against criteria covering offensive content, stereotypical representation, and racial, ethnic, gender and socioeconomic bias, and that staff involved in creating and evaluating questions receive extensive training in its fairness review process.

## Norms age, and the correction for it is contested

A score is a rank against a particular sample of people at a particular time. Raw performance drifts across generations, so instruments are renormed — and until they are, the score a person earns depends partly on how old the norms are.

The standard correction assumed roughly three points per decade. Winter, Trudel and Kaufman's 2024 analysis in the *Journal of Intelligence* unsettles it. Examining two counterbalanced validity studies from the WAIS-5 Technical Manual — 186 adolescents and adults aged 16 to 90, and 98 sixteen-year-olds — they found the same unexpected result in both: approximately **1.2 IQ points per decade**.

The stakes are not academic. The authors note that Flynn-effect adjustments feature in intellectual disability determinations under contemporary diagnostic guidance, and in capital cases where those determinations govern eligibility. A correction of three points where the true drift is 1.2 is an over-correction with consequences. They raise the question rather than resolving it, and the unresolved state is itself a limitation worth naming: the standard fix for an ageing norm is currently uncertain. The underlying mechanism is covered in [why the average IQ score is always 100](/blog/what-the-average-iq-score-means/).

## Predicting an outcome is not the same as improving a decision

The most under-appreciated limit is about use rather than measurement.

A 2026 framework paper in the *Journal of Intelligence* separates **incremental validity** — the predictive gain from adding a cognitive measure to an existing evidence base — from **decision utility**, whether using the score actually improves the decision being made. Cognitive measures relate moderately to strongly with academic outcomes, and often add predictive value at transition points. But that gain varies with range restriction, grading differences and unequal opportunity; where a target outcome is rare, false positives multiply; where places are capped, the operational meaning of a prediction changes; and a measure can improve average prediction while worsening outcomes for a subgroup.

The authors' conclusion is a discipline worth borrowing: testing should be conditional and sequential, used only where it can plausibly change what happens next. A score that cannot change a decision is not neutral — it consumes time, money and attention, and it confers unearned authority on whatever is done anyway.

## Why our app reports task performance and nothing else

[Mental Math & Memory Games](/apps/mental-math-memory-games/) is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and the limits above are the reason it makes no measurement claim at all.

It has no norm sample, no standardised administration, no technical manual, no published reliability evidence and no validity studies. Lacking every one of those, there is nothing it could honestly convert its numbers into. So it does not: there is no IQ estimate, no percentile, no classification, no brain-age figure and no assessment of any kind in the product.

What it reports is what it can defend — score, accuracy, streak, response time and personal bests for a run of Multiplication Rush, Mixed Operations, a memory round set or a Daily Challenge. Those are **practice** statistics. Keeping them clearly apart from **standardised psychological assessment** is not a disclaimer bolted on afterwards; it is the reason the numbers are trustworthy for what they do cover. ## What a careful reader takes away

A good test, used well, produces a bounded estimate of performance on a defined sample of tasks, with a known margin of error, against a comparison group of a known vintage. That is genuinely valuable and it is much less than the word "IQ" implies in ordinary conversation.
