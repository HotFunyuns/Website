Most sports career games ask you to be good at a sport. A quarterback career game asks you to be good at a job — reading what the defence is showing, deciding what to do about it before the play begins, and then deciding again once it does not go to plan.

[Football Career Sim 2026](/apps/football-career-sim/) is built around that narrow job. This guide covers what the archetype actually changes, how the drive-level model works, and the decisions that separate a career that stalls on a depth chart from one that ends with a starting job.

## Your archetype is a set of available options

The game asks you to set throwing hand, appearance, archetype and ratings including throw power, accuracy, awareness, speed, leadership and clutch ability. It is tempting to read that as a stat spread. It is more useful to read it as a menu.

When a pocket collapses, the simulator offers you a set of responses. Which responses appear, and how likely each is to work, depends on what you built:

| Archetype | Leans on | The escape it is offered | Fails when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pocket passer | Throw power, accuracy | Step up, reset, throw on time | Pressure arrives before the read is complete |
| Scrambling playmaker | Speed, clutch | Leave the pocket, extend, run | Second and third reads are needed under structure |
| Field general | Awareness, leadership | Pre-snap adjustment, get to the right play | Raw arm talent is genuinely required |
| Dual-threat | Speed plus accuracy | Both, but neither at the extreme | Development points are spread too thin |

The practical consequence: a scrambler who tries to win from the pocket is playing against their own build, and so is a pocket passer who runs on first pressure. Pick the archetype whose failure mode you can live with, then play to it.

## The drive is the unit that matters

Drives resolve with downs, distance, field position, turnovers, timeouts, red-zone situations, overtime and clock management. That combination is what makes the same throw a good or a bad idea.

Three states worth learning to recognise:

**Second-and-short in your own territory.** The cheapest down in football. An incompletion costs almost nothing, so this is where a shot down the field is priced correctly.

**Third-and-long, own ten, four minutes left, trailing.** Everything is expensive. A sack is field position and clock; an interception is the game. Here the correct answer is very often the boring one — take what is available, punt from a survivable spot.

**Red zone, first down, tied.** The field is compressed, so windows close faster and the value of a throwaway rises sharply. Protecting the down is worth more than protecting the ego.

The generic version of this rule: **the correct decision is a function of what a failure costs, not of what a success would be worth.** Career sims reward players who internalise that; so does the sport.

For the wider positional vocabulary — what everyone else on the field is doing while you make these choices — [football positions explained](/blog/football-positions-explained-for-drafting/) covers the roles and why they are valued differently.

## Winning the job is a trust problem, not a ratings problem

The career runs through youth football, high school, college opportunities, combine preparation and the professional draft. At each stage there is a depth chart, and the depth chart is not sorted purely by rating.

What actually moves you up:

- **Ball security over highlight throws.** A quarterback who avoids turnovers earns snaps faster than one who alternates between spectacular and catastrophic.
- **Consistency inside the structure.** Taking the available completion repeatedly reads, to the coaching model, as reliability.
- **Handling the surrounding pressure.** Training, the playbook, injuries, media attention and contracts all sit around the football, and neglecting them costs opportunities that no throw recovers.

The counter-intuitive part is that the fastest route to a starting job is usually the least exciting way to play. That is also true of the sport, which is part of why the model is satisfying.

## Where careers stall, and what to do about it

**You are accurate but never get a second contract.** You are probably producing volume without leverage — good numbers in low-stakes situations, poor numbers in the ones that decide games. Clutch ability and awareness are the attributes to feed.

**You keep losing the job after strong stretches.** Look at turnovers, not yards. A pattern of two-interception games erases a month of efficiency in almost any evaluation model, this one included.

**Your rushing production is high and your record is not.** Scrambling is a solution to a broken play, not a play call. If it is your first answer rather than your third, the offence is functioning without a passing game.

**You are stuck behind a starter.** Consider the move. Contracts, trades and free agency are in the game precisely so that a blocked path is a solvable problem rather than a dead career.

## What the simulation does and does not claim

Play outcomes are sampled from a model whose inputs are your ratings, the situation and the difficulty setting. It is not a prediction about any real player or team, and every league, team and player in it is fictional. Real-world rule frameworks — the [NCAA's published playing rules](https://www.ncaa.org/sports/2013/11/21/playing-rules.aspx) among them — govern the actual sport; the game borrows the *structure* of downs, distance and clock rather than any particular league's rulebook.

Two seasons played identically will not produce identical results, and that is the model working correctly rather than failing.
