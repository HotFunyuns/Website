When two reliable sources give different dates for the same event, the first move is not to decide which one is wrong. It is to work out **what each one is measuring**, because a date conflict is far more often a definition conflict than an error. Four causes account for nearly all of them: a calendar difference, an event with no single endpoint, a date that is really a range, and — least often — a mistake copied forward.

This matters practically. A learner who finds two dates and picks the one from the better-looking website has learned nothing and may well have picked the worse figure. A learner who works out *why* they differ usually ends up understanding the event better than the date alone would have taught them.

## Cause one: the two sources are using different calendars

This is the commonest cause for anything before the twentieth century, and it is entirely invisible unless you know to look for it.

The Julian calendar, introduced by Julius Caesar, added a leap day every four years, and as the U.S. Naval Observatory's [introduction to calendars](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/calendars) explains, that rule was slightly too generous, so "the date of the astronomical vernal equinox slowly drifted away from the date of 21 March" over the centuries. Pope Gregory XIII's 1582 reform fixed the drift by changing the leap rule: century years are no longer leap years unless divisible by 400, so 2000 was a leap year but 1700, 1800 and 1900 were not.

Countries did not switch at the same time. Royal Museums Greenwich records that when [Britain and its colonies adopted the Gregorian calendar](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/which-years-are-leap-years-can-you-have-leap-seconds) in 1752, "2 September was followed by 14 September" — eleven days simply removed — and Britain also "changed New Year's Day from 25 March to 1 January".

Two consequences follow, and both produce date conflicts.

**Different calendars in use at the same moment.** Because the Gregorian rule drops a leap day in most century years and the Julian rule does not, the gap between the two widens by roughly a day per century. An event in a country still using the Julian calendar therefore has two perfectly correct dates, and different books choose differently. Many careful sources print both, which is why you will see a date written with two values separated by a slash.

**The year starting on a different day.** Before 1752, an English document dated February 1750 sits in what we would now call 1751, because the year had not yet turned. Historians flag this with *Old Style* and *New Style*, or by writing the year as a pair. If you are reading English records from before the change and the sequence of events seems impossible by a few months, this is almost always why.

None of this is a reason to distrust either source. It is a reason to record which calendar you are using, every time.

## Cause two: the event has no single endpoint

Wars, reigns, revolutions and empires do not end the way a football match ends. They stop in stages, and a defensible end date depends on which stage you count.

Take a conflict that has a last major battle, a ceasefire, a surrender, a peace treaty ratified some years later, and a final withdrawal of occupying forces. Those are four or five different years, and a reference work that picks any one of them is not wrong — it has made an editorial decision. The same applies to start dates: a war that grew out of an earlier regional conflict can defensibly be dated from either.

This cause is easy to diagnose. If the two dates differ by a small number of years and both fall inside a period you would describe as "the end of" the event, you are looking at an endpoint definition, not an error. The fix is to write down which endpoint you mean.

## Cause three: the date is really a range

Archaeological and scientific dates come with confidence intervals. A radiocarbon result is a probability distribution, not a year, and a dendrochronological match may be precise to a single year while the event it dates — when the timber was actually used — is not. When a summary prints one year for a prehistoric event, it is usually giving you the midpoint of a range and discarding the error bars.

Two summaries can therefore print different single years while sitting comfortably inside the same published range. [How historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) covers what each dating method actually measures and why the answers move; the point here is only that a one-year figure for a scientifically dated event should be read as an abbreviation.

## Cause four: somebody copied a mistake

This is the least common cause and the one people reach for first. Errors do propagate — a typo in a widely used reference gets quoted, then quoted again, and after a while the wrong year has more citations than the right one.

The defence is corroboration, which the Digital Inquiry Group's [historical thinking chart](https://www.inquirygroup.org/history-lessons/historical-thinking-chart) treats as one of the four core historical reading skills alongside sourcing, contextualisation and close reading. Corroboration here means checking the date against a source that could not have copied the first one: an archive catalogue, a museum object record, a scholarly edition. The U.S. National Archives' [educator resources](https://www.archives.gov/education) exist to get readers to that layer, with document analysis materials and a catalogue of primary sources rather than summaries of them.

If three independent institutional sources agree and one popular site does not, you have your answer.

## A worked case from our own app

World History Timeline Sim is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and it supplies a useful teaching case precisely because we can be honest about it.

The app's underlying content was assembled in waves, and some subjects ended up with more than one record. A handful of those duplicates disagree with each other: the same war can appear twice with different end years, and several wars appear once with an end year and once without one. There is no citation attached to either record, because the app's content carries no sources and has had no external scholarly review.

The right response to finding that is not to pick the record that looks more confident. It is to notice that **a date with no stated basis cannot settle anything**, and to go and check. Run the diagnosis above: is the difference a calendar, an endpoint definition, a range, or an error? For a long medieval conflict fought across a frontier over centuries, "when did it end" is a genuine historiographical question with more than one defensible answer, and two different end years in two records is exactly the shape that question takes.

That is the honest use of the [app](/apps/world-history-timeline-sim/) as a study tool. It is good at showing you that something exists, roughly when it sat, and what it connects to. It is not a substitute for teachers, scholarly books, archives or primary sources, and no date in it should be quoted as evidence. Use it to find the question; use an archive or a peer-reviewed source to answer it.

## A five-minute procedure

When you hit a conflict, work down this list in order and stop at the first cause that fits.

1. **Check the era and region for a calendar change.** British and colonial records before 1752, anything from a state that adopted the Gregorian calendar late, and anything reckoned by a lunar or luni-solar calendar. If a calendar change is in play, record which calendar your date uses.
2. **Ask what event each date is dating.** Last battle, surrender, treaty, withdrawal, coronation, accession. Small differences here usually explain small differences in years.
3. **Check whether the date is scientific.** If it comes from radiocarbon, luminescence or a stratigraphic sequence, look for the published range rather than the headline year.
4. **Corroborate against something independent.** An archive, a museum record, a university department or a peer-reviewed article — not a second summary of the first summary.
5. **Write the date down with its basis.** "1752 (Gregorian adoption in Britain; 11 days dropped, per Royal Museums Greenwich)" is a note you can still use next year. "1752" is a note you will have to check again.

If you keep chronological notes as part of a wider routine — the approach in [how to study history on your own](/blog/how-to-study-history-on-your-own/) — add a basis field to every date you record. It costs one line and removes this entire category of problem from your future self's work.
