**A clean sheet is a match in which a team concedes no goals.** In American English the same thing is called a shutout. By convention it is credited to the goalkeeper, though it is produced by the whole team.

It is one of football's most quoted defensive statistics, and one of its least informative. Understanding why is a useful lesson in reading any binary statistic.

## The problem with a binary measure

A clean sheet asks one question — did you concede? — and throws away everything about *how much*.

Consider two seasons of 38 matches:

| Team | Clean sheets | Goals conceded | Story |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| A | 14 | 30 | Clean sheets or narrow concessions |
| B | 14 | 58 | Clean sheets, then heavy defeats |

Identical on the clean-sheet count, wildly different defences. The team that concedes four when it concedes at all is not defending as well as the one that concedes one.

Goal difference has the same input and handles it better, which is one reason it, rather than clean sheets, is used to order league tables — covered in [goal difference and league tiebreakers explained](/blog/goal-difference-and-tiebreakers/).

## Style inflates the number

The second problem is that clean sheets reward a particular way of playing, independent of quality.

A team that sits deep, keeps eleven players behind the ball and rarely attacks concedes fewer goals than its personnel would otherwise suggest. It also scores fewer, so it wins fewer matches — but its clean-sheet count looks excellent.

A team that attacks aggressively concedes more, because it commits players forward and leaves space behind. It also wins more.

So the statistic partly measures **how conservatively a team plays**, which is a tactical choice rather than a defensive standard. [Soccer formations explained](/blog/soccer-formations-explained/) covers how a shape trades territory for security, and clean sheets are one of the places where that trade shows up in the numbers.

## Opponent quality is uncontrolled

The third problem is the simplest: clean sheets are easier against poor attacking teams. A defence that keeps out weak opposition and concedes to strong opposition looks the same on this measure as one that does the reverse — which would be a much better defence.

Unless you know who a team played, a clean-sheet total tells you very little.

## What a goalkeeper's clean sheets measure

Mostly, the team in front of them.

A goalkeeper behind a strong defence faces fewer and easier chances, and records more clean sheets. A better goalkeeper behind a poor defence records fewer. The number attributed to the individual is largely generated by ten other people.

This is the same problem hockey has with its goaltending statistics, discussed in [hockey goaltending explained](/blog/hockey-goaltending-explained/) — and the same problem with plus/minus, ERA and every other statistic that attributes a team outcome to one participant.

## What to read instead

If you want to judge a defence, in a real competition or a simulated one:

1. **Goals conceded per match.** Continuous rather than binary, so it distinguishes narrow from heavy.
2. **Chances allowed.** Defending well and being lucky look identical on the scoreboard and different here.
3. **Distribution of results.** Ten 1–0 defeats and three 4–0 defeats are different problems.
4. **Opponent strength.** Without it, none of the above means much.
