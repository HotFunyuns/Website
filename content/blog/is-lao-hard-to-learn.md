Lao has one of the friendliest grammars an English speaker will ever meet and one of the least forgiving reading systems. That combination makes the usual difficulty question — "is it hard?" — nearly useless, because the answer depends entirely on which half you are asking about.

Speaking basic Lao: easier than you expect. Reading Lao at speed: harder, slower, and the place almost everyone stalls.

## The grammar is genuinely easy, and this is not flattery

Lao is an analytic language. The practical meaning of that:

- **Verbs do not conjugate.** No tense endings, no mood endings, no person agreement. The verb is the verb.
- **Nouns do not decline.** No case endings, no gender, no plural marking. A noun is a noun whether there is one of them or several.
- **There are no articles.** Nothing corresponding to *a* or *the*.
- **Word order is subject–verb–object** — the English order — and the subject is frequently dropped when context supplies it.

Tense is handled by time words and particles: a word for *yesterday*, a completion particle on the end, a progressive particle before the verb. Most Lao words are monosyllabic.

Compare that with the three grammatical genders and pervasive agreement of a language like Marathi, or with the case system of Russian, and it is obvious how much difficulty Lao has simply declined to have. A beginner can build correct sentences remarkably early, because there is very little to get wrong structurally.

## The script is where the work is

The Lao script is an abugida with 27 consonants, 7 consonantal ligatures, 33 vowels and 4 tone marks. Those numbers look manageable, and in isolation they are.

The complication is what the consonants are *for*. The 27 consonant letters cover only 21 distinct consonant sounds, and the surplus exists because the letters are divided into three tone classes — high, middle and low. Two letters may write the same sound and put a syllable into different tone categories.

So a Lao letter is carrying two pieces of information at once: a sound, and a contribution to the tone of the syllable it starts. Neither is optional, and the second is invisible to an English-trained reader who has only ever had to extract a sound from a letter.

Vowels add their own layer. They attach above, below, before and after the consonant, sometimes wrapping around it, and vowel length is contrastive — and feeds into the tone calculation.

## Tone is a consequence, not a separate subject

Here is the point that reorganises how you should study.

A Lao syllable's tone is determined by the class of its initial consonant, the tone mark if one is present, the vowel length, and the syllable type, all working together. The tone mark alone does not tell you the tone — the same mark produces different tones on different consonant classes.

That means **tone study and reading study are the same activity.** You cannot reliably produce the right tone on a written word until you can read the word, because reading it is how you compute the tone. Learning a tone chart in the abstract produces a fact you cannot apply.

The sequencing consequence is set out in full in [Lao tone rules](/blog/lao-tone-rules/): consonant class first, tone marks second, because the marks are meaningless without the classes.

Vientiane Lao, the basis of the standard written language, has six tones. Northern Lao, the speech of Luang Prabang, has five. That variation is worth knowing about early so that a chart which does not match what you are hearing does not make you doubt yourself.

## Where our learners actually stall

We can be precise about this, because the Lao course was reordered after it happened.

The sticking point is not the alphabet lesson. People finish the alphabet lesson. **The stall is in the gap between recognising a letter and reading a word without stopping.**

Those are very different skills and a course can easily conceal the difference. A learner who can name every character, correctly and confidently, and who has passed every recognition exercise, will then look at a four-letter word and need eight or nine seconds to produce it — because naming a letter is one lookup and reading a syllable is four: what sound, what class, what mark, what vowel length, then combine.

The original course treated recognition as the milestone. Learners passed it, felt ready, moved into the vocabulary units, and hit real words at a speed that made the vocabulary units feel impossible. The visible symptom was people abandoning the course in the *word* lessons, which looked like a vocabulary problem and was not.

What changed was the insertion of an explicit stage between the two. Reading drills now sit between character recognition and vocabulary: short real words, repeated, timed, with the whole class-mark-length computation exercised deliberately until it collapses into one step. The alphabet material is also broken into interactive practice rather than presented as a chart to memorise, for the same reason.

The honest limitation is that this stage is boring and it is not short. There is no version of it that is quick, and we cannot make decoding automatic on anyone's behalf — it is repetition, and the repetition has to happen. What we can do is put it in the right place, so that the difficulty arrives while a learner still expects the script to be the hard part, rather than three units later when they have been told the hard part is over.

## So: is it hard?

**Easier than expected:** grammar, sentence construction, the absence of inflection, early speaking.

**Harder than expected:** reading at speed, and everything downstream of it.

**Roughly as expected:** tones, pronunciation, and the general effort of a language with an unfamiliar sound system.

**Unusual:** the resource problem. Lao is a language very few apps cover at all, so a large part of the difficulty is not intrinsic — it is that there is not much to learn from.
