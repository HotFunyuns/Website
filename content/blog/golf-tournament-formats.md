Every golf format is a decision about one question: how much should a single bad hole be allowed to cost?

Stroke play answers "all of it." Stableford answers "a couple of points." A scramble answers "nothing at all." Once you see the formats as answers to that question, choosing between them stops being a matter of tradition and becomes a matter of who is playing and what the day is for.

## Individual formats

**Stroke play.** Every shot counts and there is no ceiling on the damage a hole can do. It is the format of most professional tournaments, and the reason avoiding disasters is a scoring strategy in its own right. [Golf scoring](/blog/golf-scoring-explained/) covers how a tournament total is assembled.

**Match play.** Scored hole by hole: win, lose or halve. A hole where everything falls apart costs exactly one hole, no more than losing by a single shot would. That cap makes aggression far cheaper than it is in stroke play, which is why match-play tactics diverge so sharply from stroke-play tactics.

**Stableford.** Points are awarded per hole based on strokes taken against a fixed score, usually par, adjusted for handicap — and the highest total wins, inverting golf's usual direction. It was developed by Frank Stableford to deter golfers from giving up after one or two bad holes, used informally in Wales from 1898 and first used in competition at Wallasey in 1932. It has a practical bonus: once a player has taken two strokes more than the adjusted fixed score, no points are possible, so they can pick up and move on, which speeds up play noticeably.

## Pairs formats

**Four-ball** (also called better-ball, and sometimes best ball). Each player plays their own ball and the better of the two scores counts for the pair on each hole. It can be played as match play or stroke play. Forgiving, sociable, and the format most amateur pairs events use.

**Foursomes**, or alternate shot. The pair has one ball between them and they alternate strokes until the hole is completed. It is the most demanding partnership format in golf: you are playing from wherever your partner left it, and a wayward drive is your problem to solve.

**Greensomes**, also called Scotch foursomes or modified alternate shot. Both players tee off, the pair picks which drive to use, and the player who did not hit it plays the second — then play alternates as in foursomes. It removes the harshest part of foursomes while keeping the shape.

**Gruesomes** (or bloodsomes) inverts the choice: the opposing team chooses which of your tee shots you must use, usually the worse one. It is played for entertainment rather than for competitive integrity, and it is very funny to everyone except the pair involved.

**Chapman**, also known as Pinehurst or American foursomes. Both players tee off, each then plays their partner's ball for the second shot, and after selecting one ball they alternate to the hole.

## Team formats

**Best ball.** Every team member plays their own ball throughout, and the lowest score on the team counts for each hole. Variants such as 1-2-3 Best Ball and Low Ball/High Ball change how many of the scores count.

**Scramble.** Every player tees off, the team picks the best shot, and everyone plays their next from within a clublength of it, repeating until the hole is done. The scramble is the default for charity and corporate days for an obvious reason: a beginner's bad shots are never played, so nobody spends the afternoon holding up the group. Common variants include **Ambrose** (net scoring with a team handicap), **Florida scramble** (the player whose ball was chosen sits out the next shot), and **Texas scramble** (a set number of each player's tee shots must be used, which prevents one long hitter from playing every drive).

**Patsome** rotates formats within a round: typically six holes of four-ball, six of greensomes and six of foursomes.

## Side games and betting formats

These sit on top of any of the above rather than replacing them.

**Nassau** is three bets in one: best score on the front nine, best on the back nine, and best over the full eighteen. It is probably the most common wager among golfers.

**Skins** treats each hole as its own contest. The best outright score wins the skin; if the hole is tied by any of the competitors, the skin carries to the next hole and is then worth two. That carry-over is what makes it dramatic — a run of halved holes turns an ordinary par into a large prize.

## A short chooser

| If the day is for… | Use |
|---|---|
| Deciding who is genuinely best | Stroke play |
| A head-to-head with room for aggression | Match play |
| An individual competition with mixed abilities | Stableford |
| A charity or corporate day | Scramble |
| A sociable pairs competition | Four-ball |
| Testing a partnership properly | Foursomes |
| Keeping a group interested to the last hole | Skins |

## The formats we implemented, and the ones we dropped

[Golf Career Simulator Pro Tour](/apps/golf-career-simulator/) implements one format: multi-round stroke play, with cuts, leaderboards, ties and playoffs.

That is a narrower answer than the list above, and the narrowing was deliberate.

We built match play as a prototype and cut it. The problem was not the format, which is excellent — it was that match play does not accumulate into a career the way stroke play does. A career in the game runs on finishes converting into rankings, earnings and reputation, and those in turn deciding which events are open to you next season. Stroke play produces a finishing position for every player in the field, every week, which is exactly the input that machine needs. Match play produces a bracket, and a bracket produces a meaningful result for two players and a shrug for the rest.

Stableford had a different problem. It works beautifully as a way of scoring a round, and it substantially reduces the consequence of a disaster — which is the very thing that makes the game's risk decisions matter. The moment a triple bogey costs a couple of points rather than three shots, choosing the aggressive line stops being a real decision.

So the honest position is: **stroke play with a cut was the only format that held a career narrative together**, and we shipped that rather than offering five formats where four were decorative. If you want variety of format, the game does not provide it. If you want the consequences of a single format applied consistently across seasons, it does.

The cut is doing a lot of the work there — it is what makes the first two rounds about survival rather than scoring. [How a golf cut works](/blog/how-a-golf-cut-works/) covers the mechanism and what missing one actually costs.
