Reversing a sequence is harder than repeating it because you are doing two jobs with one set of resources: holding the digits, and rearranging them before you answer. Türüdü and colleagues put the distinction plainly in a 2026 study in *iScience* — the forward task "primarily measures the storage capacity of the phonological loop", while the backward task "requires individuals to recall the sequence in reverse order, which engages the central executive for active manipulation of information." The gap between your two spans is not a defect. It is the task working as designed.

## The design already assumes the gap

You can see the assumption baked into the standard clinical procedure. In their normative study, Choi and colleagues describe presenting digits at one per second, beginning **at a length of three for forward recall and a length of two for backward recall**, with two trials at each length and a halt when both trials at a length fail.

That one-digit head start is an admission. The people who built the instrument did not expect the two directions to be equivalent, so they did not start them in the same place. If you have been quietly disappointed that your backward runs come up short, the answer is that everyone's do.

What nobody can honestly give you is a fixed size for the difference. It moves with age, with strategy, with how long the sequence is, and — as the same *iScience* work found — with how the task is delivered. A gamified online version produced significantly higher scores than both onsite and standard online versions, and crucially the boost was identical for forward and backward tasks. A friendlier interface lifted both numbers without touching whatever sits underneath.

## Storage and manipulation are different demands

The useful way to think about this is not "backward is harder" but "backward adds a transformation". Holding `4 9 1 7` is a storage problem. Producing `7 1 9 4` requires you to keep the original available *while* you walk it in the other direction, and the walking itself consumes the resource the holding depends on.

Nelson Cowan's review of memory terminology is helpful here, because it explains why the field keeps circling this distinction. He describes working memory as having been "conceived and defined in three different, slightly discrepant ways: as short-term memory applied to cognitive tasks, as a multi-component system that holds and manipulates information in short-term memory, and as the use of attention to manage short-term memory." Backward recall sits squarely in the second and third of those. Forward recall does not have to.

## Strategies that move a backward span

Because the difficulty is transformation rather than storage alone, the strategies that help are the ones that reduce how much you have to transform.

**Reverse groups, not digits.** Take `6 2 8 3 5 1`. Held as six items and reversed one at a time, you will lose the middle. Held as `62 83 51`, it is three units. Reverse the units to `51 83 62`, then reverse inside each to get `15 38 26`. Read out, that is `1 5 3 8 2 6` — which is the original read right to left. You performed two small reversals instead of one six-step one.

**Encode positions, not just values.** People who do well often report anchoring the first and last items immediately, because those are the two that the reversal makes most important. The interior can then be rebuilt around fixed endpoints.

**Say it once, then stop saying it.** Rehearsing forward while trying to output backward is the most common self-inflicted failure. One clean pass to encode, then switch modes.

These are genuine techniques, and they will improve your score. They are also the reason to be careful about what the improved score means. Norris, Hall and Gathercole, testing extensive digit span training directly, concluded that such training "does not substantially improve the capacity of verbal STM", with little evidence of fundamental capacity change and minimal transfer to related tasks such as letter span. Better strategy on a practised task is a real and useful thing. It is not the same as a bigger store, and we do not claim it is.

## The version that removes your strategy

There is a third format worth knowing about, and Mental Math & Memory Games — our own app, built by Reign Creative — ships it: a random-direction drill where you see the digits and only find out afterwards whether to type them forward or backward.

This is harder than either fixed direction, and the reason is instructive. In a fixed backward task you can choose an encoding that suits reversal from the first digit. In a random-direction task you cannot commit, so you either encode twice or encode neutrally and pay the transformation cost at the end. It is a neat demonstration that a large part of span performance is preparation rather than raw holding.

Alongside it the app ships a plain reverse-sequence mode and a backward-span ladder that mirrors the forward one, and it stores **your best forward span and your best backward span separately** rather than collapsing them into a single figure. That separation is the right design for practice, because the two numbers move for different reasons.

## What these numbers are, and what they are not

They are game statistics. They were produced on your own device, at a time you chose, with no examiner, no controlled presentation and no reference population. They are good for one thing: comparing this week's runs to last week's on the same mode at the same difficulty level.

They are not a measurement of your working memory, not a cognitive assessment, and not comparable to anything a qualified professional would produce with a standardised instrument. If a real question about memory or attention is troubling you, that is a conversation for a professional, not for a phone game.

Used within those limits, backward drills are an unusually clear window into your own processing: you can watch the point at which adding a transformation breaks a sequence you could otherwise hold.
