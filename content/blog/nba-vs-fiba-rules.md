NBA and FIBA basketball share a sport, a 24-second shot clock and a two-step travelling rule, but they differ in five ways that change how teams actually play: an NBA game lasts 48 minutes against FIBA's 40, NBA players foul out on their sixth foul rather than their fifth, the NBA's three-point arc is about half a metre deeper, FIBA lets players play the ball once it has touched the ring, and only the NBA has a defensive three-second rule.

The comparison below uses the NBA's Official Rules 2025-26, the newest edition on the league's rulebook page when we checked on 2 October 2026, and FIBA's Official Basketball Rules 2026, in force since 1 October 2026. Rule numbers are given so you can check each point at its source. Metric conversions of NBA distances are our own arithmetic. College basketball is outside the scope of this comparison.

## The differences at a glance

| Rule | NBA | FIBA 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Playing time | 4 × 12 min (Rule 5, II) | 4 × 10 min (Art. 8.1) |
| Court | 94 × 50 ft, about 28.65 × 15.24 m (Rule 1, court diagram) | 28 × 15 m (Art. 2.1) |
| Three-point arc | 23 ft 9 in, about 7.24 m (Rule 1, I(d)) | 6.75 m (Art. 2.5.4) |
| Three-point corners | 22 ft, about 6.71 m | Lines 0.90 m from the sideline, about 6.60 m from the basket |
| Area where help defenders cannot draw a charge | 4 ft arc, about 1.22 m (Rule 1, I(i); Comments on the Rules, II(C)) | 1.30 m semi-circle (Art. 2.5.7, 33.10) |
| Foul limit | 6 personal fouls (Rule 3, I(a)) | 5 fouls (Art. 41.1) |
| Defensive three seconds | Yes (Rule 10, VII) | No |
| Goaltending once the ball touches the ring | Still restricted inside the cylinder (Rule 11) | Restrictions end (Art. 31.2.3) |

| Coach's challenge | One; a second if the first succeeds (Rule 14) | One per game where replay is used (Appendix F.4) |

## Differences that change tactics

### Forty-eight minutes against forty

The NBA plays 20% more basketball per game. In player-minutes, five players on the court for 48 minutes gives an NBA team 240 to share out, while FIBA's 40 minutes gives 200. A FIBA coach has less time to rest a starter without losing the game, and every possession is a larger share of the whole.

### Five fouls instead of six

Per minute, the two limits are the same: six fouls in 48 minutes and five in 40 both work out to one every eight minutes. The differences are in the slack and in what counts. Two fouls in the first quarter leave an NBA player able to commit three more over the remaining 36 minutes without fouling out, about one every 12 minutes; a FIBA player in the same spot can commit two more over 30 minutes, one every 15. FIBA's count also includes technical, disruptive and flagrant fouls committed as a player, because its rules charge a player's technical foul "as a player foul". An early burst of fouls, or a technical for dissent, therefore costs more of a player's game under FIBA rules.

### The three-point geometry

At the top of the arc the NBA line is about 0.49 m further out than FIBA's. In the corners the two books nearly agree, at about 6.71 m against 6.60 m. That makes the corner three a distinctive bargain in the NBA, roughly 0.53 m shorter than a three from the top, whereas under FIBA rules the corner saves a shooter only about 0.15 m. Spacing plans built around corner shooters translate imperfectly between the two games for exactly that reason.

### No defensive three seconds

The NBA requires any defender standing in the 16-foot lane to be actively guarding an opponent, meaning within arm's length and in a guarding position, within three seconds; a violation is a technical foul. FIBA has no such rule, since its Article 26 restricts only attacking players. Nothing in FIBA's rules stops a team leaving its centre under the basket for an entire possession, which is exactly what the NBA rule rules out, so zone defences that pack the paint face one less constraint internationally.

### Playing the ball off the rim

The NBA treats the space above the ring as protected: touching a ball that is sitting or rolling on the ring, or any ball above the ring inside the imaginary cylinder, is basket interference. FIBA's goaltending restrictions end once the ball has touched the ring. After that, players may tap or bat the ball, although touching the basket or backboard while the ball is in contact with the ring is still basket interference. Tip-ins and defensive swats off the rim that would be violations in an NBA game are legal in a FIBA one.

## Differences that change the feel of a game

### Who can stop the clock

NBA teams have seven timeouts in regulation, and a player on the court can request one. Under FIBA rules only the head coach or first assistant coach can request a timeout, and teams get two in the first half, three in the second and one in each overtime. A trapped FIBA ball-handler cannot escape by calling time; an NBA ball-handler can, if the team has one left.

Both books give a team in its own backcourt a late-game option after a timeout. In the NBA it may inbound from the 28-foot hash mark in the frontcourt during the last two minutes. FIBA's version lets the head coach choose a frontcourt throw-in from the throw-in line when two minutes or less remain in the fourth quarter or overtime.

### Held balls

When two opponents both hold the ball, the NBA restarts with a jump ball between them at the nearest circle. FIBA uses the opening tip only once; after that, every jump-ball situation is resolved by the alternating-possession arrow. Under FIBA rules a defensive tie-up is only worth as much as the arrow says it is.

### Challenges

Both books let a head coach ask for a replay review, on different terms. An NBA team has one challenge per game and earns a second if the first succeeds. It can only challenge a personal foul called on its own team, an out-of-bounds call that went against it, or a goaltending or basket-interference call (except in the last two minutes, when only the officials can trigger that review), and it normally has to call a timeout to do it. A FIBA head coach gets one challenge per game in competitions that use instant replay, whether or not it succeeds, and may use it at any time on the situations FIBA's replay appendix lists.

## Serious fouls: the 2026 split

FIBA's 2026 rules replaced the unsportsmanlike foul with two new types. The **disruptive foul** covers tactical contact without a legitimate attempt to play the ball: stopping a fast break, stopping the clock at the end of a quarter, or hitting an attacker from behind or the side on a clear run to the basket. The **flagrant foul** covers contact that is not a legitimate play, reckless or dangerous contact, and excessive hard contact. Both cost free throws plus a frontcourt throw-in, but only the flagrant foul counts toward disqualification. FIBA also split technical fouls into a behavioural category 1, which counts toward disqualification, and an administrative category 2, which does not.

The NBA reaches similar goals by different routes. A transition take foul costs one free throw plus possession, a clear-path foul costs two free throws plus possession, and its Flagrant 1 and Flagrant 2 grades both cost two free throws and the ball, with a Flagrant 2 also meaning ejection. On the cheapest version of the play, stopping a break with a take foul in the NBA costs one free throw, while the FIBA disruptive foul on a non-shooter costs two.

Flopping shows the philosophical gap most clearly. The NBA treats a flop as a violation penalised by a technical foul that does not count as unsportsmanlike. FIBA lists faking a foul as a category 1 technical foul, and two of those disqualify a player.

FIBA's 2026 edition also redefined when a player is "in the act of shooting". It now separates drives to the basket from jump shots, and says a player must be in the frontcourt to be shooting, except for a last attempt as the game clock or shot clock expires. FIBA's change summary says the aim is to stop players "manipulating situations to create shooting fouls".

## Where the two books agree

The common ground is as useful to know as the differences:

- **Shot clock.** 24 seconds, reset to 14 when the attacking team recovers its own shot after it touches the ring (NBA Rule 7, IV; FIBA Art. 29).
- **Travelling.** Two steps after gathering the ball, with the first step counted from the first foot, or both feet, touching the floor after the gather (NBA Rule 10, XIII; FIBA Art. 25).
- **Eight seconds** to bring the ball into the frontcourt, and no returning it to the backcourt afterwards (NBA Rule 10, VIII-IX; FIBA Art. 28 and 30).
- **The penalty.** Four team fouls a quarter, with two free throws from the fifth onward (NBA Rule 12B, V; FIBA Art. 42), although the NBA adds a last-two-minutes allowance and resets the count for overtime.
- **The lane.** The NBA's 16-foot lane is about 4.88 m wide and FIBA's restricted area is 4.90 m wide.

## What it means if you follow both

A team built for the NBA's rules, with a shot-blocker who can only defend near the basket and shooters who live in the corners, is solving a slightly different problem from a FIBA team, where the same shot-blocker can stand in the lane all possession and the corners are barely closer than the top of the arc.
