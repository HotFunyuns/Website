Six slots. Centre, left wing, right wing, two defencemen and a goaltender — that is the whole lineup a hockey draft asks you to fill, and it is the smallest set of decisions in any of the major drafting sports.

Small does not mean easy. With six slots, every one of them is load-bearing, and there is nowhere to hide a mistake.

## The labels describe where you start, not where you are allowed

Before going through the positions individually, one correction that saves a lot of confusion: hockey positions are not zones of responsibility in the way that soccer positions can be. Everybody defends and everybody attacks, and the play moves too fast for anything else to be possible.

What a position actually tells you is where a player lines up for a faceoff, which part of the ice they are primarily accountable for, and what the coach expects them to do first when possession changes hands. That last one is the useful definition.

## Centre

The most demanding forward slot, and it is not close.

A centre operates through the middle of the ice, which is the most congested area available and the one where the least time exists to make a decision. Three responsibilities define the position:

**Faceoffs.** Every stoppage restarts with one, and the centre takes it. Winning a draw in the attacking end hands your line a possession in a dangerous place; losing one in your own end hands the other side the same gift. It is a small, repeated advantage that accumulates over a game.

**The middle lane.** Centres carry the puck through the neutral zone and support both wingers, which means covering more ice than anyone else on the line. Vision matters here more than raw skill, because the centre is usually the player with the widest view of what is developing.

**First forward back.** When possession turns over, the centre is typically the forward responsible for the area in front of their own net — the highest-danger real estate on the ice. A centre who does not do this reliably creates a defensive problem that no amount of scoring compensates for.

That combination is why "two-way centre" is close to the highest compliment available in the sport, and why a centre who is excellent at only the offensive half of the job is a genuinely awkward player to build around.

## Left wing and right wing

Wingers work the outer lanes and, in most systems, live along the boards.

Their attacking job is straightforward to describe and hard to do: get the puck out of the corner, drive the net, and be in a position to finish. Board battles are the substance of the role, and a winger who loses them consistently makes their line's possession numbers collapse whatever their shot is like.

Their defensive job is the part casual viewers never watch. In most defensive structures the wingers are responsible for the opposition's defencemen at the blue line — the players receiving the puck at the point and shooting from distance. A winger who drifts off that assignment gives the other side free shooting lanes, and the goal that eventually results will be blamed on somebody else entirely.

### Types of winger

- **Power winger** — wins the boards, gets to the front of the net, hard to move. Generates chances by force.
- **Skill winger** — creates separation, finds space, finishes. Generates chances by geometry.
- **Checking winger** — reliable without the puck, wins the defensive assignment, kills penalties.

None of these is the correct answer. A line with three of the same kind is the wrong answer, which is the argument made at greater length in the [line combinations guide](/blog/hockey-line-combinations-explained/).

### Handedness and the off wing

A left-handed shot playing right wing takes passes on their forehand as they cut toward the middle and shoots from an angle a right-handed shot would not. Coaches use this deliberately. It is not better or worse in the abstract — it is a different set of shot locations and passing lanes, and it is one reason two wingers with similar ratings can produce very different results in the same slot.

## Defence

Two slots, and the job runs in both directions.

**Going backwards: gap control.** The core defensive skill is managing the distance between yourself and the attacker carrying the puck. Too much space and they can shoot or pass through you; too little and they can skate around you. Everything else a defenceman does defensively — angling attackers to the outside, clearing the front of the net, blocking shots — sits downstream of getting that distance right.

**Going forwards: the first pass.** The fastest way out of your own end is a clean, early pass to a forward. A defenceman who makes that pass turns defence into attack in one motion. A defenceman who cannot has to skate the puck out under pressure or fire it away and concede possession, and the difference between those two options across a whole game is enormous.

Beyond that, defencemen hold the offensive blue line, decide when to pinch in to keep a puck alive (the classic high-risk, high-reward hockey decision), and shoot from the point.

### The types, and how a pair fits together

A **stay-at-home** defenceman prioritises denial: position, gap, net front, safe clears. An **offensive** defenceman activates into the attack and accepts more risk to generate chances. A **two-way** defenceman does an acceptable amount of both.

Pairs generally work better when the two players complement each other, because both defencemen joining the attack at once is how odd-man rushes happen going the other way. It is the same logic as lines: a pair is a unit, and the question is fit rather than ranking.

## Goaltender

The only position in the sport where one player can decide a result on their own, in either direction.

What separates goaltenders is less about spectacular saves than about three quieter things:

**Positioning.** A goaltender who is in the right place makes hard shots look routine. Most of what looks like reflex is actually pre-positioning that removed the need for a reflex.

**Rebound control.** Stopping the puck is half the job; putting it somewhere safe is the other half. A save that drops into the slot is barely a save.

**Puck-handling.** A goaltender comfortable playing the puck behind the net relieves pressure on defencemen chasing dump-ins, which quietly improves the whole defensive group.

Goaltending is also the most volatile position to evaluate, because the workload varies so much with the team in front. That volatility is precisely why it is a bad place to economise: the downside of a poor goaltender is not a slightly worse result, it is losing games your skaters won.

## The coach is a roster slot too

[84-0 Hockey Draft & GM Manager](/apps/hockey-draft-gm-manager/) asks you to pick a coach alongside the six players, and it is not decoration — the game's projection weighs coaching along with position balance, defence and goaltending.

That is a fair representation of the sport. Coaching in hockey is largely about deployment: who plays against whom, who starts in which zone, and who is on the ice with a one-goal lead and two minutes left. Those decisions are made with the roster you handed over, and they are the reason two identical lineups can produce different outcomes.

## Judging each slot at a draft board

| Slot | Ask first | The failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Centre | Can they do the defensive half of the job? | An offensive centre who leaves the slot uncovered |
| Wings | Do they win the boards and hold the point assignment? | Three finishers who all want the same space |
| Defence | Gap control going back, first pass going forward | Two of the same type paired together |
| Goaltender | Positioning and rebound control before highlight saves | Treating it as the leftover slot |
| Coach | Does the deployment style match the roster you built? | Ignoring it entirely |

The two draft rulesets test different halves of this. Unlimited Draft lets you keep comparing until the combination fits, which rewards knowing what you are looking for. Limited Draft gives you one pass with zero refreshes, which rewards deciding what each slot must provide before you see who is available. ## What the simulator is actually doing

The projected 84-game record that comes back at the end of a draft is the game's own verdict on the lineup, produced by its internal rules acting on players it generated.

Worth stating explicitly: nothing in that result describes, forecasts or relates to any real league, team or athlete, and none of it is a prediction or guidance of any kind. It is a score on a roster puzzle. The perfect record the game is named for is deliberately hard to reach, so most lineups finish short — and the size of the shortfall is the feedback the exercise exists to produce.

The way to use it is comparative: change one slot, run it again, and find out whether the model agrees with your reasoning about what that slot was worth.
