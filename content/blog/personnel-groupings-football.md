A personnel grouping is the mix of running backs, tight ends and wide receivers an offence sends onto the field for a play. Written out in full it reads like "1 RB, 1 TE, 3 WR", and the familiar shorthand compresses that to two digits: running backs first, tight ends second. So 11 personnel is one back, one tight end and three receivers; 12 is one back, two tight ends and two receivers; 21 is two backs, one tight end and two receivers.

Two digits are enough because the third number is implied. An offence has eleven players, and the rulebook requires at least seven on the line at the snap, with eligible receivers on both ends and ineligible players between them. In the setup the league diagrams for beginners, five of the eleven are linemen and one is the quarterback, which leaves five places for backs, tight ends and receivers. Receivers are five minus the first two digits.

## Decoding the numbers

| Shorthand | Written out | Backs | Tight ends | Receivers | What the defence has to respect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | 1 RB, 0 TE, 4 WR | 1 | 0 | 4 | Four receivers stretched across the width |
| 11 | 1 RB, 1 TE, 3 WR | 1 | 1 | 3 | Three receivers plus a tight end who can block or release |
| 12 | 1 RB, 2 TE, 2 WR | 1 | 2 | 2 | Two players who can be extra blockers or receivers |
| 13 | 1 RB, 3 TE, 1 WR | 1 | 3 | 1 | Size at the line of scrimmage |
| 20 | 2 RB, 0 TE, 3 WR | 2 | 0 | 3 | Two backfield threats with three receivers wide |
| 21 | 2 RB, 1 TE, 2 WR | 2 | 1 | 2 | A lead blocker in the backfield |
| 22 | 2 RB, 2 TE, 1 WR | 2 | 2 | 1 | A heavy running look with two backs |

The positions behind the numbers come from the league's glossary. The running back is typically the primary ball carrier. The fullback is typically the larger of two backs and often blocks for him. A tight end lines up at the end of the line outside the tackle and either blocks or goes out as a receiver. Wide receivers line up near the sidelines and run downfield. The glossary also describes an H-back, who does a tight end's job from a few yards behind the line, which is one reason a two-digit label can hide how a player is actually used.

## The "standard formation" and how far offences move from it

The league's glossary still describes a "standard formation": two backs behind the quarterback, one tight end and two wide receivers. In the shorthand that is 21 personnel, and it is exactly the offence the league's beginner diagram shows. The same glossary entries also record the drift away from it: a standard formation uses one tight end, "though some offenses call for two", and calls for two wide receivers, "but some offenses use three or four at a time".

Read the groupings as a family of trades against that baseline:

- **Swap the fullback for a receiver** and 21 becomes 11: more width, one fewer lead blocker.
- **Swap a receiver for a tight end** and 11 becomes 12: the same single back, but an extra player who can block at the line or release.
- **Swap the tight end for a fourth receiver** and 11 becomes 10: maximum width, minimum help in protection.
- **Add bodies near the line** (13 or 22) when the job is short yardage or running out the clock.

The labels count roster positions, not alignments. A tight end flexed out wide still counts as a tight end, which is why the same two digits can produce very different-looking formations.

Sixth-lineman snaps are worth a note. With six linemen there are only four skill players, so a snap written out as "6 OL, 1 RB, 2 TE, 1 WR" has no clean two-digit name. The rulebook requires players in eligible positions to wear eligible numbers or report to the referee, which is how a lineman can line up where a tight end normally would.

## How defences answer

Personnel is a message to the defence, and defences answer it mostly with defensive backs. The league's explainer describes a nickel package that replaces a linebacker or defensive lineman with an extra defensive back when the offence uses additional wide receivers. The glossary puts it the other way round: a fifth defensive back comes on to cover receivers, usually at the expense of a linebacker.

The matching runs in both directions, and it is the logic behind the groupings: each one is partly a way of choosing which defensive players will be on the field.

| Offence shows | What it invites | What the offence hopes to exploit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 or 11 (three or four receivers) | A nickel package, or more defensive backs | Running the ball against a lighter box |
| 12 or 21 (two tight ends, or a fullback) | A base defence with its linebackers kept on | Throwing against linebackers in coverage |
| 13 or 22 (heavy) | More size near the line | Play-action and leaks against a defence loaded to stop the run |

An offence that can run the ball well out of three-receiver sets punishes a defence that answers with an extra defensive back; one that can throw well out of two-tight-end sets punishes a defence that stays big. The grouping is the opening move, and the play call is the follow-up.

## Why personnel is a roster question

Seen from a front office, personnel decides which backups are really starters.

- **The third receiver is a starter in an 11-heavy offence.** If three-receiver sets are more than half the snaps, the player listed third on the depth chart plays more than half the snaps.
- **The second tight end is a starter in a 12-heavy offence.** He is on the field whenever the offence wants the extra blocker or the extra receiving threat.
- **Versatile players save roster spots.** A tight end who can line up in the slot, or a back who can split wide, lets one grouping look like another without a substitution.
- **The nickel back is close to a starter on defence.** If extra receivers bring on a fifth defensive back, an opponent that leans on three-receiver sets will see him on most of its snaps.
