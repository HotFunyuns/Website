In modern circuit racing, the car that is fastest over one lap does not always win. Tyres wear, lose grip and have to be changed, and when and how a team does that is **tyre strategy**. This guide uses Formula 1's 2026 rules, in Article B6 of the FIA's sporting regulations, and Wikipedia's history of F1 tyres.

## The basic trade-off

Wikipedia states the principle simply: a harder tyre is more durable but gives less grip; a softer tyre gives more grip but is less durable. Everything else in tyre strategy follows from that.

| Tyre | Sidewall colour | Grip | Durability | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Soft | Red | Highest | Lowest | Qualifying, short stints |
| Medium | Yellow | Middle | Middle | Flexible race stints |
| Hard | White | Lowest dry grip | Highest | Long stints |
| Intermediate | Green | Treaded, for wet conditions | – | Damp track, light standing water |
| Full wet | Blue | Treaded, for wet conditions | – | Heavy standing water |

The colours, as Wikipedia explains, are relative: the supplier brings three dry compounds to each event, and whichever is softest that weekend is the "soft".

## The rules that shape strategy

The 2026 regulations set out what each team works with:

- **What is supplied.** At each event, three dry-weather specifications, one intermediate and one wet, each visibly marked; at some events, an extra specification may be made available.
- **What is allocated.** The FIA's technical delegate allocates sets to each driver: at a standard event, 2 sets of the hard, 3 of the medium and 8 of the soft, plus 5 sets of intermediates and 2 of full wets. That is **13 sets of dry tyres** at a standard event; at a sprint (alternative format) event the split is 2 hard, 4 medium and 6 soft, 12 in all. Once a set has left the pit lane, it counts as used, and fixed numbers of sets must be handed back after each practice session.
- **The two-compound rule.** Unless a driver uses intermediate or wet tyres in the race, they **must use at least two different dry specifications**, and at least one must be a mandatory race specification nominated two weeks before the event.

The two-compound rule is why every dry race includes at least one pit stop. A team that ran a single set to the flag would break it.

## One stop or two?

The decision is a sum. A pit stop costs the time to drive through the pit lane at its speed limit and to change tyres; fresh tyres then give back time on every lap until they too wear out.

**A simplified example.** Suppose a pit stop costs 22 seconds, and fresh tyres are worth an average of 0.5 seconds a lap over the next stint compared with nursing old ones.

| Plan | Extra stop costs | Time gained on fresh tyres | Net |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Two-stop instead of one-stop, fresh tyres for the last 30 laps | 22 s | 30 × 0.5 = 15 s | 7 s slower |
| Same, but fresh tyres are worth 0.9 s a lap | 22 s | 30 × 0.9 = 27 s | 5 s faster |

The numbers are illustrative, but the logic is real: the more tyres degrade, the more an extra stop pays. Traffic changes the sum, because new tyres are worth nothing stuck behind a slower car, and so do neutralisations, which make a stop cheaper; [the safety car explained](/blog/safety-car-explained/) covers why.

## When to stop: undercut and overcut

Timing a stop against a rival is its own art. Stopping first and using fresh-tyre pace to jump ahead is the undercut; staying out and gaining on old tyres while the rival is stuck behind traffic is the overcut. [Undercut versus overcut](/blog/undercut-vs-overcut/) works through both.

## Qualifying shapes the race

Tyres are shared across the weekend. Every set used in practice or qualifying is one fewer fresh set for the race, and Wikipedia notes that at points in F1's history a set of the softest compound has been held back specifically for the top-ten shootout. Teams therefore plan qualifying runs with the race in mind; [how racing qualifying works](/blog/how-racing-qualifying-works/) explains the session.

## Rain resets everything

When the track is wet, the two-compound rule falls away and the question becomes when to switch between dry, intermediate and full wet tyres. [Wet-weather racing](/blog/wet-weather-racing/) covers the crossover points and the procedures for rain.
