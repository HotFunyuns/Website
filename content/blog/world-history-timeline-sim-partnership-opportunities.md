The Google Play listing says [World History Timeline Sim](/apps/world-history-timeline-sim/) is made for curious learners who want history to feel more interactive, visual and memorable, and this page is for **museums, schools and educational publishers** wondering whether working with it could serve their audiences. It covers the app's content, the forms collaboration could take, the cautions that come with alternate history, and the route to a licence or sale.

## Two thousand events and two ways to explore them

The Google Play listing describes a library of **more than 2,000 major events**, from ancient civilizations and medieval empires through revolutions, world wars, scientific discoveries and social movements. Players browse **timelines by year, era, region, country or category**, or use a **world history map** showing where events happened.

The second way in is play. **Time Travel Mode** sets out a turning point with its historical context and **what actually happened**, offers decision options and their tradeoffs, then plays out consequences with a **Butterfly Effect Score**; the listing counts **over 2,000 what-if scenarios**. Around it sit **Civilization Survival Mode**, **Timeline Detective**, boss battles, a what-if tournament and a **museum of unlockable artifacts**, plus quizzes, flashcards, crosswords, timeline ordering and map challenges. The app is free with ads and optional in-app purchases.

## Museum collaborations

A museum's collection and an app's artifact museum invite an obvious pairing. Possible starting points:

- **Exhibition tie-ins**: a themed set of events or what-if scenarios released around a temporary exhibition.
- **Gallery prompts**: quiz or timeline-ordering challenges that send visitors back to the objects.
- **Sponsorship or cross-promotion** in the app, pointing players to a real collection.

Any museum content would need the museum's own curatorial sign-off.

## Classroom and homeschool use

The listing names **students, teachers and homeschoolers** among its audiences, and the app's page on this site describes it as **a companion to coursework, not a replacement**. That framing suits partnerships that add structure around the app rather than claims to replace teaching:

- **teacher guides** that pair timeline browsing with primary-source work;
- **discussion prompts** built on Time Travel Mode, asking pupils to explain why the real outcome happened;
- **revision routines** using the quizzes, flashcards and map challenges.

## A worked example: a six-week pilot

Picture a secondary school pairing the app with a unit on revolutions. In week one, pupils use the timeline to order five revolutions by year and region. In weeks two to four, each group plays one Time Travel Mode scenario, records the real outcome the app describes, and then checks it against a textbook or a primary source. In week five, groups present why the historical choice was made, using the simulation only as a contrast. Week six is revision with the quizzes and map challenges. A partner could turn that outline into a teacher guide; the evidence for every claim would still come from the sources, not the game.

## Being straight about alternate history

The strongest caution comes from the app's page on this site: **Time Travel Mode is alternate history, and its chains of consequences are simulations, not what happened**. In a classroom that is a feature if pupils are asked to argue about plausibility, and a risk if a simulation is mistaken for fact. Partners would need to keep that distinction visible in anything they publish.

Acquisition of **this app, several apps or the portfolio** is also open for serious discussion. No prices, valuations, revenue or user numbers appear on this page, and it is not an offer.

## If the app changed owner

Ownership on Google Play moves only through Google's **formal developer-account transfer**. Google's help page explains that the original and receiving accounts both have to be **registered and active**, and the request quotes each account's **registration transaction ID**. Integrated services, such as Firebase, Google Analytics or Google Play game services, need their account settings updated, and **ads in the app must have their SDK integrations changed** so that ad traffic is credited correctly. Test groups cannot be carried across. Ratings, reviews, users and the store listing do move, so the app keeps its public history.
