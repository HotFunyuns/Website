**A primary source is evidence created at the time by someone with direct involvement. A secondary source interprets that evidence afterwards.** Simple enough — and the useful part is what the definition does not say.

## The categories

| Type | What it is | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Created at the time, by a participant or observer | Letters, diaries, official records, photographs, artefacts, treaties, contemporaneous reporting |
| Secondary | Interprets primary evidence afterwards | History books, journal articles, documentaries, biographies |
| Tertiary | Summarises secondary work | Encyclopaedias, general reference works, overview textbooks |

Tertiary sources are for orientation. They are a good place to find out what the questions are and a poor place to end up citing, because they compress arguments into statements and drop the disagreement.

## The label is relative to the question

This is the point that turns the distinction from a definition into a usable tool.

**A source is not primary or secondary in itself. It is primary or secondary for a particular question.**

- A 1950s history textbook is a **secondary** source about the nineteenth century.
- The same textbook is a **primary** source about how history was taught in the 1950s.
- A newspaper's account of a battle is a **primary** source about contemporary reporting and a **secondary** source about the battle.

So the first question is never "is this primary?" It is "primary about what?"

## Primary does not mean reliable

The most common misuse of the distinction is treating "primary" as a synonym for "trustworthy". It is not. Proximity and accuracy are different properties.

Primary sources fail in specific, predictable ways:

**Interest.** A participant writing about their own decisions has reasons to present them favourably.

**Memory.** A memoir written decades later is a primary source about the writer's recollection, which is not the same as a primary source about the events.

**Position.** Being present does not mean being able to see everything. A soldier's account of a battle is authoritative about their part of it and not about the whole.

**Institutional framing.** Official records record what an institution chose to record, in the terms it chose. Absence from the record is not absence from history.

**Survival.** What survives is not a random sample. Durable materials, valued documents and the records of powerful institutions survive at higher rates.

That last one is structural and easy to forget: the evidence base is shaped by what lasted, not by what mattered.

## A practical evaluation procedure

Four questions, applicable to any source:

**1. Who made this?** Their position, their access, their stake in the outcome.

**2. When?** At the time, or reconstructed later? Both are useful; they are useful for different things.

**3. Why?** A private letter, a public report and a legal deposition are produced for different audiences under different pressures.

**4. What were they in a position to know?** Present at the event, told about it, or inferring it?

The [U.S. National Archives' document analysis worksheets](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/worksheets) formalise essentially this procedure for teaching, and they are a good template even for casual reading.

## Where to find primary sources

More is freely available than most people assume. Two substantial, freely readable examples:

- The [*Foreign Relations of the United States*](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments) series, the State Department's official documentary record of foreign policy.
- The National Archives' [America's Founding Documents](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs).

National archives, university digital collections and government publishing offices in many countries make comparable material available.

The experience of reading a document rather than a summary of it is genuinely different, and it is the fastest way to understand why historians disagree — the raw material is more ambiguous than any account of it.

## Why this matters for a timeline

A timeline presents dates as points. Behind each point is a body of evidence of varying quality, and the presentation flattens that.

"1492" and "c. 3000 BCE" look equally definite on a screen and are not remotely equivalent claims.
