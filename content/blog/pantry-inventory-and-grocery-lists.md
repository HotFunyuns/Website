Every home inventory system dies the same way. It is set up carefully over an hour on a Sunday, maintained diligently for nine days, and then one busy week goes past without an update and the whole thing becomes fiction. After that it is worse than useless, because a wrong inventory is consulted with the same confidence as a right one.

So the design question is not "what could I record". It is **"what is the smallest set of things I can record and still answer the only question that matters in a shop: do I already have this?"**

## Track presence, not precision

For most items, the useful state is binary.

Knowing that there is rice in the house answers the question you have while standing in an aisle. Knowing that there are 430 grams of rice answers a question you will never ask, and it costs you an update after every meal that uses any.

Precision decays fastest, which is why it is the first thing to drop. An inventory that records presence stays roughly true for weeks. An inventory that records grams is wrong by Tuesday.

Quantity is worth capturing in exactly one case: where running short in the middle of cooking would genuinely spoil the evening. That is a short list in most kitchens — usually a couple of staples and whatever the household gets through fastest.

## What to deliberately ignore

The instinct with any tracking system is completeness, and completeness is what kills it. Three categories are worth excluding on purpose.

**Things you would notice running out of.** Salt, cooking oil, the basics you reach for daily. You already have a working alert system for these: the container is nearly empty and you can see it.

**The spice-rack tail.** Thirty jars, used at unpredictable intervals, almost never causing a duplicate purchase that matters. The maintenance cost is high and the payoff is negligible.

**Fresh perishables you can see.** The fridge has a door. Opening it is faster than consulting an app, and a list of fresh items goes out of date faster than anything else in the system.

What is left — tinned goods, grains and pulses, sauces and pastes, frozen items, and the bag of something bought for one recipe six weeks ago — is exactly the category that produces accidental duplicates. That is the set worth the effort.

## The list should be a subtraction

The single biggest improvement available to most shopping routines is to stop writing the list.

Written-from-scratch lists are reconstructed from memory, and memory is precisely the faculty that fails at "do we still have any of those". That is where the third jar of paprika comes from.

A better shape:

1. The week's plan states what is needed.
2. The inventory states what is present.
3. The list is the difference.

Done that way, the list is an **output**, not a document you maintain separately. It also inherits the plan's serving sizes automatically, which matters more than it sounds — a recipe scaled for six needs different quantities from the same recipe scaled for two, and scaling is not purely a multiplication problem, as [why doubling a recipe is not just doubling](/blog/recipe-scaling-and-serving-sizes/) sets out.

The sequencing here is the whole trick, and it is covered from the planning side in [how to meal plan for the week](/blog/how-to-meal-plan-for-the-week/): the plan comes from the cupboard, and the list comes from the gap.

## What our pantry tracker does not ask you for

[Easy Recipes & Meal Planner](/apps/easy-recipes-meal-planner/) keeps a list of what is in your kitchen, surfaces recipes based on ingredients you already have, and shows what is missing before you commit to a meal.

The more interesting part of that design is what it leaves out. We deliberately do not ask for:

**Expiry dates.** This was the hardest omission to accept and the one we are most confident about. Date entry is slow, the dates themselves are inconsistent between products and countries, and — most importantly — a date in an app is not a safety judgement. The FDA's own guidance is that food can make you very sick even when it does not look, smell or taste spoiled, because the bacteria that cause illness are different from the spoilage organisms that make food obviously go bad; and conversely, that anything looking or smelling suspicious should be thrown out. An app that showed a green "still fine" marker would be making a claim it has no basis for, and would quietly train people to trust it over their own judgement.

**Exact quantities as a requirement.** You can note an amount where it matters. You are never asked to.

**Storage location.** Cupboard, fridge, freezer, second shelf — all recordable in principle, all pure maintenance overhead, and none of it answering the question you had in the shop.

**Everything you own.** There is no completeness target, no percentage-complete bar, and no prompt telling you the inventory is incomplete. That is intentional, because a system that nags about completeness pushes people towards the long-tail items whose upkeep destroys the habit.

### Why partial accuracy was the right target

The argument we settled on internally: **an inventory that is roughly right and current beats one that was exactly right three weeks ago.**

A stale-but-precise list does active harm, because it is consulted with confidence. A current-but-rough list gives you the right answer to "do I already have this" most of the time, and the cost of being wrong is a duplicate tin rather than a wasted trip.

So every design decision was weighed against a single question: does this make updating cheaper or more expensive? Anything that made maintenance more expensive lost, however useful it would have been in a perfectly maintained system. Perfectly maintained systems do not exist in kitchens.

## Storage discipline is a separate system

An inventory tells you what you have. It does not tell you whether it is still good, and it is worth keeping the two apart in your head.

The FDA's published guidance on storage is specific and short enough to remember: refrigerate foods that need it as soon as you get home; stick to the two-hour rule for perishables left at room temperature; keep the refrigerator at or below **40°F (4°C)** and the freezer at **0°F (-18°C)**, checking with an appliance thermometer rather than trusting the dial; use refrigerated ready-to-eat foods as soon as possible; and throw out anything that looks or smells suspicious, including anything mouldy.

Its safe-handling guidance adds the separation rules that matter when you are putting shopping away: keep raw meat, poultry, seafood and eggs apart from other foods in the trolley, in the bags and in the refrigerator.

None of that is something an inventory can do for you. It is a habit at the point of unpacking, and it is the part that actually reduces waste.

## A routine that survives a busy week

- **Update while unpacking**, not later. Anything added to a cupboard gets added to the list in the same motion.
- **Update after cooking only for things that ran out.** Not for things that went down a bit.
- **Do a two-minute reconciliation weekly**, ideally just before planning. Open two cupboards, fix what is obviously wrong, stop.
- **Let the list build itself** from the plan minus the inventory.
- **Cook down the surplus** before it becomes waste — matching recipes against what you already own is the whole subject of [cooking from what is in the cupboard](/blog/cooking-from-what-is-in-the-pantry/), and turning a surplus into portions is covered in [batch cooking for the week](/blog/batch-cooking-for-the-week/).

If that takes more than five minutes a week, something in the scope is too ambitious. Cut it until it does not.
