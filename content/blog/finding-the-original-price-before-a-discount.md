To get from a sale price back to the original, you divide. If 30% was taken off, the sale price is 70% of the original, so the original is the sale price divided by 0.7. A ticket reading 84 after 30% off came from **120**, because 120 × 0.7 = 84.

What you must not do is add the percentage back. That is the single most common error in everyday percentage work, and it is wrong by a predictable amount every time.

## Why adding it back fails

The discount was a share of the **original**, and the original is the larger number.

- 30% of 120 is 36, and 120 − 36 = 84. That is how the sale price was produced.
- 30% of 84 is only 25.20, and 84 + 25.20 = 109.20.

You land 10.80 short, because you took 30% of the wrong base. The gap widens as the discount grows: at 50% off you would be out by a quarter of the original, and at 75% off by more than half of it.

Forward discount calculation — working out what you pay rather than what you save — is covered in [percentages in your head](/blog/percentages-in-your-head/). This article is the same machinery run backwards, which is where it breaks for most people.

## Doing the division without a calculator

Dividing by 0.7 sounds unpleasant. Converting the proportion to a fraction removes the unpleasantness entirely, because dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its reciprocal.

**84 ÷ 0.7** becomes 84 × 10/7. Eighty-four times ten is 840; 840 ÷ 7 is **120**.

The table below lists the proportions you actually pay at common discount levels, with the multiplier that reverses each one. The right-hand column is what you should be reaching for.

| Discount | You pay | Reverse by multiplying by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10% off | 9/10 | 10/9 |
| 20% off | 4/5 | 5/4 |
| 25% off | 3/4 | 4/3 |
| 30% off | 7/10 | 10/7 |
| 40% off | 3/5 | 5/3 |
| 50% off | 1/2 | 2 |
| 60% off | 2/5 | 5/2 |
| 75% off | 1/4 | 4 |

Three worked examples using the right-hand column:

- **96 after 25% off.** Multiply by 4/3: 96 × 4 = 384, ÷ 3 = **128**. Check: 128 × 0.75 = 96.
- **60 after 20% off.** Multiply by 5/4: 60 × 5 = 300, ÷ 4 = **75**. Check: 75 × 0.8 = 60.
- **51 after 40% off.** Multiply by 5/3: 51 × 5 = 255, ÷ 3 = **85**. Check: 85 × 0.6 = 51.

Every one of these is a small multiplication followed by a small division, which is why the fraction form is worth the conversion step. The habit of reaching for the fraction rather than the decimal is the same one that makes percentages tractable generally, and the conversions are collected in [converting between fractions, decimals and percents](/blog/fraction-decimal-percent-conversions/).

## Stacked discounts reverse the same way

An extra 20% off an already 30%-off price does not mean 50% off. You pay 0.8 of 0.7, which is **0.56** of the original — a 44% discount.

Reversing it is one division. A final price of **42** came from 42 ÷ 0.56 = **75**. Check it forwards: 75 × 0.7 = 52.50, and 52.50 × 0.8 = 42.

The order does not matter, because multiplication commutes: 0.7 × 0.8 and 0.8 × 0.7 are the same number. That is worth knowing when a sign promises "an extra 20% off already-reduced prices" and you want to check whether the sequence was applied as advertised.

## Why a rise and an equal fall never cancel

The same structure explains a fact people find genuinely surprising: a 10% increase followed by a 10% decrease does not return you to the start.

NRICH's *Percentage Unchanged* problem makes the point cleanly. If one quantity grows by 10%, so is multiplied by 1.1, the compensating change is multiplication by 1/1.1, which is about 0.909 — a decrease of roughly **9.09%**, not 10%. Reversing a multiplier takes its reciprocal, and a reciprocal is not a complement.

The cleanest case: reversing a 25% markup means dividing by 1.25, and 1/1.25 is exactly 0.8 — a 20% reduction. A 25% rise is undone by a 20% fall, and the asymmetry is not an accident of these particular numbers.

## The same move on tax and on markup

Every one of these problems is "a number was multiplied by something; undo it".

**Sales tax.** A tax-inclusive total is the pre-tax price × (1 + rate). At 8.25%, a total of 108.25 came from a pre-tax price of 108.25 ÷ 1.0825 = **100**. Subtracting 8.25% from the total would give 99.32, wrong for the same reason as the discount case. The forward version, and why 8.25% decomposes so nicely, is in [estimating sales tax from friendly blocks](/blog/estimating-sales-tax/).

**Retail markup.** If a seller marks up cost by 25%, the price is cost × 1.25, so cost is price ÷ 1.25 — which, as above, is price × 0.8.

## Checking a "was / now" claim for internal consistency

Reverse percentages give you a cheap consistency test on advertising. If a sign says "40% off — now 45", the implied original is 45 ÷ 0.6 = **75**. If the ticket beside it claims the original was 70, the two statements disagree, because 70 × 0.6 is 42, not 45.

That is an arithmetic observation, not a legal one. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission publishes a set of guides on deceptive pricing at 16 CFR Part 233, which cover comparisons made against a former price. Whether any particular claim complies is a question for the regulator and the seller; what the arithmetic gives you is the ability to notice when a sign is not internally consistent with itself.

The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide on fractions instruction recommends building conceptual understanding of ratio, rate and proportion strategies before handing learners cross-multiplication as a procedure, and this is a good illustration of why. A reader who understands that the sale price is a proportion of the original will never add the percentage back; a reader who has only memorised a formula has nothing to catch the mistake.
