**The question has no single answer, and the reason is more useful than a number would be: word frequency is extremely uneven.** A small set of very common words accounts for a large share of ordinary speech, and each additional thousand words after that buys progressively less.

That shape — steep at first, flattening quickly — is what makes word-count targets simultaneously encouraging and misleading.

## The coverage idea

Vocabulary research often frames this as **text coverage**: what proportion of the words in a typical text or conversation you would recognise, given a vocabulary of a certain size.

The pattern that emerges consistently across studies of many languages is:

- The **most frequent few hundred words** cover a large proportion of everyday spoken language.
- The next few thousand add meaningfully but far less per word.
- Beyond that, each additional thousand words covers a progressively smaller share.

We are deliberately not quoting specific percentages here. The exact figures vary by language, by corpus and by how a "word" is counted, and picking one number from one study and presenting it as a general fact is the kind of precision that misleads. The *shape* of the curve is robust; the exact numbers are not.

## Why which words matters more than how many

Because of that curve, **a thousand high-frequency words outperform three thousand chosen at random**, comfortably.

This has a direct practical consequence: study a frequency-ordered list, or study words grouped by the situations you will actually be in — which approximates frequency for your purposes. Do not study words because they appeared in a text you happened to read.

It also explains why vocabulary from a themed lesson feels more useful than vocabulary from a novel. A lesson on ordering food teaches words you will use; a novel teaches words the author needed.

## Active and passive vocabulary are different sizes

You understand far more words than you can produce. This is true in your first language too — you recognise plenty of words you would never use.

| | Passive (recognition) | Active (production) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it grows | Reading and listening | Speaking and writing |
| Typical size | Larger | Smaller |
| What it lets you do | Understand | Be understood |

The gap matters because **conversation requires active vocabulary**, and most study methods build passive vocabulary. Flashcards, reading and listening all grow recognition. Only production practice grows production.

The fix is not more input. It is retrieval: producing the word from memory rather than recognising it. Retrieval practice is one of the better-supported learning techniques in the [Dunlosky review of effective learning techniques](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/), and it is what makes a word available when you need it.

## What "knowing a word" actually means

There are at least four stages, and conflating them is where word counts become fiction:

1. **Recognise it** when you see it written.
2. **Recognise it** when you hear it in speech — much harder, because speech is fast and unsegmented.
3. **Produce it** when you have time to think.
4. **Produce it** under time pressure, in a sentence you are building.

Only stage four is useful in conversation. A learner counting stage-one words and reporting a vocabulary size is not lying, but the number does not describe what they can do.

## How to build vocabulary that survives

**Learn in context, not in isolation.** A word learned inside a phrase comes with its grammar, its collocations and a retrieval cue attached.

**Learn the word with everything attached to it.** Gender and article in Italian; tone mark in Vietnamese or Thai. Anything that attaches to every word should be learned with the word, or it has to be learned twice.

**Space the reviews.** Distributed practice substantially outperforms massed practice for verbal recall — the effect synthesised across a large body of studies by [Cepeda and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/). [Spaced repetition for language learning](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-language-learning/) covers what that means in practice, including what our apps do and do not implement.

**Practise retrieval, not recognition.** Cover the answer. Produce it before checking.

**Accept forgetting.** Words drop out. Reviewing something you have forgotten is not evidence of failure, it is the mechanism working.

## A better target than a word count

CEFR levels describe what you can do rather than what you know, which sidesteps the whole problem — [CEFR levels explained](/blog/cefr-levels-explained/) covers them. So do concrete tasks: order a meal and handle the follow-up, understand a phone call about an appointment, read a news article without a dictionary.

Those are testable. A vocabulary count is a proxy, and a loose one.
