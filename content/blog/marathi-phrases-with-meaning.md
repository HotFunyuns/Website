The ordinary Marathi goodbye is येतो (*yeto*), and it means "I come".

Not "I go". Not "farewell". A male speaker leaving says the verb for coming, in the present tense, first person — roughly the way an English speaker says "I'll be off" while standing still. A female speaker says येते (*yete*), same verb, different ending, because the verb agrees with her gender.

Two words, and you have already met Marathi's verb agreement, its present tense, and the fact that idioms do not translate. That is the case for learning phrases with their literal sense attached rather than as flashcard pairs.

## Greetings, and the one that does all the work

**नमस्कार** — *namaskār* — hello. Sanskrit-derived, usable at any hour and in any company, and the only greeting you strictly need. It is closer to "salutations" than to "hi", but it does not land as stiffly as that suggests, because it is simply what people say.

**सु-प्रभात** — *su-prabhāt* — good morning, literally "good dawn". **शुभ-संध्या** (*shubh-sandhyā*) is good evening and **शुभ रात्री** (*shubha rātrī*) good night. These are real but somewhat formal; *namaskār* covers all three in most situations.

**येतो / येते** — *yeto* / *yete* — goodbye, literally "I come". **पुन्हा भेटू** (*pūnhā bhetū*) is "let's meet again", the Marathi equivalent of "see you".

## Asking how someone is, and meeting gender immediately

This is the phrase that teaches the most per syllable.

- **तू कसा आहेस?** — *tu kasā āhes* — how are you, to a man.
- **तू कशी आहेस?** — *tu kashī āhes* — how are you, to a woman.
- **आपण कसे आहात?** — *āpaṇ kase āhāt* — how are you, polite or plural.

The changing piece is *kasā / kashī / kase*, a declinable adjective agreeing with the person addressed. The verb shifts too. Nothing about this is advanced or optional — it is the second sentence anyone learns, and it already requires the agreement system.

The reply is **मी ठीक आहे** (*mī ṭīk āhe*), "I am fine", or the warmer **मी मजेत आहे** (*mī majet āhe*), roughly "I am in good spirits".

Note आपण in the polite form. The same word is Marathi's *inclusive* we — "you and I" — as against आम्ही (*āmhī*), "us but not you". Marathi makes a distinction here that neither English nor Hindi makes, and it turns up in perfectly ordinary sentences. [Marathi verbs for beginners](/blog/marathi-verb-basics/) works through how the pronoun choice ripples into the verb.

## Introducing yourself

- **तुझं नाव काय आहे?** — *tujha nāv kāy āhe* — what is your name, informal. Literally "your name what is".
- **आपले नाव काय आहे?** — *āpale nāv kāy āhe* — the same, polite.
- **माझं नाव ... आहे** — *māzha nāv ... āhe* — my name is ...
- **तुम्हाला भेटून आनंद झाला** — *tumhālā bhetūn ānanda jhālā* — pleased to meet you; literally "having met you, happiness happened".

That last literal reading is worth sitting with. Marathi builds a lot of emotional and experiential statements as things that *happen to* a person rather than things a person *does*, and seeing it once in a set phrase makes the pattern recognisable when it returns in grammar.

## Food, shops and prices

This is where numbers stop being an academic exercise. **किती?** (*kitī*) — how much / how many — is the single highest-value word in a market, and it is useless without the numbers on the other side of it. [Marathi numbers 1 to 100](/blog/marathi-numbers-and-counting/) explains why those take longer than you would expect and how to drill them in blocks.

Useful companions: **हे काय आहे?** (*he kāy āhe*) "what is this?", **मला ... पाहिजे** (*malā ... pāhije*) "I want ...", literally "to me ... is wanted" — again, the thing happens to you rather than being done by you.

## Getting around, and the two words that rescue a conversation

**कुठे?** (*kuṭhe*) — where. **इथे** (*ithe*) — here. **तिथे** (*tithe*) — there.

And the pair every traveller actually uses: **मला समजलं नाही** — *malā samajlaṁ nāhī* — "I did not understand", literally "to me it was not understood"; and a request to slow down. Being able to say you did not follow something is worth more than another ten nouns, because it keeps the conversation alive instead of ending it.

## How the course groups these, and the group we removed

The vocabulary units in [Learn Marathi Language Fast](/apps/learn-marathi/) are organised by situation rather than by part of speech: greetings and introductions, family, food, shopping and prices, transport and directions, work, travel, health, numbers and time. Cultural notes and etiquette sit alongside the words rather than in a separate chapter, because in Marathi the register you choose *is* the etiquette.

Situation grouping is not decoration. Retrieval is cued by context, and a word learned inside the scene where you will need it comes back faster than the same word learned in an alphabetical list.

One group did not survive. An early version had a unit built around formal and written correspondence — the shapes of a letter, formal address, the vocabulary of official communication. It was well made and almost nobody opened it.

The reason, once we looked at who this course is for, was obvious. Marathi learners using a phone course are overwhelmingly heritage learners reconnecting with family, people moving to or travelling in Maharashtra, and Marathi speakers running the course in reverse to study English. None of those three needs to draft a formal letter in Marathi, and the two who might would reach for something other than a beginner app.

We cut it and moved the effort into the health and emergency vocabulary instead, which is dull to write and is the material people are most grateful for at the worst possible moment.

The limitation worth stating plainly: because the groups are built around a visitor's or a family member's day, the course under-serves anyone who needs Marathi for formal, academic or literary purposes. That is a real gap, chosen deliberately, and a different kind of resource is the right answer for it.
