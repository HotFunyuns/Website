Most problems labelled "proportion" are scaling problems, and scaling is easier than solving. Find the factor that takes the known quantity to the wanted one, then apply that factor to everything else. Cross-multiplication is the fallback for the minority of cases where no clean factor exists — not the default, and certainly not the first thing to reach for.

The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide on fractions instruction recommends exactly this ordering for teaching: develop conceptual understanding of strategies for ratio, rate and proportion problems **before** introducing cross-multiplication as a procedure. The same ordering works for an adult who wants to do this in an aisle or a kitchen rather than on paper.

## Two words that are not synonyms

A **ratio** compares quantities with one another: three parts of this to two parts of that, written 3:2. A **proportion** is the claim that two ratios are equal — that 3:2 and 12:8 describe the same relationship.

The distinction matters because of where ratios go wrong. **In a 3:2 ratio, the first share is three fifths of the whole, not three halves of it.** Add the parts — 3 + 2 = 5 — and each part is a fifth. So the first share is 60% of the total and the second is 40%.

Read a ratio as a fraction of the whole without adding the parts first, and every subsequent step inherits the error. This is the single most productive check in the subject: **what do the parts add to?**

## Scaling by a factor

**Clean factors.** A recipe serving 4 needs to serve 6. The factor is 6/4 = 1.5, and it applies to everything: 300 g of flour becomes 450 g, 2 eggs become 3, 180 ml of milk becomes 270 ml.

Serving 10 from the same recipe is a factor of 2.5: 300 g becomes 750 g, 180 ml becomes 450 ml, 2 eggs become 5.

**Awkward factors: go via one.** Serving 7 from a recipe for 4 is a factor of 1.75, which nobody wants to multiply by repeatedly. Instead, divide once to get the per-serving amount and multiply back up. Flour is 300 ÷ 4 = 75 g per serving, so seven servings need **525 g**. Milk is 45 ml per serving, so 315 ml.

That second route is the **unit rate**, and it is the same move that makes a shelf comparison work. NIST describes unit pricing as the cost per unit of measure precisely because reducing everything to one unit makes otherwise incomparable quantities comparable. The shopping version of the same reasoning is in [unit price: comparing two sizes without a calculator](/blog/unit-price-comparison/).

## Sharing an amount in a ratio

A three-step routine, and the third step is the check.

**Split 480 in the ratio 5:3.** Parts add to 8, so one share is 480 ÷ 8 = 60. The two amounts are 5 × 60 = **300** and 3 × 60 = **180**. They add back to 480.

It extends to any number of parts. **Split 1,260 in the ratio 4:2:1.** Parts add to 7, so one share is 180, and the three amounts are **720, 360 and 180** — which sum to 1,260.

The common failures are both avoidable by the check. Dividing by 5 and by 3 separately gives two numbers that do not add to the total. Treating 5:3 as "five thirds of it" gives one number larger than the total. Adding the parts first makes both impossible.

## Map and model scales

A scale is a ratio with the units stripped out, which is why 1:25,000 means the same thing whatever you measure in.

**1:25,000.** One centimetre on the map is 25,000 centimetres on the ground. Since 100 cm is a metre, that is **250 m**, and four centimetres is therefore one kilometre.

**1:50,000.** One centimetre is 50,000 cm, which is **500 m**, so two centimetres is a kilometre.

The useful mental handle is the second figure in each case: remember "4 cm to the km" and "2 cm to the km" rather than re-deriving them, and the map becomes readable at a glance.

## Why ratios cannot be added

This is the deepest trap in the subject, and it is worth working through properly.

Mix one litre of a blend that is 1:3 with one litre of a blend that is 1:1. Adding the ratio terms gives 2:4, which simplifies to 1:2. That answer is wrong.

Convert to quantities. A 1:3 blend is one part in four, so a litre holds 250 ml of the first component and 750 ml of the second. A 1:1 blend is half and half: 500 ml and 500 ml. Combined, you have 750 ml and 1,250 ml — a ratio of **3:5**, not 1:2.

NRICH's *Mixing Paints* problem is built on exactly this point. Its published solution works in actual volumes — a can of paint mixed 1:3 contributes 250 ml of red and 750 ml of white, a can mixed 1:7 contributes 125 ml and 875 ml — and only then combines them. The reason ratio terms cannot be added is that the parts in each ratio refer to differently sized wholes, so the two "1"s are not the same quantity.

## The percentage swap is proportional reasoning

The identity that x% of y equals y% of x is a proportion trick that most people meet as a curiosity. It is worth keeping because it converts an awkward calculation into an obvious one.

- 4% of 75 is fiddly; 75% of 4 is **3**.
- 18% of 50 is fiddly; 50% of 18 is **9**.
- 12% of 25 is fiddly; 25% of 12 is **3**.

It works because both expressions are the same product, with the hundred shuffled from one side to the other. The rest of the percentage toolkit — the 1% and 10% anchors, and the difference between percent and percentage points — is in [percentages in your head](/blog/percentages-in-your-head/).

## Where cross-multiplication belongs

It belongs at the end, for the cases nothing else reaches. Given a/b = c/d, the products a × d and b × c are equal, which lets you solve for any missing term.

**7/9 = x/24.** Cross-multiplying gives 9x = 7 × 24 = 168, so x = 168 ÷ 9 = **18.67**. No scale factor between 9 and 24 is clean, so this is the right tool.

Use it when the numbers refuse to cooperate, and prefer scaling when they do. A procedure you understand is one you can check; a procedure you only execute is one that fails silently. The same argument applies to comparing two fractions, where cross-multiplication is likewise the fallback rather than the first move — see [comparing two fractions without a common denominator](/blog/comparing-fractions-mentally/).
