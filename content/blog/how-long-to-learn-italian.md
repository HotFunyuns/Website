**Any single number answering this question is hiding four assumptions: which level, which skills, what kind of study, and what languages you already speak.** Change any of them and the answer changes by a factor of several.

That is not evasion. It is the actual state of the evidence, and knowing it lets you set a target you can hold yourself to.

## Why published estimates disagree

The figures that circulate come from different places and measure different things.

**Institutional estimates** — the ones that group languages into difficulty categories with hour ranges — come from intensive full-time training programmes with selected learners, professional instruction and a specific target level. They are internally consistent and were never designed to predict what a self-studying adult with forty minutes a day will achieve.

**Course marketing estimates** measure completion of a course, which is not the same as a level of ability.

**Anecdotal estimates** measure one person's experience, usually reported at the point where they felt successful.

None is dishonest. They are answers to different questions, and quoting one as an answer to yours is where the error creeps in.

## Where Italian sits

Italian is generally placed among the more accessible languages for English speakers, alongside Spanish, French and Portuguese. Two reasons, both real:

**Shared vocabulary.** English absorbed a large Latin-derived vocabulary, so an English speaker starts with substantial passive recognition — particularly in reading.

**Transparent spelling.** Italian orthography maps closely to pronunciation, so reading aloud is learnable in days rather than months. That is an advantage over French and a very large advantage over English.

The countervailing costs are grammatical gender, agreement and verb conjugation — covered in [Italian articles and gender explained](/blog/italian-articles-and-gender/) and [Italian verb conjugation basics](/blog/italian-verb-conjugation-basics/).

Compared with something structurally further away, the gap is stark: [how long does it take to learn Russian](/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-russian/) covers a language with a case system, a different alphabet and far less shared vocabulary.

## The four skills develop at different speeds

"How long to be fluent" is really four questions, and for Italian the answers diverge unusually widely.

| Skill | Relative speed for an English speaker | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading | Fastest | Shared vocabulary, transparent spelling |
| Listening | Slower | Connected speech is faster than written material suggests |
| Speaking | Slower still | Requires agreement and conjugation in real time |
| Writing | Variable | Depends on how much precision you need |

The practical effect is a common experience: **you will understand far more than you can say for a long time.** That is normal and is not evidence that you are learning badly.

## Frequency beats total hours

For the same weekly total, distributed study outperforms massed study. This is one of the better-established findings in the learning literature — the meta-analysis by [Cepeda and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/) synthesised a large body of work on distributed practice in verbal recall.

The practical version: **thirty minutes a day beats three and a half hours on Sunday.** Seven consolidation opportunities rather than one.

This is also the argument for app-shaped study. A course you open daily for a short session is exploiting exactly the effect above; one you open occasionally for a long session is not. [Spaced repetition for language learning](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-language-learning/) covers the mechanism and, importantly, what our apps do and do not implement.

## Set a target you can measure

The [Common European Framework of Reference](https://europa.eu/europass/en/common-european-framework-reference-language-skills) describes ability in terms of what a learner can *do* at each level, which makes it a far better target than an hour count. The [ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines](https://www.actfl.org/educator-resources/actfl-proficiency-guidelines) do something similar in a different framework.

[CEFR levels explained](/blog/cefr-levels-explained/) covers what each level means in practice.

Alternatively, set a task-based target:

- Order a meal and handle the follow-up questions.
- Understand a phone call about an appointment.
- Read a news article without a dictionary.
- Have a ten-minute conversation about your work.

These are testable. "Fluent" is not, and it recedes as you improve — the more Italian you have, the more you notice what you are missing.

## What we will not tell you

We are not going to give you a number of hours to fluency, because we do not have data that would support one, and inventing one would be worse than useless — it sets an expectation that a real learner will fail to meet and then blame themselves for.

What we can say honestly is the ordering: **frequency matters more than intensity, comprehensible material matters more than difficult material, and speaking practice with a person cannot be substituted.**
