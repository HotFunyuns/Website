You learn that *beli* means buy. Then you walk past a shop and the sign says **pembelian**, the receipt says **dibeli**, the notice in the window says **membeli**, and the person at the counter is a **pembeli**. Four words, one root, and three of them are not in your head.

Malay does not conjugate for tense, person or number, and it has no grammatical gender. What it does instead is attach affixes to a root, and those affixes carry the work an English speaker expects to find in verb endings, auxiliaries and word order. Learn the root, learn roughly a dozen affix jobs, and a word you have never seen becomes readable on sight.

## The root is the unit worth memorising

Malay roots are short, usually two syllables, and they are what a dictionary is organised around. *Beli*, *tulis*, *jalan*, *makan*, *besar*, *kerja*.

Everything else is built on top of them. This is the opposite of the situation in a language with case endings, where a word's forms have to be learned as a set. In Malay you learn one form and then learn the machinery that generates the rest, and the machinery is finite.

The practical consequence for a beginner is that **your vocabulary is larger than you think it is.** If you know *tulis*, you already hold most of *menulis* (to write), *penulis* (a writer), *tulisan* (writing, a piece of text) and *ditulis* (was written). Nobody has shown you how to unlock them yet.

## meN-: the prefix that changes shape

This is the one that makes Malay look harder than it is, because it is written five or six different ways.

meN- marks an agent-focused verb: the doer is the subject. But the nasal sound at the end of the prefix assimilates to whatever consonant starts the root, and in some cases swallows it.

| Root starts with | Prefix becomes | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a vowel, or g, h, kh | **meng-** | *ajar* to **meng**ajar, teach; *gulung* to **mengg**ulung, roll |
| **k** | **meng-**, k absorbed | *kenal* to **meng**enal, know or recognise |
| b, f, v | **mem-** | *beli* to **memb**eli, buy |
| **p** | **mem-**, p absorbed | *pilih* to **mem**ilih, choose |
| c, d, j, sy, z | **men-** | *dukung* to **mend**ukung, support; *cabut* to **menc**abut, pull out |
| **t** | **men-**, t absorbed | *tulis* to **men**ulis, write |
| **s** | **meny-**, s absorbed | *sapu* to **meny**apu, sweep |
| l, m, n, ny, ng, r, w, y | **me-** | *layang* to **me**layang, glide; *rampas* to **me**rampas, seize |
| one-syllable roots | **menge-** | *bom* to **menge**bom, bomb |

Stare at that table for a moment and a single rule falls out of it: **the voiceless consonants p, t, k and s get absorbed, and their voiced partners b, d, j and g survive.** *Beli* keeps its b in *membeli*. *Pilih* loses its p in *memilih*.

That is the whole pattern, and it is the one thing here worth committing to memory, because it also tells you how to run the process backwards. A word beginning *mem-* followed immediately by a vowel almost certainly has a root starting with *p*.

## ber-, ter- and di-: the three you meet next

**ber-** attaches mostly to nouns and adjectives and produces something closer to "to have" or "to be in the state of". *Kerja*, work, gives *bekerja*, to work. *Jalan*, road, gives *berjalan*, to walk. It shortens to *be-* before a root starting with *r* or whose first syllable contains *er*, which is why it is *bekerja* and not *berkerja*. *Ajar* is irregular and gives *belajar*, to study.

**ter-** marks something that happened without anyone choosing it, or a resulting state. *Buka*, open, gives *terbuka*, open in the sense of having been opened. *Makan*, eat, gives *termakan*, eaten by accident. When you see *ter-*, expect "accidentally", "suddenly" or "already".

**di-** is patient focus, the form usually translated into English as a passive. This is the affix a traveller meets first, because it is all over public signage. *Dilarang merokok*: smoking is prohibited. *Dibeli*: was bought. If you take one thing from this section, take this — a word starting *di-* is telling you something is being done *to* the thing named, frequently by an authority that has not named itself.

## The two suffixes people mix up

Both **-kan** and **-i** make a verb transitive. They do not do it the same way.

**-kan** usually introduces the thing acted on, or a beneficiary. *Membeli* is to buy; *membelikan* is to buy something *for someone*. *Besar* is big; *membesarkan* is to enlarge, to make something big.

**-i** usually introduces a place, surface or target that the action lands on. *Duduk* is to sit; *menduduki* is to occupy or sit on. *Naik* is to go up; *menaiki* is to board.

An honest warning: the distinction is real but it is not fully predictable. Plenty of roots take one and not the other for reasons that are historical rather than logical, and the reliable way to acquire them is in phrases, from reading, rather than off a rule sheet. Anyone offering you a clean rule here is selling a simplification.

## The noun-making family

Three patterns turn the same root into three different nouns, and once you can see them the dictionary stops looking so long.

- **peN-** makes a person or an instrument, and assimilates exactly like meN-: *beli* to *pembeli* (buyer), *tulis* to *penulis* (writer), *sapu* to *penyapu* (broom).
- **pe-...-an** and **per-...-an** make a process or a place: *beli* to *pembelian* (purchase), *jalan* to *perjalanan* (a journey), *kerja* to *pekerjaan* (a job).
- **ke-...-an** makes an abstract quality, and sometimes an accidental event: *bersih* (clean) to *kebersihan* (cleanliness), *sihat* (healthy) to *kesihatan* (health), *hilang* (lost) to *kehilangan* (a loss).

Reduplication belongs to the same toolkit even though it is not an affix. *Buku* (book) gives *buku-buku* (books); *kanak* gives *kanak-kanak* (children). It marks plurality, variety or intensity depending on the word, and it is one of the reasons Malay gets away with having no plural ending at all.

## A reading procedure that works immediately

When you hit a word you do not know:

1. **Strip the prefix.** *meng-*, *mem-*, *men-*, *meny-*, *me-*, *ber-*, *ter-*, *di-*, *pe-*, *ke-*.
2. **Strip the suffix.** *-kan*, *-i*, *-an*.
3. **Restore the absorbed consonant** if the prefix was mem-, men-, meng- or meny- and what remains starts with a vowel. *Memilih* gives *pilih*. *Menulis* gives *tulis*. *Menyapu* gives *sapu*.
4. **Look up what is left.** That is the root, and the dictionary is organised around it.
5. **Read the affixes back on as meaning.** Who is doing it (meN-), what it is being done to (di-), whether it was deliberate (ter-), and whether the word names a person, a process or a quality (peN-, pe-...-an, ke-...-an).

This takes about four seconds once it is habit, and it converts Malay from a language where every unfamiliar word is a wall into one where most unfamiliar words are a known word wearing a hat.
