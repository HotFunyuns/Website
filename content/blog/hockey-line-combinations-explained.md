Watch a coach for two periods and you will see the same forwards appear beside three different partners. It looks like indecision. It is closer to the opposite: line combinations are the main lever a hockey coach holds once the puck has dropped, and pulling it is most of the job.

Understanding why is the difference between watching hockey as a series of individuals and watching it as what it actually is — a rotation of small units, each of which is either working or not.

## The unit is the thing, not the player

Hockey groups its skaters twice over. Forwards are grouped into **lines** of three: a centre with a left wing and a right wing. Defencemen are grouped into **pairs**. Those two groupings rotate independently, so the five skaters on the ice at any given moment are normally one forward line plus one defence pair, with the goaltender staying on throughout.

That is exactly the shape [84-0 Hockey Draft & GM Manager](/apps/hockey-draft-gm-manager/) asks you to build: a starting six of centre, left wing, right wing, two defence slots and a goaltender, plus the coach who ties it together. Six decisions, which sounds small until you notice that every one of them is load-bearing.

The consequence of playing in units is that individual quality stops being the interesting question. A forward who is excellent at retrieving pucks in the corner and terrible at finishing is a problem on one line and a solution on another. Nothing about the player changed; the combination did.

## Why lines exist at all

Other sports substitute at stoppages. Hockey substitutes while the play is running, and shifts are short — deliberately, brutally short — because skating at full effort has a hard ceiling measured in seconds rather than minutes.

That single fact restructures everything. A hockey roster is not a starting lineup with reserves behind it; it is a **rotation**, and every player in it will be on the ice. The usual arrangement across the sport is four forward lines and three defence pairs, which is internally tidy: four lines of three forwards accounts for twelve forwards, and three pairs of two accounts for six defencemen.

So the question a coach is answering is not "who are my best players" but "how do I divide the available minutes so that the ones I trust most get the situations that matter most, without any of them being exhausted when it counts".

## What a working line contains

The instinct of every new drafter is to put the three highest-rated forwards together. It is also the most reliable way to produce a line that underperforms its ratings.

Functioning lines tend to contain different jobs rather than three copies of the same one:

- **Someone who gets the puck back.** Forechecking, board battles, winning it in the corner. Unglamorous and non-negotiable, because a line that cannot recover possession spends its shifts defending.
- **Someone who moves it.** Vision, passing, and the willingness to give the puck up. Frequently the centre, though not always.
- **Someone who finishes.** The release and the instinct to be in the right place when the chance arrives.

Three finishers on one line will all drift to the same space and none of them will do the retrieval work. Three grinders will win every puck and have nothing to do with it. The combinations that stick are almost always the ones where the three jobs are genuinely covered.

### Handedness, and why it is not trivia

A left-handed shot playing on the right wing receives the puck on their forehand coming across the middle and shoots from a different angle than a right-handed shot in the same spot. Neither arrangement is correct in general; they produce different shot locations and different passing lanes.

The same applies on defence, where a pair with one left-shot and one right-shot defenceman can each play their natural side without turning their body away from the play. Coaches care about this more than casual viewers expect, and it is one reason a pair that looks unbalanced on ratings can still be the better arrangement.

## The hierarchy, and what each line is for

Lines are usually numbered by the minutes and the situations they get.

| Line | Typical job | What good looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First | Score, against the opposition's best | Producing while facing the hardest matchup |
| Second | Score, against softer matchups | Genuine secondary production rather than a diluted copy of the first |
| Third | Defensive assignments, checking, tough zone starts | Coming out even against a stronger line |
| Fourth | Short, energetic shifts; preserving the top lines | Not conceding, and changing the momentum of a period |

The bottom of that table is where most people misjudge a roster. A fourth line's contribution is largely negative in the arithmetic sense — it consists of goals that did not happen and minutes the first line did not have to play. That contribution is real and it never appears next to anybody's name.

Defence pairs follow the same logic in miniature. The pair that plays the most is not necessarily the one with the highest ratings; it is the one the coach trusts with a one-goal lead and a faceoff in their own end.

## The levers a coach actually has

Once play starts, a coach controls three things.

**Matchup.** Rulebooks generally give one side the final line change before a faceoff, so one coach can choose who faces whom and the other has to react. This is why a checking line exists in the first place: its purpose is to make the opposing coach's best option unattractive.

**Zone starts.** A faceoff in the attacking end and a faceoff in the defensive end are different assignments. Coaches routinely give offensive-minded lines the favourable draws and hand the awkward ones to a line built to survive them — which then makes the raw production numbers of both lines misleading.

**Minutes.** Shortening the bench late in a close game concentrates ice time in the lines a coach trusts, at the cost of running them tired. It is a trade, and coaches make it every night.

## Why lines get shuffled

Given how much value there is in combinations settling, constant shuffling looks self-defeating. There are four sound reasons for it and one bad one.

1. **Injury.** Somebody has to move, and moving one forward usually moves three.
2. **A line that is not working.** Not the same as a line that is not scoring. A line getting outplayed on possession is failing even if the scoreboard has not noticed yet.
3. **Reviving a struggling player.** Putting a forward who has lost confidence beside a playmaker is the cheapest available fix.
4. **Chasing or protecting.** Behind late, coaches load one line up deliberately. Ahead late, they shorten the bench and lean on the lines they trust defensively.

The bad reason is reacting to a single result. Combinations need shifts together before you know anything, and a coach who rebuilds the forward group after every defeat never accumulates the evidence that would tell them what is actually wrong.

## Special teams are a different roster

When a side is short a skater, the arithmetic changes: five skaters against four, instead of five against five.

That extra space means the units are not built like ordinary lines. A power-play unit is normally assembled from whoever moves and shoots the puck best rather than from an intact forward line, which often means loading it with forwards and carrying a single defenceman for the point. A penalty-killing unit is chosen on the opposite basis — players who deny passing lanes, block shots and get the puck out of the zone without needing to do anything clever with it.

The practical implication for roster building is that special-teams ability is a separate axis. A player who is unremarkable at even strength can be genuinely valuable on one of these units, and a lineup that ignores the distinction is leaving something on the table.

All of which points at a specific drafting habit: **stop asking who is best available and start asking what this lineup is currently missing.**

## What the projection is doing

When the last pick is in and the simulator returns a projected 84-game record, that number is the game's own assessment of the lineup you built, produced by its internal rules.

Three things are worth stating plainly. The players are fictional and generated inside the game, so nothing in the result relates to any real league, team or athlete. It is a score on a roster puzzle, not a prediction, a forecast or guidance of any kind. And the perfect record the game is named for is deliberately hard — most rosters land short, and the size of the shortfall is the feedback you drafted for.

The productive loop is comparative. Change one slot, draft again, and see whether the projection agrees with your reasoning about combinations. That tells you something real about how you evaluate a lineup, even though it tells you nothing about hockey outside the game.
