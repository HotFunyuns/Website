Most draft games ask you to assemble talent. A rugby draft asks you to assemble a set piece, a decision-maker, a kicker and a backline, in that order, and then find enough depth to get the whole thing through a long season — all inside a budget.

That ordering is not a preference. It falls out of the laws. A scrum requires eight players from each side, so the pack is not negotiable, and a side that cannot compete there concedes penalties worth three points each. You can win a match with an ordinary backline. You cannot win one from behind your own scrum going backwards.

## The draft board is a supply problem, not a ranking problem

[24-0 Rugby Draft Pro League](/apps/rugby-draft-pro-league/) opens on a pool of more than 1,000 players — forwards, backs, specialists and goal kickers, drawn from different eras — and the pool rotates, so the same plan will not work twice.

The rotation is the design decision that shapes everything else about drafting here.

What rotates is which slice of the pool is on the board: not just which individuals, but which positional groups are deep and which are thin in this particular draft. One board opens with an abundance of props and no fly-half worth the name. The next inverts it.

The consequence is that **there is no memorised opening**. A player who learned a sequence that worked last time will find it does not apply, because the thing that made it work — availability — has moved. What transfers between drafts is judgement about value and position fit, which is the skill the mode is trying to test.

It also means you will sometimes pay above the odds. If the board is short of hookers, hookers are expensive, and there is no reroll that makes that untrue. The guarantee the game does make is narrower: every board contains enough of each positional group to field a legal starting fifteen. Scarcity affects price and quality, never legality.

## An order of operations that survives a bad board

Four passes, in this sequence.

**1. Secure the set piece.** Props, hooker, locks. This is the group where a shortfall cannot be papered over from elsewhere in the squad, and it is the group most drafters leave too late because it is the least exciting. [Rugby forwards and backs](/blog/rugby-forwards-and-backs/) explains why front-row cover is the least substitutable resource on the board.

**2. Buy the decisions.** Scrum-half and fly-half. The 9 decides how fast the ball leaves a breakdown and the 10 decides what happens to it, and in the season model those two attributes touch more phases of play than anything else in the backline.

**3. Buy the points.** A goal kicker. This is the pick that most consistently returns more than it costs, for reasons covered in [how rugby scoring works](/blog/how-rugby-scoring-works/): a penalty goal is worth three points, penalties are frequent in union, and a reliable kicker turns territory into score without needing a try.

**4. Buy pace last, deliberately.** Wings and outside backs. Pace is genuinely valuable in the model and it is also the attribute whose price is most inflated by how it looks on a card. Drafting it last does not mean neglecting it; it means buying it with information about what the rest of the squad already needs.

## The depth threshold, and what falling below it looks like

Rugby is a contact sport played over a long campaign, and GM mode runs 24 matches with rotation, morale, confidence and job security all moving with results.

Here is the pattern we see most often in squads that fail, and it is not the one people expect. Thin squads do not decline steadily. **They hold up for roughly two-thirds of a season and then come apart.**

The mechanism is straightforward once you see it. Early on, a thin squad plays its best fifteen every week and looks strong. Accumulated fatigue and injury then force rotation into positions where the second option is a long way behind the first. Results dip, morale follows, and the squad is now playing worse players in worse form under more pressure. By the time the problem is visible in the table, there is not enough season left to fix it.

The practical threshold is less exotic than it sounds: **every position needs a credible second option, and the front row needs more than one.** A squad of fifteen excellent players and eight passengers is not a strong squad that is slightly thin. It is a squad with a scheduled failure date.

## Playing a cap properly

GM mode runs under a $200M in-game salary cap, with trades, contracts, free agents, payroll, rotation and matchup preparation all in your hands.

A cap changes the question from *who is best* to *who is worth their price given what I still have to buy*. Two habits help:

- **Price the whole shape before the first pick, not after the fourth.** Decide roughly what proportion of the cap the pack, the half-backs, the kicker and the back three should consume, then treat any deviation as a decision rather than an accident.
- **Treat a bargain as a budget transfer, not a win.** Signing a good prop cheaply is only valuable if you spend the saving somewhere it is needed. A cap left unspent is a squad you chose not to build.

The general principles are the same across the studio's capped modes — [salary cap basics for GM games](/blog/salary-cap-basics-for-gm-games/) covers them — and the distortion that a rotating pool introduces is closest in spirit to the constrained-supply problem in [expansion drafts](/blog/expansion-drafts-explained/), where you are also picking from a board somebody else decided the shape of.
