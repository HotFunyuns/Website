Law 5 of the Laws of Cricket, with a long technical appendix, sets out what a legal bat is made of, how big it can be, and which kinds of bat may be used at which level. MCC's 2026 edition also made one of the biggest equipment changes in years: laminated bats in adult cricket.

## The size limits

Law 5.7 sets the maximum dimensions:

| Measurement | Limit |
| --- | --- |
| Overall length, handle inserted | 38 in / 96.52 cm |
| Blade width | 4.25 in / 10.8 cm |
| Blade depth | 2.64 in / 6.7 cm |
| Edge thickness | 1.56 in / 4.0 cm |
| Handle, for bats larger than size 6 | At most 52% of the overall length |
| Protective covering on the blade | At most 0.04 in / 0.1 cm thick |
| Protective material on the toe | At most 0.12 in / 0.3 cm thick |

Numbers on paper are hard to police in the middle, so every legal bat must also **pass through a bat gauge**. Appendix B gives its aperture: 4.33 in wide, 2.68 in deep in total and 1.61 in at the edge, slightly larger than the blade limits in Law 5.7. A bat must pass through it with or without the protective coverings that Law 5.4 permits.

## What a bat is made of

Law 5 splits a bat into a **handle** and a **blade**:

- The handle must be made principally of cane and/or wood, and its upper part may have a grip.
- The blade must consist **solely of wood**.
- Repairs may insert solid material into the blade, but only wood with minimal adhesive.
- No part of the bat, covered or not, may be so hard or rough that it causes "unacceptable damage" to the ball: anything beyond the normal wear from hitting bare wood.

Wikipedia's article on the cricket bat adds the traditional recipe: a blade of cricket bat willow, a variety of white willow, treated with raw linseed oil, with a cane handle spliced in. It credits the tapered splice design to Charles Richardson in the 1880s.

The wood-only rule has a history. Wikipedia recounts that in 1979 Dennis Lillee briefly used an aluminium ComBat bat; after discussion with the umpires and complaints from the English team, he reverted to wood, and the Laws were soon amended to say the blade must be made entirely of wood. In 2005, Kookaburra withdrew a bat with a carbon-fibre support down its spine after MCC advised the ICC that it was illegal.

## Types A to D

Law 5.8 sorts bats into four categories:

| Type | What is allowed | Where it can be used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | The basic Law 5 bat | Any level of cricket |
| B | Material inserted at the toe and/or along the sides | Levels set by the national governing body |
| C | As B, plus a cloth covering and more non-wood material in the handle | Levels set by the national governing body |
| D | May be laminated and/or coloured | Lower levels, as set by the national governing body |

Bats that fit none of the four are not recognised in the Laws.

## The 2026 change: laminated bats

Appendix B lets a Type D blade be **laminated**, using only wood, from no more than three pieces, with one single piece forming the face. MCC's summary of the ten major 2026 changes explains the reason: Type D bats are now permitted in open-age cricket rather than just junior cricket, as part of an attempt to slow the rising cost of bats. Laminating lets more of the best willow be used, by gluing lower-quality willow behind a high-quality face.

MCC says its testing did not suggest laminated bats give a significant performance advantage, and that it expects top-level bats to remain a single piece of willow. As the Law says, the level at which Type D bats may be used is for each country's governing body.

## Bat limits and the balance between bat and ball

MCC's preface to the Laws names maintaining a fair balance between bat and ball as one of the guiding objectives of the 2017 Code, and says those principles continued to guide the 2026 fourth edition.
