A stock quote packs a dozen numbers into a small space, and the biggest one, the last price, is not necessarily the price you will trade at. This guide goes through a typical quote screen field by field and says what each figure is, where it comes from and what it does not tell you.

## A sample quote

Here is the kind of summary a trading app or financial site shows, for a made-up company, Example Corp (ticker EXMP):

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Last price | $48.20 |
| Change | +$0.70 (+1.47%) |
| Previous close | $47.50 |
| Bid × size | $48.18 × 300 |
| Ask × size | $48.22 × 500 |
| Day range | $47.60 – $48.45 |
| 52-week range | $36.10 – $55.80 |
| Volume | 1,240,000 |
| Market cap | $12.0B |
| P/E ratio | 18.5 |
| Dividend yield | 1.6% |

## Price fields

**Ticker.** The short code that identifies the stock on its exchange, here EXMP.

**Last price.** The price of the most recent trade. It is history, even if only seconds old. It is not a price anyone is offering you. The SEC's investor bulletin on order types stresses that the last-traded price is not necessarily the price at which a market order will execute.

**Change and percent change.** The difference between the last price and the previous close: $48.20 − $47.50 = +$0.70, which is 0.70 ÷ 47.50 ≈ 1.47%.

**Previous close.** The official closing price from the last regular session. FINRA's guide to extended-hours trading notes that the prices recorded at 4 p.m. on the exchanges are the official closing prices for the day, regardless of what happens in after-hours trading.

## The bid and ask: the prices you can actually trade at

Investor.gov defines the **bid** as the highest price a buyer will pay for a specified number of shares, and the **ask** as the lowest price a seller will accept. The number after each, the **size**, is how many shares are available at that price.

In the sample, you could expect to buy near $48.22 and sell near $48.18. The 4-cent difference is the spread, a real cost of trading explained in [the bid–ask spread](/blog/bid-ask-spread-explained/). If you are about to trade, these two numbers matter more than the last price.

## Activity and range

**Volume.** The number of shares that have changed hands so far in the session. Rising volume shows more activity, but by itself it does not say whether buyers or sellers are in control; [trading volume explained](/blog/trading-volume-explained/) covers how it is used and misused.

**Day range.** The lowest and highest trades of the current session.

**52-week range.** The lowest and highest trades over the past year. A price near either end is a fact about the past, not a signal: a stock near its 52-week low can keep falling, and one near its high can keep rising.

## Summary statistics

These are calculated from the price and the company's reported figures.

- **Market cap** is the share price multiplied by the number of shares outstanding, a measure of the company's size in the market's eyes. Investor.gov defines it as the value of a corporation found by multiplying the current market price of one share by the total number of outstanding shares. See [market capitalisation](/blog/market-capitalization-explained/).
- **P/E ratio** divides the price by earnings per share. FINRA describes it as telling you how much investors are paying for a dollar of a company's earnings. It depends on which earnings figure is used, past or forecast, and is not a bargain detector on its own; [the P/E ratio](/blog/pe-ratio-explained/) explains why.
- **Dividend yield** compares annual dividends with the share price. A high yield can reflect a falling price; see [dividend yield](/blog/dividend-yield-explained/).

FINRA's guidance on evaluating stocks suggests comparing such ratios with the market as a whole and with the company's industry, since average ratios can vary significantly across industries.

## Things a quote does not show

- **Whether the data is real-time.** A quote may be delayed. Check the time stamp.
- **Which session it reflects.** Outside 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time, a quote may reflect extended-hours trading, which FINRA notes is less liquid and often more volatile.
- **Anything about the future.** Every number on the screen is either a recent price or a calculation from past results.
