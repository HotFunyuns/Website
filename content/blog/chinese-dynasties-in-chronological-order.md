The conventional order runs Xia, Shang, Zhou, Qin, Han, a long period of division, Sui, Tang, a second division, Song, Yuan, Ming, Qing. That sequence is not what people disagree about. The disagreement is over the dates attached to the early entries, and it matters because the evidence supporting a date in the second millennium BC is a completely different kind of thing from the evidence supporting a date in the fifteenth century AD.

This article gives dates only where they can be tied to something checkable, and says what kind of evidence each one rests on.

## Where the record is firmest: the late Shang at Anyang

The oldest dynasty in the list that is attested by its own writing is the Shang.

UNESCO's World Heritage inscription for Yin Xu, the archaeological site near Anyang City some 500 km south of Beijing, describes it as an ancient capital of the late Shang dynasty and gives the period as 1300–1046 BC. Excavation has produced royal tombs and palaces, a Palace and Royal Ancestral Shrines Area with more than eighty house foundations, and the tomb of Fu Hao — the only tomb of a member of the Shang royal family to have survived intact.

The decisive material is textual. The inscription states that oracle bones found at Yin Xu bear invaluable testimony to the development of one of the world's oldest writing systems, and to ancient beliefs and social systems. These are contemporary documents, produced by the dynasty, found at the dynasty's own capital. That is the strongest evidence type available for any pre-modern state, and it is why the late Shang is where most historians treat the Chinese dynastic record as passing from tradition into attested history.

## Before the list begins

Complex society in China is far older than the dynastic sequence, which is a political framework rather than a history of settlement.

UNESCO dates the Archaeological Ruins of Liangzhu City, in the Yangtze River basin on the south-eastern coast, to roughly 3300–2300 BCE, and describes an early regional state with a unified belief system based on rice cultivation in Late Neolithic China — earthen monuments, urban planning, a water conservation system, and a social hierarchy visible in differentiated burials.

Liangzhu is not part of the dynastic list and was never claimed to be. Its existence is a useful corrective to the idea that Chinese history begins with a named dynasty, and a reminder that a list of regimes is not the same object as a chronology of a region.

## The Xia question, stated honestly

The Xia sits at the head of every traditional list and occupies a different evidential position from everything after it.

The Shang can be read in its own inscriptions. The Xia cannot: it is known from transmitted texts composed long after the period they describe, and any connection to the archaeological record has to be made by identifying particular Bronze Age sites with the dynasty named in those texts. That identification is an interpretation, and specialists disagree about it — some treating the Xia as a historical state not yet securely located, some as a later literary construction, some as a plausible polity whose name and boundaries cannot be recovered.

The honest summary is that the question is open, that it turns on what counts as adequate evidence for identifying an archaeological culture with a named dynasty, and that no reader should be told it has been settled in either direction.

## The Chronology Project and its critics

Between 1995 and 2000, a state-sponsored project in China set out to fix this. As described in a 2024 review in the Cambridge journal *Early China*, the Xia-Shang-Zhou Chronology Project was a five-year effort to determine an absolute chronology for the Western Zhou dynasty and approximate chronologies for the Xia and Shang. It issued a provisional report at the end of the five years and published a promised full report in 2022.

The review, by Edward L. Shaughnessy of the University of Chicago, argues that numerous discoveries made in the twenty-plus years between the two publications show the Project's absolute chronology for the Western Zhou to be fundamentally flawed, that the 2022 report acknowledges some of the problems, and that it nonetheless maintains the Project's chronology without correction. The review discusses four such discoveries — inscribed bronzes including the Lai pan and two Lai ding vessels, and the Jue Gong gui — and closes with a call for an authoritative statement acknowledging the errors.

Two things follow for a general reader. A set of dates can be official and contested at the same time. And when a published chronology is challenged, the challenge usually comes from newly excavated inscriptions rather than from reinterpretation of the same material — which is the ordinary way chronologies get revised, as [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) describes across methods.

## Anchors you can check for the later dynasties

From the Qin onwards, the list can increasingly be tied to things that still stand.

- **Qin.** UNESCO's inscription for the Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor names Qin, who died in 210 BC, as the first unifier of China, buried surrounded by terracotta warriors at the centre of a complex designed to mirror the urban plan of the capital. The site was not found until 1974.
- **Han and Tang.** UNESCO's Silk Roads inscription identifies Chang'an and Luoyang as the central capitals of China under the Han and Tang dynasties, with the overland network west of them taking shape between the second century BC and the first century AD.
- **The division and the Tang.** The Longmen Grottoes are inscribed for containing the largest collection of Chinese art of the late Northern Wei and Tang dynasties, a span UNESCO gives as 316–907.
- **Sui.** The Grand Canal, built in sections from the fifth century BC onwards, was first conceived as a unified imperial communication system in the seventh century AD under the Sui.
- **Yuan.** The Site of Xanadu preserves the remains of Kublai Khan's capital, designed in 1256, from which he established the Yuan dynasty that ruled China for over a century.
- **Ming and Qing.** The Forbidden City is described as the seat of supreme power for over five centuries, from 1416 to 1911, with the Imperial Palace at Shenyang testifying to the foundation of the last dynasty to rule China and to Manchu cultural traditions.

Each of those is a place a reader can look up, which is a different standard of proof from a date in a list.

## What "in order" conceals

A clean sequence implies one regime succeeding another on a fixed date. The reality includes long periods when several states ruled parts of the territory simultaneously, dynasties whose legitimacy was disputed at the time, and transitions that took decades. The Three Kingdoms, the Northern and Southern dynasties and the periods between the Tang and the Song are all cases where "the next dynasty" is a summary rather than a description.

That is why a list is a navigation aid rather than an account. It tells you roughly where to look; it does not tell you what was happening.
