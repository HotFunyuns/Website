Almost everything written about career modes is about getting better. Draft position, training plans, breaking into a lineup, earning a role. Almost nothing is written about the second half, which is longer than people expect and is where most of the interesting decisions live.

This is what the research actually says about when athletes peak and how they decline, and how to use that as a mental model for the back half of a simulated career.

## Peak age is not one number

The first thing to abandon is the idea of a single age at which athletes are best.

An editorial by Tanaka and Toussaint in *Frontiers in Physiology*, reviewing work on growth, peaking and aging in competitive athletes, describes the position plainly: "the age of peak athletic performance varies widely across different sports and between sexes/genders, typically ranging from 20 to 30 years old." A ten-year band is not a rounding error. It reflects genuinely different demands.

How wide the variation gets inside a single sport is visible in a large dataset. Ganse and colleagues analysed 27,088 competitive results from track and field athletes aged 11 to 89 across twelve disciplines and reported that "on average women peak approximately 2 years later than men (21.6 years vs. 19.7 years)," with the 400 metres showing the highest peak age in both and the shorter sprints and jumps peaking earlier.

Two things follow. Peak age tracks what the event asks for, and any model that uses one number for every role is a simplification you should expect to notice.

## Decline is gradual, and it starts earlier than people assume

Faulkner and colleagues, reviewing aging in elite male athletes in the *Clinical Journal of Sport Medicine*, note that "most elite athletes begin to show some decline in performance by their early thirties," while some exceptionally gifted athletes hold an elite level into their forties.

The shape matters as much as the timing. The Ganse analysis found performance declining in a broadly linear way until around age 70, after which the decline in the running events accelerated — while the throwing and jumping disciplines did not accelerate in the same way. Decades of gentle, predictable loss, then a change of regime much later.

For a career mode, that argues against a cliff. A player who is excellent at 30 and unplayable at 32 is not modelling an aging curve; they are modelling a switch.

## Different capacities age differently

This is the part that changes how you play.

Because peak age depends on the demands of the event, it follows that the different capacities inside one athlete are not on the same schedule. Explosive output — the thing sprinting and jumping test — peaks earliest in the track and field data. Events with a larger pacing and judgement component peak later. The editorial's ten-year band across sports is largely a reflection of that mix.

Translated to a baseball career: a player built entirely on speed and raw power is betting on the capacities that come off first. A player who has also accumulated plate vision, pitch selection and situational judgement has assets that hold their value longer, because they are not the same kind of asset.

The practical version of this is a diversification argument. A narrow build produces a spectacular peak and a short one. A broader build peaks lower and stays useful for more seasons, and over a full career the second often ends with the better totals.

## How a career simulation handles it

The relevant design detail is how development is computed. Contact, power, plate vision, speed, baserunning, fielding, arm strength, pitching control, velocity, stamina and baseball IQ all develop by position, performance, age and training — four inputs, not one. Age is in the formula alongside what you did and what you trained, which is why the same training week does not buy the same thing in different seasons, and why a plateau is not automatically an age problem.

That is worth holding onto when something stops working. Our overview of [how player career modes work](/blog/how-player-career-modes-work/) covers the general structure; the diagnostic below is specific to the back half.

## Diagnosing a plateau

When progress stops, check the inputs in this order.

1. **Playing time.** Performance cannot drive development if you are not in the lineup. A drop in appearances is the most common cause and the easiest to miss.
2. **Role match.** A catcher and a closer are asked different questions in the same game, and training that suits one will underperform for the other.
3. **Training focus.** A plan that built a young player's strengths may now be adding to a capacity that has stopped responding, while an underused one would still move.
4. **Health.** Availability is a compounding input, and it interacts with everything above. Our piece on [injuries in career games](/blog/injuries-in-sports-career-games/) covers that interaction.

Only when those four are ruled out is age the explanation, and even then it is a slope rather than a wall.

## Playing the back half well

Three adjustments that tend to pay off once a player is past their best years.

**Trade peak for availability.** Seasons played is a multiplier on everything cumulative. A slightly reduced role that keeps you on the field out-produces an ambitious one that does not.

**Lean into what aged well.** If judgement and situational skill have held up, put the player where those decide outcomes rather than where raw output does.

**Manage the schedule, not just the season.** Pitch counts, rest and the decision to play a key moment in detail or move through it quickly are real levers late in a career, in a way they are not at 23.

## Retirement is a decision about the record

The last thing a career mode asks is when to stop, and it is genuinely a choice rather than a trigger.

A career run leaves behind season history, career totals, awards, playoff appearances and, at the end, Hall of Fame recognition. Those totals are the output of the whole run. Extra seasons at a reduced level add counting statistics and dilute the rate statistics, so the right stopping point depends entirely on which of those you are playing for.

Players chasing hits, home runs or strikeouts should generally play on. Players chasing a peak-heavy profile should stop earlier than feels comfortable. There is no wrong answer, but there is an inconsistent one — playing on while caring about rate statistics is the version that produces regret.
