RPO stands for run-pass option. An RPO and a play-action pass both try to make a defender choose between run and pass before he can know which is coming, but they get there in opposite ways. On play action the decision was made with the play call: the handoff is a fake, and the quarterback throws. On an RPO the decision has not been made at the snap: the run is real, the line blocks it as a run, and the quarterback decides afterwards whether to hand off or throw, usually by watching one defender.

The difference is tactical, and it is also written into the rulebook. On an RPO the linemen are run-blocking while a pass is still possible, and the NFL does not allow an ineligible lineman to be more than one yard downfield before the pass is thrown. The college rulebook allows three. Charting data distributed through the nflverse project treats the two as separate things, with one flag for play-action passes and another for plays "considered run-pass option".

## One snap, seen twice

Picture a linebacker lined up over a slot receiver. On a run toward his side, his job is to fill the gap just outside the offensive tackle. On a pass, his job is the short zone behind him. He cannot do both at once, and both concepts are built to exploit that. Call him the conflict defender: a player whose two assignments pull in opposite directions.

### Snap one: play action

1. The ball is snapped and the quarterback turns to extend it toward the running back.
2. The back closes around the fake as if he has the ball. Under the NFL rule, a player to whom a teammate pretends to give the ball may be tackled until he leaves the pocket area, so the defence is entitled to treat the fake as real while the back is inside the pocket area.
3. The linebacker sees run and takes a step toward his gap. That step is what the play was called for.
4. The offensive linemen are bound by the same downfield rule as on any pass: no more than a yard past the line until the ball is thrown.
5. The quarterback turns back to the field and throws into the zone the linebacker just vacated, or behind defenders who came up for the run.

The decision on play action is made before the snap. What the quarterback reads afterwards is where to throw, not whether to throw. The cost is time: he spends part of the play with his back to the defence, so his protection has to hold for longer than on a quick pass.

### Snap two: run-pass option

1. The ball is snapped and the line blocks a run, firing forward at the defenders in front of them.
2. The quarterback holds the option to give the ball to the back or keep it and throw, and his eyes go to the linebacker over the slot.
3. If the linebacker fills his run gap, the quarterback pulls the ball and throws to the slot receiver in the space the linebacker left.
4. If the linebacker stays in coverage, the quarterback hands off, and the run now has one fewer defender to block at the point of attack.
5. Either way the decision has to come quickly, because the linemen are blocking a run while a pass is still possible, and the rule limits how far they can go before the ball is thrown.

On an RPO, the quarterback reads the defence instead of the defence reading the offence. The linebacker is wrong whichever way he goes, provided the quarterback reads him correctly and the ball comes out on time.

## The two concepts side by side

| | Play action | Run-pass option |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What the run is | A fake | Real, unless the quarterback pulls the ball |
| When run or pass is decided | Before the snap, in the call | After the snap, by the quarterback |
| What the line is doing | Protecting a pass | Blocking a run |
| Who is reading whom | The defence reads the backfield action | The quarterback reads one defender |
| Where the throw goes | Behind defenders who stepped up for the run | Into the space the read defender left |
| What can go wrong | Protection breaks while the quarterback is turned away | The read is slow and a lineman drifts too far downfield |

## The rules that shape each one

### Linemen downfield: the RPO's constraint

| | NFL, 2026 rulebook (8-3-1) | College, 2026 rules book (7-3-10) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How far an ineligible player may be | No more than one yard beyond the line | No more than three yards beyond the neutral zone |
| How it is measured | The entire body must be past the limit | Any part of the body past the limit |
| When it applies | On a play during which a legal forward pass is thrown, until the throw | Until a legal forward pass that crosses the neutral zone is thrown |
| Penalty | Five yards from the previous spot | Five yards from the previous spot |

The NFL rule has an important qualification. A lineman who makes contact with an opponent within one yard of the line during his initial charge may be carried further while he is still legally blocking. What he cannot do is release into the open field, or keep going after losing contact, before the ball is thrown.

Read together, the two codes explain why the same concept behaves differently at each level. A college lineman can work up to three yards downfield while his quarterback is still deciding; a professional one has a yard. That puts the NFL version of the read on a tighter clock. Receivers face a separate constraint: under the NFL rule, an offensive player who blocks more than a yard downfield clearly before a pass is thrown commits offensive pass interference, a 10-yard penalty, so the run-blocking and pass-catching parts of an RPO have to stay out of each other's way until the ball is gone.

### Tackling the faker: play action's price

The rule that lets defenders tackle a player who pretends to have the ball applies until he leaves the pocket area, which the rulebook defines as the space between the outside edges of the normal tackle positions, extending back to the offence's end line. A good fake therefore has a physical cost as well as a tactical one. The back who sells it can be hit for his trouble.

## Which quarterback each suits

An RPO rewards quick decisions and accurate short throws, because the window closes as soon as the linemen reach their limit. Play action rewards a quarterback who can sell the fake with his back to the defence and still deliver from the pocket. A quarterback who is also a running threat makes both harder to defend, because the read defender has one more thing to account for.
