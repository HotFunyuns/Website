Here is the case for what-if questions, and it is stronger than their reputation suggests: **you cannot claim anything caused anything without one.**

Say that a particular decision helped win a war. You have just asserted that without it the war would have gone differently — a counterfactual, embedded in an ordinary historical sentence. Niall Ferguson's defence of the method rests on exactly this point. Historians produce counterfactuals constantly; the only question is whether they do it explicitly, where it can be examined, or implicitly, where it cannot.

That is the strongest argument for the method. The arguments against it are also strong, and worth having in full before enjoying any simulation of this kind, including ours.

## What counterfactual history actually is

It is a form of historiography that attempts to answer the what-if questions arising from counterfactual conditions. Remove or alter one specified thing about the past, then ask what follows.

The purpose is **assessment of causal weight.** If removing a factor changes everything downstream, that factor was load-bearing. If removing it changes little, it was not — however prominent it looked at the time. The exercise is a way of testing how much explanatory work a given cause is doing.

Critically, a counterfactual is anchored to a specific, identifiable change. "What if the Roman Empire had been run better" is not a counterfactual; it is a mood. "What if the eastern and western administrations had not been divided" is one, because you can say precisely what has been altered and reason about what depended on it.

## Why it is not alternate history fiction

The two are often conflated and they do different jobs.

**Counterfactual history** focuses on evaluating the historical importance of the negated event, with the broad subsequent changes outlined. It stops when the assessment is done.

**Alternate history fiction** takes the same starting point and develops it: specific characters, specific events, a narrative built out of the writer's invention. That is a creative activity, and a perfectly good one, but the evidential constraint has been released.

The distinction is the stopping point. A counterfactual that keeps going past the assessment has become fiction, and the transition is easy to make without noticing — which is one of the reasons critics distrust the method.

## The criticisms, stated properly

Three objections, and none of them is easily dismissed.

**Wishful thinking.** Richard Evans has argued that most counterfactuals are written by right-wing historians engaging in wishful thinking. Strip the politics and a general point remains: a counterfactual has no data to discipline it, so the historian's preferences can shape the outcome without resistance. You find what you were hoping to find, and nothing in the method pushes back.

**Single-cause reduction.** Evans also objects that reducing outcomes to a single cause ignores the complexity of influences on outcomes. Real historical outcomes are overdetermined — several sufficient causes often operate at once — so removing one and watching everything change may simply misrepresent how tangled the causation was.

**The impossible standard.** Aviezer Tucker rejected multi-causal counterfactuals, insisting that each should be posed with all other variables held constant. That is the right methodological demand and it is close to unsatisfiable, because historical variables are not independent. Change one and you have changed the conditions for the others.

Behind all three sits the mainstream position: counterfactual history is perhaps entertaining but does not meet the standards of mainstream historical research, because it is speculative.

**We think that is basically correct, and it is the position we design around.**

## Where the method is genuinely useful

Three uses survive the criticisms.

**Making implicit claims explicit.** If an argument depends on a counterfactual, stating it lets someone check it. The concealed version cannot be checked at all.

**Testing how contingent an outcome was.** Some outcomes are robust — remove any single cause and they still happen. Others turn on one decision. Distinguishing the two is a real historical question, and it is one the debates over [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/) are substantially about: was the western empire doomed by structural conditions, or did specific contingent events tip it?

**Teaching.** Asking a learner what they would have done, with the actual constraints and information available at the time, is a far more demanding exercise than asking what happened. It forces engagement with the situation rather than with the outcome — which is the main thing hindsight takes away from you.

## How Time Travel Mode handles it, and what we refused to model

[World History Timeline Sim](/apps/world-history-timeline-sim/) is built around this problem, so it is worth being specific about the design.

Time Travel Mode drops you into a turning point. It gives you the **historical context and what actually happened**, offers realistic decision options with their trade-offs, then plays out consequences as a chain of effects across war, diplomacy, science, culture, economy and technology, with a Butterfly Effect Score attached. There are over 2,000 what-if scenarios, and the Rewrite History Campaign runs through the eras while the Daily Time Jump lands you somewhere unplanned.

Three decisions about what the model does *not* do matter more than the ones about what it does.

**We do not compute a single correct alternative history.** The consequence chain is one plausible line, and the app presents it as one. Producing "what would have happened" would require exactly the certainty the criticisms above say nobody has.

**We do not attach probabilities.** It would be trivial to print a percentage next to each outcome and it would be fabricated precision. There is no distribution to sample from. A number would make the output feel more rigorous while making it less honest, and that is the worst trade available.

**We do not extend chains indefinitely.** Consequences resolve across a bounded set of domains and then stop. Every additional step compounds speculation, and a hundred-step chain tells you about the model rather than about history. Where the chain ends is a judgement call and we have deliberately ended it early.

**And we do not model the later lives of individual real people** as a consequence of a changed decision. That is the point at which a counterfactual becomes fiction about real human beings, and it is a line we are not interested in crossing in a product aimed partly at students.

The vocabulary is chosen accordingly. These are **scenarios**, not predictions, and the mode gives you the real context and the real outcome before it gives you the options — so the counterfactual is always framed against what actually happened rather than replacing it.

The Butterfly Effect Score deserves a note of its own. It is a game mechanic measuring how far a chosen line diverges from the recorded one within the model. It is not a measure of historical plausibility, and reading it as one would be a misuse of it. We would rather say that here than have it inferred.

## Using it well

If you are using a what-if scenario to learn rather than to play:

- **Read the real outcome first**, properly, before choosing. The mode gives it to you for a reason.
- **Ask what the decision-maker actually knew.** Most counterfactuals collapse once you notice the chosen option required information nobody had.
- **Treat the consequence chain as a prompt, not an answer.** The useful question is "is that plausible, and why", not "so that is what would have happened".
- **Go and read something.** The scenario has done its job if it makes you want the argument, and the argument is in books — [how to study history on your own](/blog/how-to-study-history-on-your-own/) covers getting at it.
