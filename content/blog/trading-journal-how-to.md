A brokerage statement tells you what you traded. It cannot tell you why, what you expected, or what you would do differently. That is the job of a **trading journal**. The description of our own simulator, Learn to Trade Stocks Beginner, puts the habit in one line: compare the result with your reason for buying or selling. A journal is where you write the reason down before the trade, so there is something to compare against afterwards.

## What to record for every trade

| Field | Example | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Date and time | 6 Oct, 10:42 a.m. | Context: news, session, day of week |
| Symbol, side, quantity | XYZ, buy, 20 shares | The basic facts |
| Order type and time-in-force | Limit, day order | Explains fills, or the lack of them |
| Fill price and fees | $50.00, $0 commission | Needed for basis and true results |
| Reason | "Bounced off support at $49 twice" | The hypothesis you are testing |
| Planned exit | Target $58, stop $46 | Written before entry, not after |
| Size and risk | $1,000 position, $80 at risk | Keeps any one loss small |
| Result | −$84 | The outcome |
| Lesson | "Stop filled below $46 after a gap" | What changes next time |

[Time-in-force order types](/blog/time-in-force-order-types/) and [stop versus stop-limit orders](/blog/stop-order-vs-stop-limit-order/) explain the order fields.

## Write the exit before the entry

The most useful line in a journal is the one written before the trade: what would make you sell. Deciding a target and a stop in advance turns a vague hope into a testable plan, and it fixes your risk before emotion gets a vote.

**A worked entry.** Suppose a $10,000 practice balance, and you buy 20 shares at $50.00, with a stop at $46 and a target at $58.

- Risk per share: $50 − $46 = $4; risk on the trade: $4 × 20 = **$80**, or 0.8% of the account.
- Potential reward: $58 − $50 = $8 a share, twice the risk.
- What happened: the stock gapped down and the stop order filled at $45.80, a loss of **$84**.

The extra $4 is itself a lesson. The SEC explains that a stop price is not a guaranteed execution price: once triggered, a stop becomes a market order, and the fill can deviate significantly from the stop. The journal records the difference so you learn to expect it.

## Reviewing in batches

Single trades are noisy. Review in batches, such as weekly or every 10 to 20 trades, and calculate a few numbers:

- **Win rate:** the share of trades that made money.
- **Average win and average loss.**
- **Expectancy:** win rate × average win − loss rate × average loss, the average result per trade.

Win rate on its own misleads:

| Ten trades | Win rate | Average win | Average loss | Expectancy per trade | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trader A | 40% | $120 | $60 | +$12 | +$120 |
| Trader B | 60% | $50 | $100 | −$10 | −$100 |

Trader B wins more often and still loses money, because the losses are twice the size of the wins. Include **fees** in every figure: FINRA notes that frequent trading can come with higher costs that might erode your returns, as well as potential tax implications.

## Separate the decision from the outcome

A well-planned trade can lose and a reckless one can win. Grade each entry twice: did I follow my plan, and did the trade make money? A string of rule-following losers may mean the rule needs work; a winning trade that broke your rules is a warning, not a success. The app's listing adds a caution about practice records: simulated results cannot predict what will happen in the real market; [what paper trading can and cannot teach](/blog/what-is-paper-trading/) covers the limits.

## A journal is also a tax record

For real trades, the records have a second use. IRS Topic 305 says to keep records relating to property until the period of limitations expires for the year in which you dispose of it, because you need them to figure your basis and your gain or loss. The general limitation period for assessing tax is three years from filing, and longer in some situations, such as six years for substantially under-reported income.

Purchase dates and prices settle questions that statements do not always answer, such as whether a sale was short- or long-term, covered in [capital gains on stocks](/blog/capital-gains-tax-on-stocks/), and whether a repurchase in another account created a [wash sale](/blog/wash-sale-rule-explained/). The SEC also advises checking every account statement and trade confirmation and contacting your firm immediately, by phone and in writing, about errors or trades you did not authorise.
