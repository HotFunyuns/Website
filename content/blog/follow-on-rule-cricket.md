Normally the two sides in a Test take turns: A bats, B bats, A bats again, B bats again. The **follow-on** breaks that order. If the side batting second falls far enough behind on first innings, the captain of the side that batted first can send them straight back in. It is set out in Law 14 of the Laws of Cricket, in MCC's 2026 edition, and Wikipedia explains its purpose: to reduce the chance of a draw by letting the stronger side finish the match sooner.

## The minimum leads

Law 14 sets the lead needed by the length of the match:

| Match length | Minimum first-innings lead |
| --- | --- |
| 5 days or more | 200 runs |
| 3 or 4 days | 150 runs |
| 2 days | 100 runs |
| 1 day | 75 runs |

The ICC's October 2026 Test playing conditions use the same 200-run figure for five-day Tests, and its four-day Test conditions use 150.

Two procedural points matter. The captain must tell the opposing captain and the umpires, and **once notified, the decision cannot be changed**. And if no play is possible on the first day, Law 14 counts the match length from the day play actually starts: a five-day Test that loses its first day is treated as a four-day match, so 150 runs is enough.

## A worked example

Side A makes 450 in its first innings. Side B replies with 230.

- **Lead:** 450 − 230 = 220 runs, at least 200, so A's captain may enforce the follow-on.
- **If enforced,** B bats again immediately. If B makes 300 second time round, its two innings total 530, so A needs 81 to win: 530 − 450 + 1.
- **If declined,** A bats again, extends its lead and chooses when to declare, leaving B to bat last.

## Why a captain may decline

Wikipedia lists what a captain weighs: the score, the apparent strength of the two sides, the weather, the pitch and the time remaining. Its discussion of the choice comes down to three questions:

1. **Are my bowlers fresh?** Enforcing means bowling two innings back to back, which Wikipedia calls tiring.
2. **Will the second innings be harder?** Wikipedia notes it can be more difficult to dismiss a team in their second innings than in their first.
3. **What about the draw?** Preventing a draw is the main reason to enforce: Wikipedia explains that a side batting last can bat cautiously to use up time, and the follow-on makes that harder. Declining lowers the leading side's risk of losing, but raises the chance of a draw.

Wikipedia's article shows both choices working in one series, India's 2017 tour of Sri Lanka:

| Test | India's choice | Scores | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First | Captain Virat Kohli declined the follow-on | India batted again before bowling Sri Lanka out | India won by 304 runs |
| Second | Follow-on enforced | India 622/9 declared; Sri Lanka 183 and 386 | India won by an innings and 53 runs |

The innings margin is simple arithmetic: Sri Lanka's two innings made 183 + 386 = 569, which is 53 short of India's single innings of 622, so India never needed to bat again.

## The four Tests won after following on

Wikipedia records only four Tests won by the side following on:

| Test | Winner following on | Margin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sydney, 1894–95 | England v Australia | 10 runs |
| Headingley, 1981 | England v Australia | 18 runs |
| Kolkata (Eden Gardens), 2001 | India v Australia | 171 runs |
| Wellington, 2023 | New Zealand v England | 1 run |

At Headingley, England's second innings left Australia needing 130, and Bob Willis's 8 for 43 bowled them out for 111. At Kolkata, Australia, unbeaten in their previous 16 Tests, made 445 and bowled India out for 171. Following on, India made 657 for 7, built on V. V. S. Laxman's 281 and Rahul Dravid's 180, then bowled Australia out for 212.
