Three things decide whether a vertical shooter is worth learning on a phone, and none of them is the ship roster. Can you see the bullets against the background? Does your own finger cover the thing you are trying to steer? And does the frame rate hold when the screen fills up — which is the only moment the answer matters?

Everything else — sectors, upgrade trees, ship variety — is the part you enjoy after those three have already decided whether you will still be playing in a week.

## The genre, briefly

Shoot 'em ups — shmups, or STGs in Japan — are a subgenre of action games, and there has never been full agreement about the boundaries: some definitions restrict it to spacecraft and particular movement styles, others admit characters on foot and a range of perspectives. The vertical scrolling variety is the narrow, well-behaved centre of that: the field scrolls downward, your craft sits low, enemies arrive from above in formations.

It is also, almost by accident, the genre best suited to a phone held upright. A tall portrait screen is a bad shape for most games and the correct shape for this one.

## The five checks, and how to run them in one session

| Criterion | What good looks like | How to test it in one run | Verification source | Last checked |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bullet contrast | Enemy fire reads instantly against every background, including bright ones | Reach a stage with a busy or light background and see whether projectiles still pop | Bullet hell genre conventions, Wikipedia | 15 September 2026 |
| Hitbox honesty | Collision is confined to a small part of your craft, not the whole sprite | Deliberately clip a wing through a bullet edge and see whether you survive | Bullet hell, Wikipedia — hitbox convention introduced in Batsugun, 1993 | 15 September 2026 |
| Control offset | Dragging moves the ship relative to your finger, so your hand never covers it | Place your thumb low on the screen and check the ship moves without being under it | First-party testing criteria, Reign Creative | 15 September 2026 |
| Frame stability under load | No visible dip when the screen fills during a boss phase | Ignore stage one; judge during a multi-phase boss | First-party testing criteria, Reign Creative | 15 September 2026 |
| Failure clarity | You can tell what killed you, every time | After each death, ask whether you know which projectile hit you | Shoot 'em up genre overview, Wikipedia | 15 September 2026 |

The last row is the one people skip and the one that predicts whether you keep playing. A shooter where deaths feel arbitrary is a shooter you stop learning, because there is nothing to learn from. A shooter where you always know what hit you is one you will replay a stage to beat, even if it is harder.

## Small hitboxes are the genre working, not the genre cheating

New players sometimes read a small hitbox as a fudge. It is the opposite: it is the mechanism that makes the format possible at all.

Confining collision to a fraction of the player's craft rather than the entire vessel was introduced in 1993 with Batsugun, explicitly to make increasingly complex bullet patterns fair, and it has remained a tenet of the genre since. Once you know it, the whole thing reads differently — you stop steering a spaceship and start steering a point that happens to have a spaceship drawn around it, and gaps that looked impossible become obviously passable.

Games differ in how honest they are about where that point is. Some draw it. Some leave it implicit. Either is fine; what is not fine is a hitbox that behaves inconsistently, which is the fastest way to make deaths feel unfair.

The distinction between a dense-pattern bullet hell and a classic formation shooter matters here too, because they ask for different skills — [bullet hell versus classic shmup](/blog/bullet-hell-vs-classic-shmup/) sets out which is which, and it is worth knowing which one you are shopping for before you judge a game for being too busy or too sparse.

## What we prioritise, and what we gave up for it

[Space Galaxy Attack Hardcore](/apps/space-galaxy-attack-hardcore/) is a vertical shooter with anime and manga-inspired artwork, and the art style created the exact problem this article is about: a detailed, colourful background is lovely and it is also the enemy of bullet readability.

Three decisions came out of that.

**Contrast is ranked above art.** When a projectile and a background element compete, the projectile wins. That means some of our sector backgrounds — the neon cities especially — are less saturated than the concept art was, and the asteroid fields are darker than they "should" be. We would rather lose some visual punch than have you die to a bullet you could not see.

**The frame budget is protected during boss phases.** Multi-phase galaxy bosses carry destructible modules and weak points, which is exactly when the screen has the most going on. Effects that cost frames get cut in those windows rather than in the quiet stretches, because a smooth average with a dip in the boss fight is the wrong shape — the dip lands precisely where precision matters. If you want the general version of this argument, [frame rate and resolution explained](/blog/frame-rate-and-resolution-explained/) covers why stability beats peak numbers.

**Controls are offset drag, not a virtual pad.** Touch input for this genre is not a hard problem as long as you accept the constraint that a finger is opaque. [Auto-fire and touch controls](/blog/auto-fire-and-touch-controls/) goes into why automatic firing is close to mandatory on a touchscreen and what it changes about the design of a run.

The honest cost of all three: the game looks slightly flatter in screenshots than it does in motion, and screenshots are what most people judge from. That is a trade we made knowingly and it is a fair thing to hold against us.

## The things a store listing can tell you, and the things it cannot

Before installing anything, the listing gives you the "Contains ads" and "In-app purchases" labels and the Data safety section, and that is roughly the limit. There is no field for bullet readability, no field for control scheme, and no field for whether the game holds 60 frames per second on a mid-range phone in 2026.

Our own listing states that the game is free, supported by adverts, and does not offer in-app purchases — which is a fact about the monetisation model rather than a claim about quality, and it is checkable in about four seconds. What it does not tell you is anything in the table above.

Which is the point: this genre has to be judged by playing it, and one run is enough. If offline behaviour is part of your decision, the airplane-mode method in [what "offline" actually means on Android](/blog/best-offline-arcade-games-android/) settles that in about ten minutes as well.

## A sensible order to decide in

1. Install one candidate and play a single run to a boss.
2. Judge contrast, control offset and frame stability. Nothing else yet.
3. Die on purpose once and check whether you know what killed you.
4. Only if all four pass, look at ships, modes and progression.
5. If you are travelling, run the airplane-mode test before you rely on it.

The order matters because steps 1–3 take five minutes and step 4 takes hours. There is no sense evaluating an upgrade tree in a game whose bullets you cannot see.
