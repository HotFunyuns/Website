The scholars gathered under the heading "Islamic Golden Age" wrote specific books, and reading what is actually in them is more interesting than either of the two summaries usually on offer — that the period merely preserved Greek learning for Europe, or that it invented everything. Al-Khwarizmi wrote a manual of equation-solving aimed at inheritance disputes and trade. Ibn Sina wrote a medical encyclopaedia that organised the whole field. Ibn al-Haytham argued that vision works by light entering the eye and set out how to test it. Ibn Khaldun wrote a theory of why states rise and fall. Those are four different projects, four centuries apart in places, and lumping them together is where most of the confusion starts.

## The label, and why it is argued about

Two problems with "Islamic Golden Age", both worth stating before the names.

The first is geography. Al-Khwarizmi worked in Baghdad. Ibn Sina was born near Bukhara in Central Asia and died at Hamadan in what is now Iran. Ibn al-Haytham did his optical work in Cairo under the Fatimids — a rival caliphate. Ibn Khaldun was born in Tunis in 1332 and wrote his major work in the 1370s. A Baghdad-centred golden age places most of these people in the wrong city and several of them in the wrong century.

The second is the built-in decline. A golden age has to end, and the usual ending — that religious conservatism strangled enquiry sometime after the eleventh century — has been argued against directly. George Saliba's *Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance* traces continuing and original astronomical work long after the supposed collapse, and connects it to what European astronomers were doing later.

The House of Wisdom has a similar problem. The familiar account, which the MacTutor History of Mathematics Archive gives in its biography of al-Khwarizmi, describes an academy founded by the caliph al-Ma'mun where Greek scientific and philosophical works were translated, with a library assembled alongside it. Dimitri Gutas's study of the translation movement argues that the institution itself was much narrower than the legend, and that the astonishing scale of the translation effort is better explained by demand running right through Abbasid elite society than by any single foundation. Both positions are in print; what a reader should not do is treat the legend as settled.

## Al-Khwarizmi, and a book with a practical purpose

Even the basic facts are contested. MacTutor gives his dates as roughly 780 to 850 and then spends several paragraphs on the disagreement about where he came from — whether the name points to Khwarazm south of the Aral Sea, whether an epithet in al-Tabari indicates Zoroastrian ancestry, and whether that epithet refers to al-Khwarizmi at all or to a second person whose name was merged with his by a copying error. The archive's own comment on one reconstruction is that it was built "with naive confidence" on an error.

That is a useful reminder about biography at this distance: the evidence is a handful of later references, and confident life stories are usually inference dressed up.

What survives is the work. His treatise on *al-jabr* and *al-muqabala* — restoring and balancing — gives English the word algebra; the Latinised form of his name gives us algorithm. MacTutor describes it, carefully, as a text that can in a certain sense be considered the first book written on algebra, which is a more defensible claim than the flat superlative you usually see.

What was genuinely new in it was not the solving of individual problems, which older traditions had done, but the systematic reduction of problems to a small number of standard forms, each with a general procedure and a geometric justification. And the purpose was stated plainly. In Rosen's translation, quoted by MacTutor, al-Khwarizmi says he intended to teach what is easiest and most useful in arithmetic, such as men constantly require in cases of inheritance, legacies, partition, lawsuits, and trade. Islamic inheritance law generates hard fractional problems; a general method for solving them was worth having.

## Ibn Sina, and an autobiography that is also an argument

Ibn Sina — Avicenna in the Latin tradition — was born in 980 near Bukhara and died at Hamadan in June 1037. He lived through the collapse of the Samanid dynasty and worked for a succession of unstable courts, which is part of why his career reads as a series of relocations.

His *Canon of Medicine* organised medical knowledge into a structured whole: general principles, simple drugs, diseases by part of the body, diseases affecting the whole body, and compound remedies. It was used as a teaching text in Europe for centuries after its translation into Latin. His *Book of Healing* did something comparable for philosophy and the sciences.

The provenance point here is unusually neat. We know a great deal about his life because he wrote an autobiography and a student continued it. MacTutor's caution is exactly right: the autobiography is not simply an account of a life but a demonstration of how a person reaches the ultimate truth, and it has to be interpreted accordingly. A first-person source is not automatically a transparent one — which is the general lesson of [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) applied to the friendliest kind of evidence there is, and the reason [how we know what historical figures actually did](/blog/how-we-know-what-historical-figures-did/) treats autobiography as a category needing its own handling.

## Al-Biruni and Ibn al-Haytham: measuring and testing

Al-Biruni, working in Khwarazm and later in Ghazna, produced work on astronomy, chronology, mineralogy and geodesy, and a long comparative study of India written after he travelled there in the entourage of a conqueror. That book is remarkable for its method: he learned Sanskrit, read Indian texts, and set out to describe Indian religion and science on its own terms while saying openly where he disagreed. Comparative study by someone attached to an invading army is an awkward provenance, and he is unusually candid about it.

Ibn al-Haytham's *Book of Optics* argued against the then-dominant view that vision works by something emitted from the eye, and in favour of light entering the eye from objects. What makes it a landmark is less the conclusion than the way he gets there: controlled arrangements with apertures and dark rooms, designed to discriminate between hypotheses rather than to illustrate one.

## Ibn Khaldun, four centuries later and a continent away

Ibn Khaldun, born in Tunis in 1332 and dead in 1406, belongs to a different world entirely. The *Muqaddimah* — the long introduction to his universal history, completed in the 1370s — set out to explain historical change rather than narrate it. His central concept, *asabiyya*, describes the group solidarity that lets a coalition seize power and then dissipates over generations of settled rule until the dynasty is vulnerable to the next one.

Put him in a list headed "Islamic Golden Age" and you are implying a continuity with ninth-century Baghdad that does not exist. Put him where he belongs — post-plague North Africa, a career of court service and exile, a man who had watched dynasties fail at close range — and the argument of the book becomes legible.

## What this looks like as a network

There is one thing the label gets right. Ideas, books and people moved across a very large connected zone, and they moved because that zone had roads, ports, a shared administrative language and scholars who travelled for employment. Indian numerals reached Baghdad; Greek texts were translated into Arabic, often via Syriac; Arabic works were translated into Latin in Iberia and Sicily. The infrastructure described in [the Silk Road for beginners](/blog/the-silk-road-for-beginners/) is the same infrastructure, used for manuscripts instead of silk.

## The version worth remembering

Five people, six centuries, at least four separate states, one shared written language, and a set of books you can still read. The transmission story is true as far as it goes — a very great deal of Greek learning does reach modern readers through Arabic — and it is not the interesting part. The interesting part is what these writers did with material once they had it, and that is only visible if you go and look at what each of them actually wrote.
