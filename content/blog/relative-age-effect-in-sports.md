**The relative age effect is the tendency for players born early in a selection year to be over-represented in youth sport, above all in selected and representative teams, compared with players born late in it.** Youth competitions usually group children by birth year, so two teammates can be almost a full year apart. Meta-analyses spanning many sports find the effect is widespread, usually small on average, strongest among adolescent boys at regional and national level, and weaker in female sport. It shrinks at senior level, though it does not always disappear.

## How a cut-off date creates an advantage

Annual age groups need a cut-off date. The IOC, FIFA and the six continental football confederations use 1 January, while 1 September is used in the UK.

The arithmetic is blunt. With a 1 January cut-off, a child born on 1 January and one born on 31 December of the same year play in the same age group, 364 days apart. For two nine-year-olds, that gap is more than a tenth of a lifetime.

Researchers usually split each cohort into birth quartiles: Q1 covers the first three months after the cut-off and Q4 the last three. If births were spread evenly and selection ignored birth date, each quartile would supply about a quarter of a squad. The headline statistic is an odds ratio comparing Q1 with Q4. A value of 2 means roughly two Q1 players for every Q4 player, where an even spread would give one of each.

Why the relatively older get picked more often is not mysterious. Researchers describe them as often having anthropometric, physiological and psychosocial advantages at youth level, and as being favoured, consciously or not, by scouts and coaches. Being selected then tends to build competence and confidence, so an early edge can compound into a larger one.

## What the meta-analyses found

| Review | What it pooled | Headline finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cobley and colleagues, 2009, *Sports Medicine* | 38 studies from 1984 to 2007: 253 samples, 14 sports, 16 countries | Consistent but small effects that grew with the size of the age gap; adolescent males aged 15 to 18 at regional and national representative level in highly popular sports were most affected |
| Smith and colleagues, 2018, *Sports Medicine* | 57 studies, 308 samples, 25 sports, all female | A significant but small effect (Q1 against Q4 odds ratio 1.25, or 1.21 adjusted), larger at ages 11 and under and 12 to 14, at higher competition levels, and in physically demanding sports |
| Brustio and colleagues, 2026, *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* | 26 rugby union and rugby league studies covering 318,663 male and 41,379 female players | Males: 34.8% born in Q1 against 16.9% in Q4 (odds ratio 1.99), stronger in youth (2.42) than senior rugby (1.41). Females: 27.5% against 23.6% (1.15) |

Three readings of that table matter more than any single figure in it.

**Small on average, large in places.** An overall odds ratio of 1.25 is a modest tilt. The same literature finds much steeper tilts in particular age groups and at selective levels, which is exactly where selection decisions are made. In male youth rugby, the odds ratio was 2.09 for under-7 to under-14 players and 2.18 for under-15 to under-17s, falling to 1.52 for under-18 to under-20s.

**Published averages can overstate.** In the rugby review, a standard correction for publication bias cut the pooled male estimate from 1.99 to 1.32, and the authors stress that studies differed substantially from one another. The effect is real; its exact size is less certain than one number suggests.

**Selection amplifies it, probably.** Among youth males in rugby, the over-representation of Q1 players was descriptively larger at national than at regional or local level, but the difference between levels was not statistically significant. The authors read it as selection pressure likely amplifying the disparity, which is a reasonable interpretation rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

## Hockey: the obvious explanation that did not hold

If the effect is about size, a sport with heavy contact looks like the obvious amplifier. Ontario minor hockey offered a test when Hockey Canada changed its body-checking rules. Hancock and colleagues compared relative age effects among nine-year-olds in games with and without body-checking, and followed players moving from a season that allowed checking to one that prohibited it.

The effect was there either way: Q1 players made up between 27% and 39% of the samples and Q4 players between 10% and 20%. Reductions after checking was banned were limited to some middle quartiles. The authors concluded that body-checking is not a critical mechanism, and pointed instead to the physicality of the sport in general and to accumulated experience. Removing the sport's most visible form of contact left the birth-quartile pattern intact.

## When the advantage fades: the underdog question

The rugby review discusses what researchers call the underdog hypothesis. Relatively younger players who survive early selection may, through years of competing at a disadvantage, develop stronger technical and psychological skills, while the anthropometric and physiological edge of the relatively older may prove transient once growth evens out.

The data are consistent with part of that story: the effect was weaker in senior than in youth rugby. But it persisted into senior levels, and a smaller adult effect could also reflect who stays in the sport. Treat the hypothesis as a plausible explanation the authors discuss, not as a finding that late-born players end up better.

## Fixes, and how strong the evidence is

**Present age information where the eye can use it.** Mann and van Ginneken asked talent scouts from an elite football club to watch junior games and rank players on potential. Scouts with no age information showed a selection bias towards the relatively older, and the bias remained when they were given players' birthdates. When scouts knew that shirt numbers corresponded to relative age, the bias was eliminated. The limits matter: it was one controlled study with one club's scouts, and it measured rankings rather than careers. It does suggest that knowing a birthdate is not the same as adjusting for it while watching.

**Bio-banding.** Instead of grouping by birth year, bio-banding groups adolescents by estimated biological maturity, most often by the percentage of predicted adult height they have reached. A 2026 systematic review by Han and colleagues found 13 studies involving 861 youth soccer players; nine estimated maturity with the Khamis–Roche predicted-adult-height method. In bio-banded games, late-maturing players showed better decision-making and composure and more involvement in winning the ball, while early maturers were less dominant. The review's own verdict is cautious: the evidence relies on short-term match responses and proxy outcomes, not direct evidence of more accurate selection or better long-term development. Maturity is also a different measurement from birth date, since a player can be relatively young and early-maturing or the reverse, so bio-banding targets the maturation side of the problem rather than relative age as such.

**Other interventions.** Adjusted cut-off dates, wider age bands, birth quotas and average-team-age rules have also been tried. The studies cited here do not evaluate them, so this article offers no verdict on them.

## Reading a youth pathway with this in mind

[Football Career Soccer XI Sim](/apps/soccer-career-sim-xi/) runs a fictional footballer's career from youth football through the academy, university football and turning professional, with minutes earned through Coach Trust and attributes such as pace, strength, passing and vision developed along the way. Nothing in the game's description says it models birth dates or physical maturation, so there is no birth-month mechanic to look for. What the research offers is a way of reading any youth pathway, real or simulated.

**Judge the trajectory, not the snapshot.** A ranking taken at 13 mixes ability with timing. The same caution applies at the other end of a career, where [ageing curves](/blog/aging-curves-in-career-sims/) rather than birth dates shape what a single season shows.

**Small tilts compound across stages.** Suppose, purely for illustration, that Q1 players were 1.2 times as likely as Q4 players to clear each of three selection stages. After all three, the ratio would be 1.2 × 1.2 × 1.2 ≈ 1.73: a modest per-stage bias becomes a large one at the top of the funnel. That is the same multiplication of probabilities across independent steps described in [understanding probability in sports simulations](/blog/understanding-sports-sim-probability/), and it fits the pattern of larger effects at more selective levels.

**A pool of survivors hides the filter.** Draft games such as [24-0 Rugby Draft Pro League](/apps/rugby-draft-pro-league/) build their boards from real players' careers across eras, with ratings that are the game's own estimates. Every name on such a board already made it through youth selection; the players the filter screened out never reach a board, which is one reason a pool of finished careers can make early talent look easier to spot than it was.
