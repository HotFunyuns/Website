A missing number problem hands you an equation with a gap in it — `47 + ? = 112` — and asks what belongs in the gap. One move solves all of them: apply the operation that undoes the one written in the equation. What makes them worth an article is that *which* inverse you need depends on where the gap sits, because subtraction and division do not behave like addition and multiplication. Get that wrong and the method fails quietly, producing a plausible number that is not the answer.

This is not a sequence puzzle. There is no pattern to spot and no list of terms to extend; the rule is already written down in front of you, and the work is entirely in reversing it. If you are here for patterns instead, the taxonomy of sequence types is in our guide to [number sequence puzzles](/blog/number-sequence-puzzles-explained/), and the rest of the [logic and pattern puzzles hub](/blog/topics/logic-and-pattern-puzzles/) covers the other families.

## The equals sign is a statement, not an instruction

Most of the trouble starts before any arithmetic does. Read `47 + ? = 112` as "forty-seven plus something, now give me the answer" and the gap has nowhere to go. Read it as "the left side and the right side name the same number" and the gap becomes a constraint you can work with.

The distinction has a name in mathematics education — an operational versus a relational reading of the equals sign — and it is unusually consequential. A study by Percival Matthews and Lynn Fuchs, published in *Child Development*, tracked children's understanding of the equals sign in second grade against their algebra performance two years later, and reported that equal-sign knowledge was the strongest single predictor of later algebraic competence in their model, accounting for as much unique variance as the other predictors combined. Children holding the operational reading characteristically reject formats like `4 + 1 = 2 + 3` as wrong, because nothing after the equals sign looks like an answer.

That matters for puzzles, because good missing number problems use those layouts deliberately. `8 + 7 = ? + 6` is not a trick. The left side is 15, so the right side must also be 15, so the gap is 9. Check: 9 + 6 = 15.

## Choosing the inverse depends on where the gap is

Addition and multiplication are commutative, so a gap on either side is handled the same way. Subtraction and division are not, and that is where the two common wrong answers come from. The table below gives the move for each of the seven shapes you will actually meet, with `a`, `b` and `c` standing for numbers that are written down.

| Equation shape | The move | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `a + ? = c` | `c - a` | Order does not matter |
| `? + b = c` | `c - b` | Same move as above |
| `? - b = c` | `c + b` | The gap is the starting amount |
| `a - ? = c` | `a - c` | **Not** `a + c` |
| `a * ? = c` | `c / a` | Order does not matter |
| `? / b = c` | `c * b` | The gap is the amount being divided |
| `a / ? = c` | `a / c` | **Not** `a * c` |

The two bolded rows are the whole difficulty. When the gap is the amount being taken away or divided by, it sits in the position the inverse operation also wants, and the operation appears to reverse itself. Worked through, they are unremarkable:

- `91 - ? = 57`. The gap is 91 - 57 = **34**. Check: 91 - 34 = 57.
- `204 / ? = 17`. The gap is 204 / 17 = **12**. Check: 204 / 12 = 17, and 17 x 12 = 204.

Compare those with the friendlier versions, where the gap is the starting amount:

- `? - 38 = 26`. The gap is 26 + 38 = **64**. Check: 64 - 38 = 26.
- `? / 9 = 13`. The gap is 13 x 9 = **117**. Check: 117 / 9 = 13.

A quick sanity test separates the two cases without memorising the table. Ask whether the gap should be bigger or smaller than the number you can see. In `91 - ? = 57` you are removing something from 91 and landing on 57, so the gap has to be smaller than 91 — which rules out `91 + 57` before you calculate anything.

## Chains: undo the last thing first

Once an equation contains two operations, a single inverse is not enough. Peel it from the outside in, undoing whichever operation was applied last.

`6 * ? + 9 = 51`

Nine was added at the end, so remove it first: 51 - 9 = 42. That leaves `6 * ? = 42`, and dividing gives **7**. Substituting back: 6 x 7 = 42, and 42 + 9 = 51.

A longer one, `(? + 5) * 4 - 12 = 40`:

1. Undo the subtraction: 40 + 12 = 52.
2. Undo the multiplication: 52 / 4 = 13.
3. Undo the addition: 13 - 5 = **8**.

Check forwards: 8 + 5 = 13; 13 x 4 = 52; 52 - 12 = 40. That order reversal is the part readers get wrong often enough to deserve its own treatment, which is why [working backwards](/blog/working-backwards-problems/) has a separate article covering longer chains and the problems where the chain is buried in a story.

## Missing operators, and why some have two answers

A related family gives you all the numbers and removes the symbol instead: `12 ? 4 ? 3 = 9`. The method changes, because you are now searching a small space rather than inverting a known step — and the space really is small, since there are only four operators to try in each gap. Reading left to right, 12 - 4 + 3 = 11, 12 / 4 + 3 = 6, and 12 x 4 / 3 = 16. The combination that lands on 9 is 12 / 4 x 3. Check: 12 / 4 = 3, and 3 x 3 = 9.

NRICH, the mathematics education project based in the Faculty of Mathematics at the University of Cambridge, uses this format in its "Which Symbol?" task, and its teachers' notes point out that several of the two-gap versions have two valid solutions rather than one. That is worth knowing before you decide you have the wrong answer: a missing-operator puzzle is only guaranteed unique if the setter checked that it was.

## Practising the move rather than the puzzle

Reading a method and owning it are different things, and the gap between them closes through repetition on items you have not seen before. Mental Math & Memory Games, which is our own app, ships a **Missing Number Puzzle** mode built around this exact task, with Easy, Medium and Hard settings on a 75-second clock, plus an **Equation Builder** mode that works the other way round — you assemble the pieces that make a given equation true. The point of the timer is not speed for its own sake; it is that a time limit discourages solving by trial and error, which is the habit that stops the inverse from becoming automatic.

Two cautions about drilling this. First, accuracy before speed: an inverted sign you never notice is worse than a slow correct answer, and the quickest way to install the error permanently is to practise it at pace. Second, always substitute. Substitution is a complete check for a single unknown — if your number makes the original equation true, it is right, regardless of how you found it.
