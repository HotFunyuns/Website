Lao vowels are easiest to learn as pairs. Wikipedia's Lao language article states that all Lao vowels make a phonemic length distinction, so nearly every vowel has a short and a long partner, and the script writes them with related signs. Add the fact that signs sit before, after, above and below the consonant, and that a few change shape inside a word, and you have most of the system. This guide works through the pairs, the shape changes and the special vowels. Romanisations follow the BGN/PCGN column of Wikipedia's Lao script table; the dotted circle ◌ stands for any consonant.

## Short and long pairs, side by side

| Short | IPA | Long | IPA | BGN/PCGN |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ◌ະ / ◌ັ◌ | a | ◌າ | aː | a |
| ◌ິ | i | ◌ີ | iː | i |
| ◌ຶ | ɯ | ◌ື | ɯː | u |
| ◌ຸ | u | ◌ູ | uː | ou |
| ເ◌ະ / ເ◌ັ◌ | e | ເ◌ | eː | é |
| ແ◌ະ / ແ◌ັ◌ | ɛ | ແ◌ | ɛː | è |
| ໂ◌ະ / ◌ົ◌ | o | ໂ◌ | oː | ô |
| ເ◌າະ / ◌ັອ◌ | ɔ | ◌ໍ / ◌ອ◌ | ɔː | o |
| ເ◌ິ | ɤʔ | ເ◌ີ | ɤː | eu |
| ເ◌ັຍ | iaʔ | ເ◌ຍ / ◌ຽ◌ | ia | ia |
| ເ◌ຶອ | ɯaʔ | ເ◌ືອ | ɯa | ua |
| ◌ົວະ | uaʔ | ◌ົວ / ◌ວ◌ | ua | oua |

Two things make the table easier than it looks. First, many pairs differ by a single stroke: ◌ິ and ◌ີ, ◌ຶ and ◌ື, ◌ຸ and ◌ູ. Second, the short member of a pair often just adds ◌ະ to the long one: ເ◌ is long *é*, ເ◌ະ is short. Wikipedia notes that, as in Thai, ◌ະ represents a glottal stop after a vowel, and the Lao language article explains why that matters: a short vowel must be followed by a consonant, and a final glottal stop can only follow a short vowel.

Note that the romanisation column does not show length. *A* stands for both ◌ະ and ◌າ, which is one reason to learn the script rather than rely on romanised spelling.

## Final and medial forms: why ◌ະ becomes ◌ັ

Wikipedia notes that some vowels change form depending on whether they come at the end of a syllable or in the middle, before a final consonant. The table above shows both where they differ. Short *a* is ◌ະ at the end of a syllable but the small mark ◌ັ, called *mai kan*, when a consonant follows, as in ຫັກ below. Short *o* is ໂ◌ະ at the end but ◌ົ, *mai kong*, before a final consonant. Long *ɔː* is ◌ໍ when final and ◌ອ◌ when medial, and long *ia* is ເ◌ຍ when final and ◌ຽ◌ when medial.

## The silent ອ and the named vowel marks

Lao vowels cannot stand alone or begin a syllable. Wikipedia explains that the silent consonant **ອ**, which can also function as a vowel in its own right, is used as the base when a word begins with a vowel sound. The vowels are named by saying *sala* before the sign, and a few have their own names:

- ໃ◌ *mai muan*, "rolled stem"
- ໄ◌ *mai mai*, "unwound stem"
- ◌ົ *mai kong*, "straight stem"
- ◌ັ *mai kan*, "ear stem"
- ◌ຽ *wilam*, and ◌ໍ *nikkhahit*

Wikipedia adds a historical note: the 1975 spelling reform made the main vowels explicit in writing, where earlier Lao relied more on an inherent vowel built into the consonant.

## Four special vowels: ໄ◌, ໃ◌, ເ◌ົາ and ◌ໍາ

| Sign | IPA | Romanised | Older spelling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ໄ◌ | aj | ai | ◌ັຍ |
| ໃ◌ | aj | ai | ◌ັຍ |
| ເ◌ົາ | aw | ao | ◌ັວ |
| ◌ໍາ | am | am | ◌ັມ |

ໄ◌ and ໃ◌ sound the same in the standard language, which makes them a spelling matter rather than a pronunciation one. Wikipedia notes one exception: in the Northern, Luang Prabang, dialect ໃ◌ is pronounced [aɰ], and in the Northeastern, Houaphanh, dialect it is [ɯ].

## A worked example: four words built from h and k

Wikipedia's Lao language article lists four words with the same consonant sounds, h and k, that differ only in vowel length and in the initial letter used:

1. **ຫັກ** /hák/, "break": medial short ◌ັ, high tone.
2. **ຮັກ** /hàk/, "love": the same short vowel after a different h-letter, low tone.
3. **ຫາກ** /hȁːk/, "if, inevitably": long ◌າ, mid-falling tone.
4. **ຮາກ** /hâːk/, "vomit, root": long ◌າ, falling tone.

Swap ◌ັ for ◌າ and both the vowel length and the tone change. That is why [Lao tone rules](/blog/lao-tone-rules/) treats vowel length as one of the inputs to tone, alongside consonant class, tone marks and syllable type. The same article notes that syllables ending in p, t or k take one of four tones, and those ending in a glottal stop take one of only two.
