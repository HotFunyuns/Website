You can find the Thai for "thank you" written as *khop khun*, *kob khun*, *korb koon* and half a dozen other ways, and every one of them is a defensible attempt at the same word. None of them tells you what tone to say it on.

That single fact is the strongest argument for learning the script, and it is worth understanding before you decide how much effort to put in.

## The romanisation trap

Thailand's official transliteration standard is the Royal Thai General System of Transcription, maintained by the Office of the Royal Society. It is a good system for its actual purpose, which is putting Thai names and places into the Latin alphabet consistently — on road signs, in passports, in library catalogues.

It has one property that matters enormously to a learner: **it does not record tone, and it does not record vowel length.** Neither does the ad-hoc romanisation on a menu or in a phrasebook. Library cataloguing systems such as the ALA-LC romanization tables solve a different problem again and are not built to be pronounced from.

So a romanised Thai word is missing two of the four things you need to say it. You can memorise the missing information word by word, from audio, and plenty of people do. But you are then carrying in your head what the writing system would have carried for you, and you have no way to read a new word correctly on sight. The ceiling arrives sooner than people expect — usually at the point where two words you know look identical romanised and are obviously different to a Thai speaker.

Learning the script removes the problem at the root. It is not a prerequisite for saying "hello" on a beach holiday. It is a prerequisite for getting better after month two.

## What kind of writing system Thai is

Thai is not an alphabet in the European sense. It is an **abugida**: consonant letters carry a built-in vowel, and other vowels are written as signs attached to the consonant.

That distinction sounds academic until you see where the vowel signs go.

### Vowels do not sit in a line

In Latin script, letters run left to right and you read them in the order they appear. Thai vowel signs are placed **before, after, above or below** the consonant, and some are made of pieces that sit on more than one side of it at once.

The consequence is that **written order is not always spoken order**. A vowel sign written to the left of a consonant is pronounced after it. A beginner who reads Thai strictly left to right will produce syllables in the wrong order and conclude that the script is chaotic. It is not — it is consistent — but it is consistent according to a rule you have to be taught rather than one you can infer.

This is the single most common early stumble, and it is why alphabet practice that separates consonants, vowels, tone marks and syllables into stages works better than presenting a full chart on day one. It is how the alphabet section of [Regal - Learn Thai Language](/apps/learn-thai/) is organised: consonants, vowels, tone marks and syllables in sequence, with reading and writing basics, before sentences get long.

## Why 44 consonants for far fewer sounds

Here is the fact that makes Thai look harder than it is, and then makes it make sense.

The consonant inventory is usually counted at **44 letters**. Two of them, ฃ and ฅ, have dropped out of ordinary modern use. Those letters do not map to 44 different sounds — several letters are pronounced identically. Learners reasonably ask why the extra letters exist.

The answer is that Thai consonant letters do two jobs at once. They tell you the sound, **and** they tell you which of three **tone classes** the letter belongs to: high, mid or low. Two letters that sound the same can belong to different classes, and that difference changes the tone of the whole syllable.

In other words, the redundancy is not redundancy. The tone information has to live somewhere, and Thai puts a large part of it in the choice of consonant letter. Once you see that, memorising the class alongside the sound stops feeling like arbitrary extra work and starts feeling like the point.

## Five tones, spelled rather than marked

Standard Thai distinguishes **five tones**: mid, low, falling, high and rising. There are **four tone marks**.

Four marks cannot label five tones, and that mismatch is the key to the whole system. The tone of a syllable is worked out from four things together:

1. The **class of the initial consonant** — high, mid or low.
2. The **length of the vowel** — long or short.
3. The **ending of the syllable** — whether it stops on a final consonant sound like -k, -t or -p, or ends openly.
4. The **tone mark**, if there is one.

The tone mark is one input among four, not the answer. This is why "just learn the four tone marks" is bad advice, and why learners who try to shortcut the consonant classes hit a wall a few weeks in.

The upside is real, though, and it is worth stating plainly: **the system is regular.** Once you know the rules, you can look at a Thai word you have never seen and derive its tone. That is something a romanised word can never give you, and it is the moment the script starts paying you back.

Recognising a tone and producing one are separate skills, and both are harder in conversation than in a quiet room. An app can give you controlled listening practice and prompt you to speak; it cannot hear you the way a fluent speaker can. Treat pronunciation work here as a foundation to bring to a real conversation, not a replacement for one.

## Three things nobody warns you about

**There are no spaces between words.** Thai runs continuously, and spaces mark the ends of clauses and sentences instead. Where a word ends is something you infer from context and from Thai syllable structure. Letter recognition comes fast; reading speed lags behind it for months, and this is why.

**There are no capital letters and no full stops.** Nothing on the page marks a proper noun or the start of a sentence. Punctuation exists but does less work than in English.

**Thai has its own digits.** ๐ ๑ ๒ ๓ ๔ ๕ ๖ ๗ ๘ ๙ run from zero to nine alongside the Arabic numerals you already read. You will meet them on official documents, temple signage and occasionally on price lists, and they take about an afternoon to learn — one of the highest returns on effort in the whole script.

## The handful of phrases worth getting exactly right

Kept deliberately short, because a phrase learned with the wrong tone is a phrase you will have to unlearn.

**สวัสดี** (*sawatdi* in RTGS, often written *sawasdee*) is the general greeting, usable at any time of day.

**ครับ** (*khrap*) and **ค่ะ** (*kha*) are politeness particles added to the end of a sentence, and which one you use depends on the speaker's gender rather than the listener's: **ครับ** for a male speaker, **ค่ะ** for a female speaker. They are not optional decoration. Thai marks politeness structurally, and leaving the particle off makes an otherwise correct sentence sound abrupt. The female form also shifts depending on whether you are making a statement or asking a question, which is a detail worth learning from a course rather than from a phrasebook.

**ขอบคุณ** (*khopkhun*) is "thank you", and it takes the same politeness particle on the end.

A note on which Thai you are learning: courses, including ours, teach **Standard Thai**, which is based on the Central Thai variety and is the language of education, broadcasting and government. Northern, Northeastern and Southern regions have their own varieties with real differences in vocabulary and pronunciation, and Northeastern Thai in particular is closely related to Lao. Standard Thai is understood everywhere; it is not the only Thai you will hear.

## Where Thai sits on the difficulty scale

The clearest public benchmark is the US Foreign Service Institute's training categories, which group languages by how long FSI students typically need to reach an integrated score of 3 on the Interagency Language Roundtable scale. Thai is in **Category III**, at approximately **44 weeks of full-time instruction (1,012 class hours)**, alongside Russian, Khmer, Polish and Vietnamese.

That figure describes intensive full-time classroom training for diplomats — FSI describes a typical week as around 23 hours of class plus 17 hours of self-study — so it is not a prediction about app study. Use it as a relative scale instead: Thai is demanding, but a full category easier than Cantonese, Mandarin, Japanese, Korean or Arabic. The script is front-loaded difficulty; the grammar behind it is considerably kinder than the writing system suggests.

## A four-week plan for the script

**Week 1 — mid-class consonants and short vowels.** A small set of letters and a small set of vowels, learned as syllables you can actually say. Resist the urge to learn all 44 consonants first; a chart you cannot pronounce is not knowledge.

**Week 2 — high and low classes.** Learn each new consonant with its class from the first exposure, the same way you would learn a German noun with its gender. Adding the class later is much harder than learning it at the same time.

**Week 3 — tone rules on real syllables.** Take the four inputs and start deriving tones for words you already know by ear. The goal is to make the rules produce something you can verify against audio, not to recite a table.

**Week 4 — read continuous text badly.** Slowly, out loud, getting word boundaries wrong. This is the stage most people skip and it is the one that converts letter recognition into reading. Dictation is unusually good practice here because it forces you to commit to a spelling, which forces you to commit to a tone.

## What is genuinely hard, and what is not

**Genuinely hard:** the tone rules, because they require holding four things in mind at once until they become automatic. Word segmentation in unspaced text. Producing tone contrasts reliably at conversational speed.

**Not as hard as advertised:** Thai grammar. No verb conjugation for tense or person, no grammatical gender, no articles, no plural inflection. Once the script is behind you, the sentence structures come faster than they do in most European languages.

That is the shape of the language, and it is why the script is worth the front-loaded effort.
