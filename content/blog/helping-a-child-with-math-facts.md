**Short, frequent sessions on a small set of facts, with the timer introduced last and your own commentary kept neutral.** That is the whole of the advice, and almost everything that goes wrong with home maths practice is a departure from one of those four points.

This is written for the person sitting next to the child rather than for the child. The difference matters, because most of what determines whether a home session helps is decided by the adult before a single question is asked.

## What "maths facts" means, and why recall is the goal

Maths facts are the small results that should arrive without being worked out: number bonds to ten, doubles, and the multiplication tables. The distinction that matters is between *recalling* 7 × 8 and *reconstructing* it from 7 × 4 doubled. Both produce 56. Only one of them is free.

The reason it is worth caring about is capacity. Research on arithmetic consistently finds working memory involved in solving problems — Cragg and colleagues, in [When is working memory important for arithmetic?](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5724815/), found that a working-memory load slowed and degraded performance across retrieval, decomposition and counting alike, rather than sparing any one strategy. A fact that has to be rebuilt mid-problem is competing with the steps around it. Fluent recall is not about impressing anyone; it clears space for the part of the problem that actually needs thought.

That also sets a limit on ambition. The claim here is about arithmetic specifically. Nothing about drilling times tables supports a broader claim about a child's intelligence or future, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something.

## A ten-minute session that works

Keep the structure the same every time, so no negotiation is required about what is happening.

**Pick a small set.** Six to eight facts, not a whole table and certainly not all of them. A set small enough to finish is a set a child will start.

**Do the easy ones first.** Beginning with facts already known establishes that the session is survivable. This feels like wasted time and is not.

**Say the answer, do not derive it, when recall is the target.** If a fact does not arrive within a couple of seconds, supply it, move on, and return to it two or three questions later. Silent struggling at a card is not practice; it is just a longer pause.

**Mix in the facts already secure.** A set of only hard facts produces a session of only failure.

**Stop before it stops working.** Five to ten minutes is enough. Ending while it is still fine is what makes tomorrow's session possible, and the general case for spreading practice thinly across days is made in [building a daily practice habit](/blog/building-a-daily-practice-habit/).

## The timer question

Timing is genuinely useful, and its placement is what people get wrong.

The What Works Clearinghouse [practice guide on mathematics intervention in the elementary grades](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/26) lists six recommendations, all rated as supported by strong evidence, and one of them is to regularly include timed activities as a way to build fluency. So the case for timing is real.

But fluency means fast *and* correct, and the order is not interchangeable. A timer over a method a child is still assembling produces errors more quickly and adds pressure to a task that may already feel risky. There is also an interaction worth knowing about: Finell and colleagues' [meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8811497/) reported a significant negative indirect effect of math anxiety on performance operating through working memory, meaning that pressure takes capacity the calculation needs. That is reason enough to establish accuracy first and add the clock afterwards.

A reasonable rule: no timer until the child is getting nearly all of a small set right without hesitation, and then use it as a game rather than a measurement.

## Your own attitude is part of the method

National Numeracy's guidance for families on [supporting children with maths](https://www.nationalnumeracy.org.uk/helping-children-maths) is short and specific. Be positive about maths, and avoid saying things like "I can't do maths" or "I hated maths at school". Point out the maths in everyday life — shopping, cooking, travelling. Praise effort rather than calling a child clever, so that improvement is framed as something produced by work.

The first of those is the one adults break without noticing. A remark meant as solidarity — *I was never any good at this either* — hands a child a ready-made explanation for stopping. Neutral is better than enthusiastic and far better than commiserating.

Two additions in the same spirit. Do not re-teach a method different from the one the child is being taught, because two competing procedures is worse than one imperfect one; ask which way they have been shown, and use that. And keep homework help separate from fact practice, because arguing over a worksheet is not a good moment to start drilling.

## Where an app fits, and where it does not

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and it is rated Everyone on Google Play in the educational game category. Used sensibly it supplies the repetition and the immediate feedback that a parent doing this by hand has to generate manually.

The parts that suit a child are specific. Accuracy Trainer runs ten questions with no timer, gives feedback and an explanation, and offers a hint — the right default. Flash Cards include a times tables deck covering the products from 2 × 2 to 12 × 12, twelve cards a session, self-graded as missed or got it. The lowest difficulty settings on each operation are genuinely low: single-digit addition, and multiplication restricted to the 1 to 9 tables. Sessions are short by design, and play works offline, so a device in aeroplane mode is a device with fewer distractions.

What it is not: it is a game, not a curriculum and not a tutor. It does not decide what a child should learn next, it does not track a syllabus, and it makes no assessment of any kind — the numbers it keeps are score, accuracy, streak, response time and personal bests on its own drills. The choice of what to practise stays with the adult and the school.
