An IQ test measures how well one person performed on a specific, fixed set of short cognitive tasks, under standardised conditions, compared with other people of the same age who took the same tasks. That is the whole claim. It is not a reading of a quantity inside anyone's head, and the number it produces is a rank, not an amount.

Almost every argument about IQ becomes clearer once that sentence is taken literally. A rank depends entirely on who is in the comparison group and which tasks were chosen. Change either and the number changes, without anything about the person changing at all.

## What is actually inside a test battery

A modern individually administered battery is not one test. It is a collection of short, separately timed subtests, each sampling a narrow skill, whose results are pooled into domain scores and then into a single composite.

Pearson's official description of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, Fifth Edition — an instrument for ages 16:0 to 90:11 — sets out the structure plainly. Its subtests appraise five cognitive domains: verbal comprehension, visual spatial ability, fluid reasoning, working memory and processing speed. Seven core subtests feed the Full Scale IQ, with further secondary and ancillary subtests available for more specialised questions. Purchasing and administering it requires Qualification Level C, meaning a credentialled professional rather than anyone who can open a web page.

That last detail is not bureaucratic trivia. The qualification requirement exists because the score is only meaningful if the administration conditions were the ones the norms were collected under. A battery administered casually is not a cheaper version of the same measurement; it is a different thing that happens to use similar questions.

## The g factor, and what the evidence for it is

If you give a large group of people a dozen unrelated cognitive tasks, the scores tend to correlate positively: people who do well on one tend to do somewhat better than average on the others. This pattern is robust and it is the empirical starting point of the whole field.

What the pattern *means* is a separate question. The Cattell–Horn–Carroll framework, the model most contemporary batteries are built around, arranges abilities in three strata: individual tests at the bottom, a set of broad abilities above them — fluid reasoning, comprehension-knowledge, visual processing, auditory processing, working memory, retrieval fluency and processing speed — and a general factor at the apex.

A 2023 network analysis of CHC measures by McGrew, Schneider, Decker and Bulut is worth reading carefully on this point. Using network methods rather than conventional factor analysis, the authors recovered seven broad CHC abilities without requiring a dominant general factor to account for the correlations between tests. Their argument is that the positive correlation among tests is the primary fact, and that general intelligence is one interpretation of that fact rather than its demonstrated cause. Comprehension-knowledge, in their model, turned out to be unusually central to the network as a whole.

So "g" is best described as a well-replicated statistical regularity with a contested interpretation — not a substance, and not a thing anyone has located.

## The abilities the instrument never touches

A test can only report on what it sampled. That is an obvious point which marketing routinely obscures, so it is worth stating in full.

Not sampled by a standard cognitive battery: motivation, persistence, conscientiousness, emotional regulation, social judgement, practical problem-solving in an unstructured setting, creativity, manual and athletic skill, and any specific expertise that happens not to appear in the subtests. Also not sampled: what a person can do with unlimited time, with tools, with colleagues, or when the task matters to them.

This is why the phrase "measures intelligence" does more work than it should. A battery measures performance on its own contents. Whether those contents add up to the everyday word "intelligence" is a judgement, not a finding.

## What the score is useful for, and where that stops

Cognitive measures do relate to real outcomes. A 2026 framework paper in the *Journal of Intelligence* summarises the position for education: measures of general cognitive ability show moderate-to-strong associations with grades, standardised achievement scores and educational attainment, and adding a cognitive measure to an existing file can produce a statistically meaningful predictive gain, particularly at transition points.

The same paper is the better guide to the limits. Incremental validity is context-dependent, shifting with range restriction, grading differences and unequal opportunity. More importantly, predictive validity is not the same as **decision utility** — whether a score actually improves a decision someone has to make. Where achievement records already carry most of the signal, adding a test may not change the decision at all; where a target outcome is rare, false positives multiply; and a measure can improve average prediction while worsening outcomes for a subgroup. The authors' conclusion is that testing should be conditional and sequential, used only where it can plausibly change what happens next.

Then there is measurement error. As ETS puts it in its plain-language guide to reliability, test scores are reliable to the extent that they are consistent across occasions, across editions of a test, and across raters. No score is exact, which is why a responsible report gives a range rather than a point.

## How to read a score someone hands you

Three questions do most of the work. Who was the comparison group, and when were they tested? What tasks were sampled, and under what conditions? And what is the margin of error on the reported number?

If the answer to any of those is unavailable, the score is not interpretable — which is the usual situation outside supervised assessment.
