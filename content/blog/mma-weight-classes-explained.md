Weight classes exist for one reason, and it is not fairness in the sporting sense. It is that mass is an advantage no amount of skill reliably cancels, and a sport that lets any two people fight regardless of size is a sport that produces avoidable injuries.

Everything else about the system — the odd gaps between divisions, catch weight, the rules about missing weight — follows from that starting point.

## Where the list actually comes from

There is a published table. The **Unified Rules of Mixed Martial Arts**, maintained by the Association of Boxing Commissions and Combative Sports, set out the divisions and their limits, and state regulators adopt them by reference — Nevada's administrative code for unarmed combat, for instance, points at the unified rules rather than restating them.

That is worth knowing because it means the weight classes are a matter of public record you can check yourself rather than something the sport agrees on informally. It also means promotions do not each invent their own; they select from the published set.

## The full table

| Weight class | Limit |
| --- | --- |
| Atomweight | up to and including 105 lbs |
| Straw Weight | over 105 to 115 lbs |
| Flyweight | over 115 to 125 lbs |
| Bantamweight | over 125 to 135 lbs |
| Featherweight | over 135 to 145 lbs |
| Lightweight | over 145 to 155 lbs |
| Super Lightweight | over 155 to 165 lbs |
| Welterweight | over 165 to 170 lbs |
| Super Welterweight | over 170 to 175 lbs |
| Middleweight | over 175 to 185 lbs |
| Super Middleweight | over 185 to 195 lbs |
| Light Heavyweight | over 195 to 205 lbs |
| Cruiserweight | over 205 to 225 lbs |
| Heavyweight | over 225 to 265 lbs |
| Super Heavyweight | over 265 lbs |

Fifteen divisions. Not every promotion or commission runs all of them — the table is the menu of sanctioned divisions, not a claim about what is contested everywhere.

### The bands are not the same size, and that is deliberate

Run the subtraction down the table and a pattern appears:

- Most of the lower and middle divisions step in **10 lb** increments: 115 to 125, 125 to 135, 135 to 145, and so on.
- **Welterweight** (165 to 170) and **Super Welterweight** (170 to 175) are only **5 lbs** wide each.
- **Cruiserweight** spans **20 lbs** (205 to 225).
- **Heavyweight** spans **40 lbs** (225 to 265).
- **Super Heavyweight** has no upper bound at all.

Two forces produce that shape. The first is population: the middle of the table is where most competitors are, so finer divisions are possible and useful there. The second is proportion — five pounds is a meaningful fraction of a 135 lb fighter and a much smaller fraction of a 250 lb one, so equal-sized bands at the top would be splitting hairs while leaving divisions empty.

The practical consequence is that **"same division" means very different things at different ends of the table.** Two heavyweights can legally be forty pounds apart. Two welterweights cannot be more than five.

## Allowances, catch weight and missing weight

Three provisions govern what happens when the neat table meets reality.

**Within a division.** If both fighters weigh in inside the same contracted division, the rules impose no allowance restrictions. They have both made the limit, and that is the end of the matter.

**When somebody misses.** If a fighter misses the contracted weight and the two competitors end up in different weight classes, the rules cap the difference: the heavier opponent shall not exceed five pounds over the lower-weighing fighter. That is the safety backstop, and it is why a badly missed weight can end a bout before it is made rather than merely costing a percentage of a purse.

**Contracted catch weight.** A catch weight bout is one agreed at a figure that is not a standard divisional limit. The rules state there is no weight spread allowance between contracted catch weight fighters so long as both are below the contracted weight, and they explicitly permit commissions to deny a catch weight bout where the differential looks like a safety problem.

Catch weight is often described as a compromise, and structurally that is exactly what it is: a negotiated number, with no division, no ranking and no title attached to it.

## Why the number on the scale is a proxy, not a verdict

Weight is measured once, hours or a day before the fight, and what it is standing in for is frame — height, reach, limb length, muscle mass, the amount of force a body can generate and absorb.

Those things do not track weight perfectly. Two fighters who make the same limit can be built very differently, and the differences that matter in a fight are often the ones the scale does not see:

- **Reach.** Where punches can land from, and whether one fighter can work outside the other's range.
- **Frame and leverage.** Which matters enormously in the grappling exchanges where position is decided by leverage rather than by speed.
- **How the weight was made.** A fighter who cut hard to make a limit and one who walks around near it arrive in different condition regardless of what the scale said.

This is also why a weight class does not settle anything. It equalises one variable so that the others — technique, style, conditioning, decision-making — are what decide the result. That is the whole point of having the table.

## Quick reference

- Divisions are defined by an **upper limit**; the floor is simply the class below.
- Band widths: mostly **10 lbs**, **5 lbs** at welterweight and super welterweight, **20 lbs** at cruiserweight, **40 lbs** at heavyweight, open above 265.
- **No allowance restrictions** when both fighters make the same contracted division.
- **Five pounds maximum** between fighters who end up in different classes after a missed weight.
- **No spread allowance** for contracted catch weight fighters, and commissions may refuse the bout.
- A professional round is **five minutes** with a **one-minute** rest; bouts run one to five rounds and cannot exceed five rounds or twenty-five minutes.
