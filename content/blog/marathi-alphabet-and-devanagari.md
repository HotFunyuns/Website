Look at ळ. It is not in the Hindi alphabet chart you may have seen, and it is not decorative. It writes a retroflex lateral — the tongue curled back — and it contrasts with ordinary ल. Get the two confused and you are not producing an accent, you are producing a different word.

That single letter is a good way into what the Marathi alphabet actually is: Devanagari, yes, but Devanagari tuned for Marathi rather than borrowed wholesale from Hindi.

## Devanagari is an abugida, and that changes the learning order

Devanagari is a left-to-right abugida derived from Brahmi, in regular use by the 8th century and in its modern form by about 1000 CE. It is now used for more than 120 languages.

The word *abugida* is doing real work. In an alphabet, vowels and consonants are peers: you write them in sequence. In an abugida, every consonant letter arrives with a vowel already attached — in Devanagari, a short *a*. क on its own is not *k*, it is *ka*. To get *ki* you add a mark. To get *k* with no vowel at all you have to actively cancel the inherited one.

This has a practical consequence that most beginners get backwards. **You cannot read anything until you know the vowel system, and you can read a surprising amount once you do.** Consonants are a long list you chip away at. Vowels are a short list that unlocks structure.

## The two lives of every vowel

Each vowel in Devanagari has two forms, and you should learn them as a pair rather than as two separate lessons:

- The **independent letter**, used when the vowel begins a syllable with no consonant in front of it: अ, आ, इ, ई, उ, ऊ, ए, ऐ, ओ, औ.
- The **matra**, the dependent mark that attaches to a consonant and replaces its inherent *a*.

The matras are the part that looks alarming and is not. They attach above, below, before or after the consonant, and the one that trips people up most is the *i* matra, which is written to the *left* of the consonant it follows in speech. You read the consonant first and the mark second even though the mark sits first on the page.

The remaining pieces are the anusvara (the dot, ं, marking nasalisation) and the visarga (ः). In Marathi the anusvara does an extra job worth knowing early: it is sometimes used specifically to block schwa deletion, so that खरं is pronounced *khara* rather than losing its final vowel.

## Consonants come in rows, not in a list

The consonant chart is arranged by where in the mouth the sound is made and whether it is aspirated or voiced. Learn it in rows and the pattern does most of the memorising for you: क ख ग घ ङ, then च छ ज झ ञ, and so on through the retroflex, dental and labial series.

Two contrasts matter more in Marathi than a Hindi-oriented chart will tell you:

- **ल against ळ.** Alveolar against retroflex lateral. Marathi keeps this distinction and uses it lexically.
- **The affricates.** Marathi contrasts an apico-alveolar series with an alveopalatal one, so च and ज each have two pronunciations depending on the word — closer to *ts/dz* in some items and *ch/j* in others. Nothing in the spelling tells you which. This is one of the few places where Marathi orthography genuinely under-specifies the pronunciation, and the only fix is hearing the words.

## The letters Marathi adds, and why we teach them separately

In our Marathi course the alphabet unit is split. The letters shared with the standard Hindi Devanagari set are taught first, as one chart. **The letters Marathi adds get their own short lesson afterwards, rather than being folded into the main chart.**

That was a deliberate decision and it came from a specific failure. When the additional letters sat inside the main grid, they were learned as more chart — indistinguishable in status from क or म, and therefore not flagged as anything to watch. Learners with Hindi behind them skimmed the chart, recognised most of it, and missed exactly the characters they did not already know. Splitting them out makes the difference the point of the lesson: here is what your existing Devanagari does not cover.

The cost of the split is that a learner who starts completely fresh meets the alphabet in two passes instead of one, which feels slower on day two. We took that trade because the population coming to Marathi with some Hindi is large, and the specific error that population makes is expensive. [Marathi and Hindi compared](/blog/marathi-vs-hindi-differences/) goes through the rest of what transfers and what misleads.

## Conjuncts: the part that actually takes time

When two consonants meet with no vowel between them, Devanagari usually fuses them into a conjunct rather than writing a visible halant. Some conjuncts are transparent — you can see both parents. Others are not.

There is no shortcut here, and anyone selling you one is selling you a chart you will not retain. What works is reading volume: meeting the same conjuncts repeatedly in real words until the shape maps to a sound without a decoding step. Our alphabet lessons use tracing and recognition drills rather than a single memorisation table for exactly this reason, and [Learn Marathi Language Fast](/apps/learn-marathi/) keeps romanised text beside the script so a conjunct you cannot yet read does not stop the sentence.

## A workable order

1. All ten common vowels, independent form and matra together. One session.
2. The first three consonant rows with the *a* matra only, so you are reading two-letter words almost immediately.
3. Remaining consonant rows.
4. The Marathi-specific letters as their own pass.
5. Anusvara, visarga and halant.
6. Conjuncts, indefinitely, through reading rather than through drilling.
7. Numbers, which use their own digit forms (० १ २ ३) and are worth a separate session — [Marathi numbers 1 to 100](/blog/marathi-numbers-and-counting/) explains why the spoken forms are the harder half.
