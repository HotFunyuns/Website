**The five main types of hockey shot are the wrist shot, the snap shot, the slap shot, the backhand and the one-timer; each trades release time, power and accuracy against the others.** Redirections — tip-ins and deflections — and the wrap-around complete the usual list.

The short version of the trade-off: the more time and space a shooter has, the more release time he can afford to spend on power. The less he has, the quicker the shot has to be.

## The shots at a glance

| Shot | How it is taken | What it trades | When it suits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wrist shot | The puck is swept from the blade towards the target | Accuracy and disguise for some power | Most situations, especially in traffic |
| Snap shot | A short wind-up, then the blade snaps into the puck | A little control for more speed off the stick | Catching a goalie before he is set |
| Slap shot | A full backswing into the ice just behind the puck | Release time for maximum power | When there is time and space |
| Backhand | Shot off the back of the blade | Power and lift for an unexpected angle | Close to the net, cutting across |
| One-timer | A pass shot directly, without stopping it | Control for timing | Beating a goalie moving across |
| Tip-in or deflection | Redirecting a teammate's shot | Everything, for a change of direction | In front of the net |
| Wrap-around | Carrying the puck from behind the net around the post | Time for a surprise angle | When the goalie is slow across |

## Reading shot-type statistics

Shot type is part of the play-by-play data analysts work from: MoneyPuck's expected-goals model, for example, lists "Shot Type (Slap, Wrist, Backhand, etc)" among its fifteen inputs. That makes it tempting to rank the shots by how often each one goes in. Two cautions come first.

**Type and location are tangled together.** A shooting percentage by type mixes the shot with where it was taken from. Expected-goals models treat those separately — MoneyPuck's model lists shot type and distance from the net as different inputs, and Evolving-Hockey found distance to be the most important variable in its model. So if slap shots go in less often than wrist shots, that does not prove the slap shot is worse from the same spot; slap shots may simply come from farther out.

**The labels are judgement calls.** Official statistics are entered by people: the rulebook assigns real-time scorers to every game to record them, so the line between, say, a wrist shot and a snap shot is a scorer's call rather than a measurement.

## The shots in more detail

### Wrist shot

USA Hockey's practice guide for its youngest players describes the basic technique: stand sideways to the target so weight can transfer, start with the puck behind the back foot, sweep it towards the target, keep the head up and follow through. Because the puck stays on the blade until release, the shooter can aim it and disguise when it is coming. That is why it is taught first, alongside the backhand, and why it is the default when time is short.

### Snap shot

The snap shot shortens the wrist shot's sweep into a quick wind-up and a snap of the blade into the puck. It gives up a little of the wrist shot's control in exchange for speed off the stick, which makes it a shooter's tool for beating a goaltender before he is set.

### Slap shot

The slap shot is built for power: a full backswing, then the stick strikes the ice just behind the puck. The cost is time. A long wind-up gives defenders a chance to block and the goaltender a chance to set, which makes the slap shot a weapon for players with space, such as a defenceman at the point. The rulebook makes one allowance for its arc: a player is not called for high-sticking for accidental contact during the normal wind-up or follow-through of a shooting motion, although a wild swing at a bouncing puck gets no such protection.

### Backhand

The backhand comes off the back of the blade. It is harder to put power and lift on, but it can be released from angles a forehand shot cannot, especially while cutting across the front of the net. USA Hockey teaches it alongside the wrist shot from the youngest age group.

### One-timer

A one-timer is a pass shot directly, without being stopped. The value is timing: a goaltender has to move across with the pass, and the shot arrives before he is set. It is also one of the later skills to be taught — USA Hockey's skill progressions do not list it until the 14-and-under level.

### Redirections and wrap-arounds

Tip-ins and deflections change the puck's direction after a shot, which gives a goaltender almost no time to react. MoneyPuck notes that the highest-probability shots in its model are quick rebounds close to the net after a large change in angle — the same principle. Wrap-arounds, carried from behind the net around the post, try to beat a goalie to the far side before he can push across.

## The rules that shape shooting

The NHL's 2026-27 rulebook limits shooting in several places:

- **The blade.** The curve of a stick blade may not exceed three-quarters of an inch, measured from a straight line along the blade.
- **What counts as a goal.** A puck deflected in off an attacking player's skate or body counts, but one kicked in with a distinct kicking motion does not, and neither does one deflected in off a stick above the crossbar.
- **Penalty shots and shootouts.** The lacrosse-style move of lifting the puck on the blade is allowed only if the puck stays below shoulder height and is not carried higher than the crossbar when released; the spin-o-rama is banned outright.

## The order players learn them

USA Hockey's *Skill Progressions for Youth Hockey* lays out which shooting skills are taught at each age, and the order says something about difficulty:

| Age group | Shooting skills on the list |
| --- | --- |
| 8 and under | Wrist shot, backhand |
| 10 and under | Adds flip shots, screen shots, deflections and shooting off rebounds |
| 12 and under | Adds the snap shot, slap shot and fake shots |
| 14 and under | Adds one-timers, stick position in scoring areas and shots in close |
| 16/18 and under | Adds adjusting the angle of the shot just before shooting |

Control comes before power, and power before timing. A player who cannot place a wrist shot gains little from a harder slap shot.

## Choosing a shot: four questions

1. **How much time do I have?** Little time means the quickest release — a wrist or snap shot.
2. **How far out am I?** From distance, power matters more, so a slap shot can be worth its wind-up if nobody is closing.
3. **Is there traffic in front?** A redirection changes the puck's direction close to the net and leaves the goaltender almost no time to react, so a low, accurate shot a teammate can tip may be worth more than a hard one the goalie sees all the way.
4. **Where is the goalie moving?** If he is moving across, a one-timer or a quick shot to the side he is leaving takes advantage.
