A professional boxing match is a fixed number of three-minute rounds, up to twelve, with a one-minute rest between them. A referee is the only person in the ring who can stop it, and three judges score every round. When a boxer is knocked down, the opponent is sent to a neutral corner and the referee counts; a boxer who gets up still receives a mandatory count of eight, and one who is down at ten has been knocked out.

Under the Association of Boxing Commissions' unified rules, the model rules that many state commissions adopt, three more things are true that surprise newcomers: there is no standing eight count, there is no three-knockdown rule, and a boxer who is down cannot be saved by the bell in any round. What follows is drawn from the ABC's Unified Rules of Boxing (last amended 3 August 2016), its referee manual (revision 08/2025), Nevada's unarmed-combat regulations, and the WBC's and WBA's own rulebooks, as published on 2 October 2026.

## Folklore, checked against the rulebook

| What people say | What the rules say | Where it is written |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Three knockdowns in a round and it's over." | Not under the unified rules, which have no three-knockdown rule. The WBA's contest rules do treat three knockdowns in one round as an automatic knockout. | ABC rule 8; WBA rule E.12 |
| "He was saved by the bell." | A boxer who has been knocked down cannot be saved by the bell in any round. | ABC rule 10; Nevada NAC 467.740 |
| "The referee gave him a standing eight." | There is no standing eight count. | ABC rule 7; WBC championship rule 13 |
| "He was up at two, so they carried on." | No knocked-down boxer may continue until the referee has counted to eight. | ABC rule 5; Nevada NAC 467.740 |
| "His corner threw in the towel." | In US professional boxing a towel does not stop the bout; the chief second tells the commission inspector, who tells the referee. | ABC referee manual |

The second column is the one that matters on fight night, and the third is there so you can check it yourself.

## Who actually sets the rules

Two kinds of organisation are involved, and they do different jobs.

**A commission regulates the event.** It licenses the boxers and officials, appoints the referee and judges, and enforces its rules on the night. The Association of Boxing Commissions writes model rules that commissions adopt. Nevada, for example, requires championship boxing contests to follow the ABC's championship rules, which its code adopts by reference, and states that where a sanctioning organisation's rules conflict with the state's own chapter, the state's rule controls.

**A sanctioning body awards the title.** The WBA, WBC, IBF and WBO publish their own rules for the bouts they sanction. They expect those rules to apply, but they also defer to the regulator: the WBC's championship rules say that where a local commission's rule is inconsistent with the WBC's, the commission enforces its own.

That is why "the rules of boxing" is slightly the wrong question. The useful question is which rulebook governed this particular fight. How the sanctioning bodies' rankings are themselves regulated is covered in [how a fight stable is managed](/blog/managing-a-fight-stable/).

## Rounds, rest and how long a fight lasts

Every round is three minutes, with a one-minute rest in between, under both the unified rules and Nevada's code. The number of rounds is set in advance. Twelve is the professional ceiling. Fights early in a career, and most non-title fights, are booked for somewhere between four and ten, and two-minute rounds turn up in some jurisdictions, most often for women's bouts.

Title fights sit at the top of that range. The WBC's rules set men's world title bouts at twelve three-minute rounds and forbid its champions and challengers from boxing more than twelve. The WBA schedules its sanctioned bouts at ten or twelve.

A twelve-round fight that goes the distance therefore contains 36 minutes of boxing and 11 minutes of rest: 47 minutes in all.

## What counts as a knockdown

The ABC's referee manual gives the definition. As a result of a legal blow, a boxer is down if they touch the canvas with any part of the body other than the soles of their feet, if they are hanging defencelessly on, through or over the ropes, or if only the ropes are stopping them from falling. A boxer who goes down voluntarily, by taking a knee, is also down.

A slip is not a knockdown. When a boxer goes to the floor without a legal punch causing it, the referee waves both hands low across the body and calls "slip" or "no knockdown".

The distinction matters well beyond the count, because a knockdown normally turns a round into a 10-8 on the judges' cards. Our guide to [how boxing scoring works](/blog/boxing-scoring-explained/) explains why that is a starting point rather than an automatic score.

## The count, step by step

1. **The standing boxer goes to the farthest neutral corner.** If they leave it, the referee stops counting until they return, then resumes from where the count was interrupted.
2. **The referee takes the count from the timekeeper**, calling each second aloud and showing it with the fingers so the fallen boxer can see and hear it.
3. **Nobody restarts before eight.** A boxer who rises at two still waits while the referee counts to eight. That is the mandatory eight count.
4. **The referee assesses.** The manual suggests a few questions and a "gait test" — walk a few steps to one side, turn, walk back — for a boxer who looks unsteady. Its general rule is blunt: if the referee has any doubt, stop the bout.
5. **Ten is a knockout.** A boxer still down at ten has been counted out. The referee may also stop the count early and end the fight if the boxer cannot defend themselves.

A few edge cases are written down too. If a boxer stands and then falls again without being hit, the count resumes where it left off rather than starting over. If both boxers go down together and neither beats the count, the result is a technical draw. And a boxer knocked out of the ring has twenty seconds to get back in, unassisted, under the unified rules. Nevada splits that case: twenty seconds for a boxer who has gone off the ring platform, ten for one who has landed on the apron outside the ropes.

### Why the bell cannot save anyone

Nevada's regulation spells out the mechanism. If the referee is counting when the round's time runs out, the bell is not sounded until the downed boxer is back on their feet. A boxer who fails to rise before ten loses by knockout in the round that has just ended, and a legal punch in the final seconds that drops a boxer after the bell counts as a knockdown in that round.

**Worked example.** Assume a fight run under the ABC's referee manual. A punch already in motion lands just as the round's time expires and drops the boxer. The manual treats any punch thrown before and as the bell sounds as legal, so the referee starts counting and the bell does not ring. The boxer rises at six; the referee still completes the mandatory eight and checks them. Only when the referee lets the fight continue does the bell sound, ending the round and starting the minute's rest. Had the boxer stayed down past ten, the result would have been a knockout in that round — not a reprieve.

## Fouls, warnings and lost points

Nevada's code lists more than thirty acts that count as fouls in boxing. They fall into four rough groups:

- **Where you hit:** too low, behind the ear, the back of the head or neck (the "rabbit punch"), and deliberately over the kidneys.
- **What you hit with:** the head, shoulder, knee or elbow; the open glove, the inside or back of the hand, or the wrist; and a thumb to the eye.
- **When you hit:** after the bell, on the break, while the opponent is down or getting up, or while their head is outside the ropes.
- **How you behave:** holding and hitting, holding on to keep a clinch, wrestling, pushing an opponent into the ropes, crouching below the opponent's belt, spitting out the mouthpiece deliberately, and ignoring the referee.

The low-blow line is a good example of jurisdictions differing in the detail. Nevada's list describes hitting "below the navel". The ABC's referee manual instead tells referees to use the top of the hip — the belt line — and says neither the waistband of the trunks nor the navel determines it.

### What a foul costs

Referees usually escalate: soft warnings by hand signal, then a "hard" warning with time called, after which the referee manual tells them to consider a deduction for the next serious offence. A severe foul can cost a point without any warning, and in Nevada the referee sets the number of points by the foul's severity and deducts them in the round where it happened.

Two outcomes are fixed by the unified rules. An intentional foul that causes an injury severe enough to end the fight immediately is a loss by disqualification. If the injured boxer can continue, the deduction of two points from the offender is mandatory.

Accidental fouls are treated differently. If an accidental foul causes an injury that stops the bout before four rounds are complete, the result is a no decision. After four completed rounds it goes to the scorecards as a technical decision, with the partial round scored. Those labels are what end up in a fighter's record, which our guide to [reading a boxing record](/blog/how-boxing-records-are-written/) unpacks.

Low blows have their own rule. A boxer hit by an accidental low blow may take up to five minutes to recover; one who cannot continue after that loses. Nevada goes further: a boxer cannot be declared the winner on the strength of a low-blow claim, and one who refuses to continue because of it loses by technical knockout.
