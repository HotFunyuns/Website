Take the cap off a team-building game and it stops being a game. Without a budget, the correct move at every slot is the highest-rated available player, the puzzle solves itself, and there is nothing left to think about after run three.

The cap is the constraint that makes the decisions decisions. Understanding what kind of constraint it is — and what it quietly costs you — is most of the skill in a GM mode.

## What a cap actually is

A salary cap is a ceiling on the **total** a team may commit to player salaries. Not per player, not per position: the sum.

Real competitions adopt caps for two stated reasons — keeping teams competitive with each other, and controlling costs — and the mechanism is the same in a simulation. It removes the option of solving every problem by acquiring the best available answer to it.

The immediate effect is that quality stops being the only axis. Every player now has two numbers, and a pick is only good if the pair is good.

## Hard caps and soft caps produce different rosters

This distinction is worth internalising because it changes the strategy completely, not marginally.

A **hard cap** is absolute. There is no mechanism to exceed it. A roster over the limit is invalid, so your budget is a genuine wall and every unit you spend is permanently unavailable.

A **soft cap** can be exceeded, but only in defined ways — commonly through named exceptions that permit specific transactions, or by paying a penalty on the amount you go over. That penalty structure is usually called a **luxury tax**: rather than blocking the spend, it prices it.

The strategic consequence is direct. Under a hard cap, overspending is impossible, so all your errors are allocation errors. Under a soft cap, overspending is a *decision* — you can buy your way past the constraint if you accept the cost — so your errors are cost-benefit errors instead.

Games use both, and it is worth checking which one you are in before you plan, because a plan that assumes flexibility fails immediately against a wall.

## The vocabulary, defined once

- **Cap space** — the cap minus your current committed salaries. What you can still spend right now.
- **Cap hit** — the amount a single player's contract counts against the cap in a given period. Not always the same as the money actually paid.
- **Dead money** — cap consumed by a player who is no longer on the roster, or otherwise no longer contributing. It buys nothing.
- **Minimum salary** — the floor price of a roster slot. It is the reason your usable budget is smaller than your cap.
- **Luxury tax** — a penalty applied to spending above a threshold, in systems where exceeding the limit is permitted rather than forbidden.
- **Exception** — a defined circumstance under which a soft-cap team may make a signing it otherwise could not.

## Your real budget is smaller than your cap

This is the arithmetic beginners skip, and it is the one that ruins drafts.

Suppose a cap of **100 units**, a **10-player** roster, and a **minimum salary of 2 units**. Ten slots at the minimum costs 20, so only **80 units** are genuinely discretionary. Spend 60 on one player and you are not left with 40 to allocate freely — you are left with 40 that must cover nine slots, of which 18 is already committed as minimums. Your actual remaining freedom is **22 units spread across nine players**.

The habit worth building is to track *discretionary money per remaining slot* rather than raw cap space:

```
(cap space − remaining slots × minimum salary) ÷ remaining slots
```

Watch that number as you draft. When it approaches zero, you have already decided the rest of your roster — you just have not seen it yet.

## The only comparison that matters under a cap

Rating per unit of money is the obvious metric and it is misleading, because you are never choosing between a player and nothing. You are choosing between a player and the cheapest legal alternative.

Work it with numbers. Say a minimum-salary player at this position rates **60** and costs **2**.

- **Candidate A**: rated 90, costs 30. Improvement over the fallback: 30 rating points, for 28 extra units. That is **30 ÷ 28 ≈ 1.07 rating points per unit spent**.
- **Candidate B**: rated 80, costs 12. Improvement: 20 rating points, for 10 extra units. That is **20 ÷ 10 = 2.0 rating points per unit spent**.

Candidate B is close to twice as efficient, and the gap widens further once you account for what the 18 units you saved can buy elsewhere.

Two honest qualifications. Ratings do not necessarily convert to results in a straight line — the last ten points of quality may matter more than the first ten, in a real sport or in a simulation, and if they do, efficiency arguments understate the case for the expensive player. And efficiency is not the goal; the goal is the best roster you can field. Buying eleven efficient players and no good ones is its own failure mode.

Use the ratio to notice when you are overpaying, not as a rule that decides for you.

## Contract length is a cost you do not see on the sheet

In a single draft, price is the whole story. In a franchise that runs across seasons, **duration** is a second currency.

A long contract does three things at once: it secures a player, it commits money you cannot redeploy, and it removes an option you might want later. The first is why you sign it. The second and third are what you are paying, and they do not appear next to the salary.

This is where dead money comes from. Committing years to a player is a commitment to a future you cannot see, and when it goes wrong the money does not come back — it sits on your cap doing nothing while you try to build around it.

The practical rule that survives contact with actual play: be more willing to overpay in money than in years. An expensive short deal is a mistake you can exit. A cheap long one can be a mistake you carry.

## How this shows up in play

[Basketball Draft GM Franchise](/apps/pro-basketball-draft-gm-mode/) includes **Salary Cap Draft** as one of eight formats, alongside Unlimited, Quick, Blind and Franchise Drafts plus VS Computer, Battle Mode and a Daily Challenge. Running the same board twice — once unconstrained, once under the cap — is the fastest way to feel what a budget does. The rosters will not resemble each other, and the cap roster is usually the more interesting one to defend.

Past draft day, its GM Mode covers **contracts, trades and rotations**, which is where the duration problem becomes real. Trades are also the only tool that genuinely fixes a structural allocation mistake: they convert a surplus you cannot use into coverage you need, at a price.

[20-0 Football Draft & GM Mode](/apps/football-draft-gm-mode/) puts a Salary Cap Draft next to Unlimited, Limited and Playoff formats, and the football roster is the one where cap decisions bite hardest simply because there are more slots to fund. [162-0 Baseball Draft & GM Team](/apps/baseball-draft-gm-team/) includes payroll-focused drafting, and payroll review sits inside its Franchise GM Mode alongside the batting order, rotation and bullpen.

In all three, the currency is fictional and the resulting record is a model's score for how well the roster fits together, not a forecast of anything real.

## A checklist for your next cap draft

1. **Find out whether the cap is hard or soft** before you plan. Everything downstream depends on it.
2. **Subtract the minimums first.** Know your discretionary budget, not your headline budget.
3. **Price each candidate against the cheapest legal alternative**, not against zero.
4. **Recompute money-per-remaining-slot every few picks.** It is an early warning system.
5. **Fund your worst slot before upgrading your best.** A cap punishes weak links harder than it rewards strong ones, because you cannot buy your way out late.
6. **Prefer money over years** when a franchise runs across seasons.
