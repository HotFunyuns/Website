Anime hair is not shaded. It is **divided.**

The look everyone is trying to reproduce comes from cel shading, which is defined by exactly this: smooth lighting values are quantised into a small number of discrete shades, so that shadows and highlights appear as blocks of colour rather than being smoothly mixed in a gradient. The hard edge between tones is not a shortcut or a limitation. It is the style.

Which means the whole problem is choosing **where the boundaries go**, not how smoothly they blend.

## Three values, and why a fourth ruins it

Base, shadow, highlight. That is the working set.

- **Base** — the hair colour, flat across the whole mass.
- **Shadow** — one darker tone, in a defined shape, where the hair is behind or underneath.
- **Highlight** — one lighter band where the mass turns toward the light.

Adding a fourth tone is the single most common way a clean piece of hair turns muddy. With three well-separated values the eye reads three surfaces. With five closely spaced values it reads noise, because the differences are too small to be information and too large to be invisible.

If you want more complexity, **increase the separation between the three you have** rather than adding a fourth. A shadow that is clearly darker does more work than two shadows that are almost the same.

**Choose the shadow by hue, not by black.** Darkening a colour by mixing black toward it drains its saturation and greys the whole mass. The better move is to shift the hue slightly — toward blue or purple for cool light, toward red for warm — while lowering the lightness. Working in a hue-lightness-saturation space rather than raw RGB makes this a single deliberate adjustment instead of three fiddly ones; the [colour palettes article](/blog/color-palettes-for-anime-art/) goes into building a set that holds together across a whole figure.

## The highlight follows the skull, not the strands

This is the part beginners get wrong most often, and it is a conceptual error rather than a technical one.

A specular highlight is the bright spot that appears on a shiny object, and it sits where the surface is oriented halfway between the direction of the light and the direction of the viewer. Hair is drawn as a **curved mass sitting on a head**, so the place where that condition is met is an arc following the curve of the skull.

That is why the classic anime hair highlight is a band, usually above the eye line, running roughly with the shape of the head rather than with the direction of individual strands. Draw it along the strands instead and it will read as a stripe painted on hair rather than as light falling on it.

Two refinements worth knowing:

**Break the band.** A continuous unbroken arc looks like a plastic wig. Splitting it into two or three segments that follow the clumps keeps the curve readable while suggesting that hair is made of separate masses.

**Size tracks shininess.** Smooth, glossy surfaces produce sharp concentrated highlights; rougher surfaces scatter the reflection into a broader, dimmer one. A narrow hard band reads as silky hair; a wider, softer, lower-contrast band reads as matte or textured hair. That is a deliberate lever, not an accident of how carefully you drew it.

## Shade the clumps, not the hairs

Hair in this style is a small number of large forms with detail concentrated at the edges — the tips, the parting, the places where one clump crosses another.

So the shadow shape should describe **clumps**: this section falls behind that one, so it is in shadow; this section sits under the fringe, so it is darker; the hair at the nape is further from the light than the crown.

The failure mode is treating hair as thousands of strands and shading each one. It destroys the silhouette, which is the thing actually carrying the character design, and it takes ten times as long to produce something that reads worse at normal viewing size.

A quick check: **squint at it.** If the hair still reads as a clear shape with a light side and a dark side, the shading is working. If it dissolves into texture, there are too many values or too much strand-level detail.

## The order, and why it is not negotiable

1. **Flat base.** Fill the whole hair region in one colour. No shading, no variation.
2. **Shadow shape.** One tone, placed as a deliberate shape rather than brushed in.
3. **Highlight band.** One tone, following the skull curve, broken into segments.
4. **Edge detail.** A few strand tips, the parting, any small accents.

Each stage finishes before the next begins. The reason is practical: **the base is the reference for both the other tones.** Change it after the shadow and highlight are placed and both of them are now wrong relative to it, so you are redoing the work rather than adjusting it.

This is the same discipline as flats-before-shading in general digital colouring, which [line art and flats explained](/blog/line-art-and-flats-explained/) covers, and the broader ordering principles are in [digital colouring techniques](/blog/digital-coloring-techniques/).

## Doing it on a phone

The specific problem with a touchscreen is that the two operations here want opposite views.

**The shadow shape needs you zoomed out.** It is a large-scale decision about which parts of the mass are in light and which are not, and you cannot judge it looking at a quarter of the head.

**The highlight band needs you zoomed in.** It is a narrow shape, often only a few pixels wide at full view, and placing it at a zoomed-out scale with a fingertip produces something clumsy.

So the workflow is: zoomed out for step two, zoomed in for step three, zoomed out again to check. Toggling the view is the technique. Trying to do both at one zoom level is why phone colouring often looks either coarse or fussy.

## How the app is set up for this

[Anime Coloring Book: Paint Art](/apps/anime-coloring-book/) gives you line art with fill, a freehand brush, two erasers and zoom, pan and centre controls. Two decisions in it are directly relevant to hair.

**Hair is kept as separate fill regions rather than one shape.** It would be simpler to author a piece of line art with the whole head of hair as a single fillable region — fewer regions, faster to colour, cleaner to produce. We split it into clumps instead, so that a tap can fill one section darker than its neighbour.

That means the flat-fill tool alone can produce the base-and-shadow structure described above, without any brush work at all: fill the mass in the base colour, then fill the underneath clumps in the shadow tone. For someone colouring on a bus with a thumb, that is the difference between shaded hair being possible and not.

The cost is real and worth stating. **More regions means more taps**, and it means a stray tap can fill a small clump with the wrong colour. Someone who wants the whole head of hair in one flat colour has to make several taps instead of one. That is a deliberate trade — we optimised for the person who wants to shade over the person who wants to fill — and it is not the right trade for everyone.

**The zoom range is set so a highlight band stays tappable.** The upper limit of the zoom was chosen against a specific test: at maximum magnification, a highlight-sized region on a typical piece of hair has to be comfortably larger than a fingertip. Zoom that stops short of that makes fine work a matter of luck; zoom that goes far past it means a lot of panning for no benefit. Centre snaps the view back when you have lost your place.

## A short checklist

- Three values, well separated. Not four.
- Shadow by hue shift, not by black.
- Highlight follows the skull curve, broken into segments.
- Shade clumps, not strands.
- Base, shadow, highlight, edges — in that order, no going back.
- Squint at it. If it dissolves, simplify.
