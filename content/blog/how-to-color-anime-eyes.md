In anime art, the eyes carry the character. Wikipedia notes that exaggerated eye size is a common anime design convention, traced back to Osamu Tezuka, whose style allowed the full range of emotions to be shown through the eyes alone. That makes eyes the part of a page most worth getting right. This guide breaks colouring them into steps, with the colours worked out.

## The parts you are colouring

Anime eyes simplify real anatomy, but the parts carry over. Wikipedia describes the **iris** as the ring-shaped, coloured part of the eye, with the **pupil**, the black circle, at its centre, all surrounded by the white **sclera**. Eye colour is defined by the iris. For colouring, that gives four areas:

| Area | Role | Suggested value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iris | The colour of the eye | Three shades: dark, mid, light |
| Pupil | The centre of the iris | The darkest value |
| Sclera | The white of the eye | Light, shaded under the lid |
| Highlights | Reflections of the light | The lightest value |

Wikipedia adds that anime artists may add variable shading to the eyes, particularly the cornea, for depth, and that generally a mixture of **a light shade, the tone colour and a dark shade** is used. That three-shade iris is the foundation.

## Step 1: a flat iris

Fill the iris with its **tone colour**, the mid value. Use the fill tool, then zoom in to check the edges for tiny gaps between the iris and the lash line.

## Step 2: the gradient

Next, darken the **top** of the iris and lighten the **bottom**. The reasoning is to treat the upper eyelid and lashes as overhanging the eye and shading its upper part. That simple gradient helps a flat disc read as a curved, recessed eye.

**A worked example.** Start with a blue tone colour, RGB (58, 123, 213), hex #3A7BD5:

- **Dark shade**: multiply each channel by 0.6 for (35, 74, 128), #234A80, across the top third.
- **Tone colour**: #3A7BD5 through the middle.
- **Light shade**: mix 40% of the way towards white for (137, 176, 230), #89B0E6, in a crescent near the bottom.

The light crescent sits opposite the dark band, which strengthens the sense of depth. The same proportions can be applied to any iris colour, from brown to green to violet.

## Step 3: the pupil

Add the **pupil** at the centre of the iris in the darkest value, such as near-black or a very dark version of the iris colour; Wikipedia describes the real pupil as a circular black spot. Keep it round and centred unless the art style calls for something else, and let the top of it sink into the dark band so the two read as one shadowed area.

## Step 4: shade the whites

Wikipedia calls the sclera white, but a flat white can look pasted on. Add a soft shadow under the upper lid, in a pale cool grey or grey-blue, so the white of the eye curves away under the lid rather than looking pasted on. Keep the rest light.

## Step 5: highlights last

Highlights go on last, on top of everything. A **catch light**, as Wikipedia explains, is a light source reflected as a specular highlight in a subject's eye, and photographers use it to draw attention to the eyes. Wikipedia's article on specular highlights notes that, in 3D computer graphics, they give a strong visual cue to an object's shape and its position relative to light sources.

Two suggestions follow:

1. **Consistency.** Put the main highlight in the same position in both eyes, on the side facing the light, so both eyes agree about where the light is.
2. **Restraint.** One larger highlight and one small secondary one is a simple starting point; adding many more can clutter the iris.

## Matching eyes to the face

Eyes sit in skin, so their shadows should agree with the face's. If the face is shaded from the upper left, the iris gradient and highlights should match.
