Start from the destination. If you are heading to Indonesia, study Indonesian. If you are heading to Malaysia, Brunei or Singapore, study Malay. If you have family on one side of that line, study theirs. Everything else in this article is refinement, and the refinement matters less than people expect, because the two standards are close enough that the second one costs you weeks rather than years.

## They are two standards of one language, not two languages

This is the fact that reframes the whole question. Indonesian is described in reference sources as a standardised variety of Malay, standardised from Classical Malay of the Riau-Lingga tradition. Malay in the wider sense is official in Brunei, Malaysia and Singapore, and in Indonesia in the form of Indonesian.

So the choice is closer to British versus American English than to French versus Italian, except that the divergence is a bit wider and lands in different places.

| Dimension | Malay (Standard) | Indonesian | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Official status | Brunei, Malaysia, Singapore | Indonesia | Malay language; Indonesian language |
| Speakers (2020 figures) | About 80 million L1, close to 300 million including L2 across the family | 75 million L1, 252 million total | Malay language; Indonesian language |
| Script | Latin (*Rumi*); Jawi co-official in Brunei and Malaysia | Latin | Malay language |
| Main loanword layer | English | Dutch | Comparison of Indonesian and Standard Malay |
| Spelling of borrowings | *televisyen*, *kualiti* | *televisi*, *kualitas* | Comparison of Indonesian and Standard Malay |
| Everyday divergence | *pejabat pos* (post office) | *kantor pos* (from Dutch *kantoor*) | Comparison of Indonesian and Standard Malay |
| Shared spelling reform | 1972 reform aligned both on c and j | Same | Comparison of Indonesian and Standard Malay |

The grammar column is missing from that table because there is nothing to put in it. Both have Latin script, no tones, no grammatical gender, no case, no verb agreement for person or number, and the same affix machinery — the meN-, ber-, ter-, di-, -kan and -i system covered in our [Malay affixes guide](/blog/malay-affixes-guide/) works the same way in both.

## Where the divergence actually bites

Conversationally, less than you would think. In writing and in formal registers, more.

Wikipedia's comparison article reports an intelligibility test in which around **30 per cent of the totality** of Indonesian newspaper content was found "odd, unintelligible and unusual" by Malaysian speakers. That is one reported test in a survey article rather than a settled measurement, and it should be read as an order of magnitude rather than a number. But the direction is right, and it matches what learners report: a conversation goes fine and a newspaper editorial does not.

The reason is the loanword layer. Indonesian took its administrative, technical and institutional vocabulary largely from Dutch; Malaysian Malay took a comparable layer from English. Those are the words that cluster in formal writing, which is exactly where the two texts stop resembling each other. There is also a coordinating body, MABBIM, spanning Brunei, Indonesia and Malaysia, and the same source is blunt that its coordination "is not working properly" — so do not assume the two standards are converging on your timetable.

## The resourcing question, and where we are honestly weaker

Here is the argument against choosing Malay, and it is a real one.

Indonesian reaches more people by the published figures, and in our own experience of building for these languages, more material exists for learners: more courses, more graded readers, more podcasts, more people to practise with online. When a language has a larger learner population, everything downstream of that gets easier, and that advantage compounds over the year or two that actually matters.

If your goal is simply "a Southeast Asian language, I do not mind which", that consideration should probably decide it, and it points away from us.

## Why we built a Malay course and not an Indonesian one

The short version: we build one language per app, and Malay was the one we could resource properly.

The longer version is that the decision forced specific choices we then had to live with. Committing to Standard Malay meant committing to Malaysian spelling of borrowings throughout — *televisyen* rather than *televisi*, *kualiti* rather than *kualitas* — and to the Malaysian everyday word where the two differ, *pejabat* rather than *kantor*. It meant our travel, shopping and transport units are built around Malaysian and Bruneian contexts. It meant that a learner who is actually going to Jakarta will find a course that is 80 per cent useful to them and confidently wrong in the remaining 20, which is a worse experience than being obviously wrong would be.

So [Learn Malay - Bahasa Melayu](/apps/learn-malay/) is explicit about what it is. The culture and regional notes exist precisely to flag where usage differs across communities, and the course does not treat Indonesian as the same language. Vocabulary is grouped by situation rather than presented as lists, grammar is taught on Malay terms — word order, pronouns, possession, questions, classifiers, reduplication, passive forms and the affix system — and the review modes, Daily Review, Mistake Rescue, Listening Blitz, Sentence Builder and Dictation Mode among them, pull back whatever you got wrong. There is no hearts or lives system, so mistakes never block you.

What it is not: an Indonesian course with a different flag on it.

## What to do if you pick the other one

Learn Indonesian, and then read Malay later if you need it. The transfer is genuinely high, and the adjustment is a vocabulary list plus a spelling convention, not a second grammar.

The reverse is equally true, which is the most reassuring thing in this article: **whichever you pick, you have not spent the other one.** Choose by destination, and if you have no destination, choose Indonesian for the resourcing and accept that you will one day spend a fortnight learning that *kantor* is *pejabat*.
