**From the bottom up, affiliated minor league baseball runs: rookie complex leagues, Single-A, High-A, Double-A and Triple-A, with the major leagues above.** Minor League Baseball's own description is 120 full-season affiliated teams across four levels — Triple-A, Double-A, High-A and Single-A — plus rookie leagues in Arizona, Florida and the Dominican Republic each summer. Its list of teams by affiliate shows every major-league organisation with exactly one club at each full-season level.

Two things trip people up. The first is that many explainers online, including some official ones, describe an older structure with short-season and differently named Class A levels; this article uses Minor League Baseball's current pages, checked on 2 October 2026. The second is that the levels are not just stepping stones of difficulty. Each one tells you something about a player's stage of development, and a promotion between them is a signal in its own right.

## The ladder in one table

| Level | Leagues listed by MiLB (2026) | Season | Typical role in a player's path |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rookie | Arizona Complex League, Florida Complex League, Dominican Summer League | Summer | First stop for many newly signed and drafted players |
| Single-A | California League, Carolina League, Florida State League | Full season | The lowest full-season level: the first real professional season |
| High-A | Midwest League, Northwest League, South Atlantic League | Full season | A stepping stone between the lower and upper minors |
| Double-A | Eastern League, Southern League, Texas League | Full season | The start of the "upper minors," where prospects are sorted |
| Triple-A | International League, Pacific Coast League | Full season | The last stop before the majors, mixing prospects and veterans |

That is 11 full-season leagues, matching the count Minor League Baseball gives on its about page, plus the three rookie leagues. Minor League Baseball also notes that players reach the system by being drafted or by signing as undrafted or international free agents, and that there are no open tryouts.

## Rookie ball: the complex leagues

The lowest level is the rookie leagues: the Arizona and Florida complex leagues and a summer league in the Dominican Republic. MiLB's 2016 explainer, written for an earlier structure, gives the reasoning that still applies: the draft happens in the middle of the major-league season, and many newly drafted players — especially those straight out of high school — are not ready to step into an ongoing season. Rookie ball lets them settle into professional life first.

What a promotion out of rookie ball signals: the organisation thinks the player is ready for a full professional season.

## Single-A and High-A: the first full seasons

Single-A is the lowest full-season level in the current structure. The 2016 MiLB explainer describes the lowest full-season level as where players first go through spring training and play a season of more than five months, and it notes a common split: players straight out of high school usually start at the lower of the two A levels, while college players from major programmes can begin their first full season at the higher one.

High-A is the second rung. The older MLB.com farm-system explainer calls the advanced Class A level a stepping stone between the lower and upper minors, and notes that not every prospect stops at every level.

What a promotion to High-A signals: the player has handled the lowest full-season level and is being asked to face better competition.

## Double-A: where prospects are sorted

Double-A is where the minors get serious. MLB.com's explainer states the conventional wisdom directly: moving up to Double-A is the biggest jump before the one to the majors, and it separates true prospects. MiLB's explainer calls it the beginning of the "upper minors" and says the bar to reach it is high enough that anyone there has major-league potential. Both note that players sometimes go straight from Double-A to the majors.

What a promotion to Double-A signals: the organisation now treats the player as a real candidate for the major-league roster, not just a developing one.

## Triple-A: the waiting room

Triple-A is the level closest to the majors, made up of two leagues, the International League and the Pacific Coast League. It is also the most mixed level in the system. Both explainers describe a blend of young prospects, older players with major-league experience, and established major leaguers on rehab assignments working back from injury.

That mix is why age and statistics at Triple-A need care. A 30-year-old with a strong season may be a depth player the organisation already knows well; a 22-year-old with ordinary numbers may be a top prospect adjusting to the hardest competition below the majors.

What a promotion to Triple-A signals: proximity. The player is next in line when a major-league opening appears — though, as both explainers note, some of the best prospects skip the level entirely.

## Reading a minor league career

A practical way to read any player's path through the system:

1. **Pace matters more than the level itself.** The same level means different things for a teenager and for a college graduate a few years older.
2. **Double-A performance is the most informative.** If the conventional wisdom is right that it is the biggest jump before the majors, it is also the best test below them.
3. **Triple-A numbers come with context.** Veterans and rehab assignments make the level's statistics harder to compare.
4. **Skipped levels are a signal.** An organisation moving a player past a level is telling you what it thinks of him.

The broader logic — that development is front-loaded and opportunity depends on earning trust — is the same one [aging curves in career sims](/blog/aging-curves-in-career-sims/) and [how player career modes work](/blog/how-player-career-modes-work/) describe for simulated careers.

## The levels as a testing ground: 2026

The minors are also where baseball tries out rules, and the rules now differ by level. MLB.com notes that the pitch timer and the ABS challenge system both began as minor league experiments. Its preview of the experimental rules for 2026, which it says have no imminent arrival in the majors, shows how far they vary: Double-A cut the disengagement limit from two to one per plate appearance, the Triple-A Pacific Coast League added a check-swing challenge from May 5, and Single-A batters may not call time during an at-bat barring special circumstances. [MLB.com's rundown](https://www.mlb.com/news/new-rule-changes-coming-to-minor-leagues-in-2026) has the full list.

The practical consequence: statistics from different levels, or from the same level in different seasons, are not always produced under the same rules.
