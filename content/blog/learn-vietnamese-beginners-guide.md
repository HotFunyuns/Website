**Vietnamese removes one of the largest barriers a beginner usually faces — the script — and replaces it with a different one.** You will not spend a month learning to read. You will spend that month learning to hear and produce distinctions your ear has been trained to ignore.

This guide sets out what the language actually asks for, in the order it asks for it.

## What is easier than you expect

**The alphabet is Latin-based.** Vietnamese is written in *chữ Quốc ngữ*, an alphabet derived from Latin script. The letters are largely familiar, which means you can read a word aloud — badly — on day one. That is a genuine advantage over Thai, Khmer or Russian, where the first weeks go on decoding.

**The grammar is analytic.** Verbs do not change for person or tense. Nouns have no grammatical gender. There is no case system. Where an English speaker expects conjugation tables, Vietnamese uses separate words and word order.

**Vocabulary is compact in structure.** Most words are one syllable, and compounds are built transparently from those syllables.

## What is harder than you expect

**Tone.** Vietnamese is a tonal language, and the tone is part of the word rather than an accent on it. The same syllable with different tones is a different word. This is the single largest adjustment for a speaker of a non-tonal language, and it is covered in detail in [Vietnamese tones explained](/blog/vietnamese-tones-explained/) and, more generally, in [what is a tonal language](/blog/what-is-a-tonal-language/).

**Vowel precision.** Vietnamese distinguishes more vowel qualities than English does, and the diacritics that mark them are not decoration. The [Latin Extended Additional Unicode block](https://www.unicode.org/charts/PDF/U1E00.pdf) exists in large part to encode the Vietnamese combinations of vowel and tone marks — a good indication of how many distinct forms there are.

**Pronouns.** This is the one that surprises people most. Vietnamese does not have a single neutral "I" and "you". The words used depend on the relationship between the speakers — relative age, family relationship, social context. Choosing wrongly is not a grammar error; it is a social one. [Vietnamese pronouns explained](/blog/vietnamese-pronouns-explained/) covers the system.

**Classifiers.** Nouns take a classifier when counted, and the classifier depends on the kind of thing being counted. This is familiar to learners of Thai or Chinese and unfamiliar to most English speakers.

## A realistic first month

Four weeks, assuming short daily sessions rather than long occasional ones.

**Week 1 — Sounds and marks.**
Learn the alphabet with its diacritics, and start tone practice immediately. Do not postpone tone until "after the basics"; tone *is* the basics. Aim to recognise the six tones in isolation by the end of the week.

**Week 2 — High-frequency words in context.**
Greetings, numbers, family terms, food, and the vocabulary of a simple transaction. Learn words with their tone marks written, every time, without exception.

**Week 3 — Sentence patterns and pronouns.**
Basic word order, questions, negation, and the pronoun system as it applies to a few common situations. This is the week where Vietnamese starts feeling like a language rather than a list.

**Week 4 — Listening and consolidation.**
More listening than production. Review everything from weeks 1–3 in mixed order, and start noticing tone in connected speech rather than in isolated syllables.

## The habits that decide whether it sticks

- **Never write or learn a word without its tone.** A word learned untoned has to be learned again.
- **Do short sessions daily.** Tonal discrimination is a perceptual skill, and perceptual skills respond to frequency far more than duration.
- **Listen more than you speak, at first.** You cannot produce a distinction you cannot hear.
- **Get feedback from a fluent speaker.** No app can tell you which of six tones you actually produced, and that is the one thing you most need to know.
