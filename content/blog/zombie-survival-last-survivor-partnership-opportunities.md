[Zombie Survival: Last Survivor](/apps/zombie-survival-last-survivor/) is a horde-survival game set in a dead city, built around moving well and making upgrade choices under pressure. This page is for **survival-genre publishers** and **content collaborators**, and it covers the game's design, where new content fits, licensing and publishing options, and the sale route.

## One control, many decisions

The app's page on this site gives players **one job: keep moving**. Weapons **fire on their own**, so the game lives in positioning: staying out of the middle of the horde, keeping distance from fast enemies, never letting a swarm close in behind.

Depth comes from **mid-run levelling**. Experience collected during a stage promotes the survivor mid-battle, and each level is a choice among **weapon boosts, damage increases, survival upgrades and special abilities**. Because choices are made under pressure, the app's page on this site says, **no two runs end up the same shape**; the listing puts it as "every battle feels different as you choose upgrades".

## Where runs take place

The city is divided into stages: **dark streets, infected zones and abandoned areas**, each with its own enemy types and rising pressure. **Bosses** arrive with unique attack patterns that reward spotting openings over raw damage. Runs are short enough for a break and long enough to matter when things go well. The game is free with ads and optional in-app purchases.

## Content slots for collaborators

The structure invites modular content:

| Slot | What a collaborator could create |
| --- | --- |
| Stage | A new district with its own layout and hazards |
| Enemy type | Undead with a distinct movement pattern |
| Boss | A pattern-based encounter for a stage finale |
| Upgrade | A new weapon boost or special ability that combines with existing builds |

Each addition would need balancing against existing builds, which is why co-development may fit better than a one-off delivery.

## Publishing or licensing the zombie game

Publishers in the survival genre might consider **publishing and distribution** in new markets, **localisation** of menus and upgrade text, **licensing**, **white-label versions where technically feasible**, and **cross-promotion** with complementary action games. The audience described on the app's page on this site, players who like **roguelike survival runs with upgrade choices**, people who want **heavy action without complicated controls**, **zombie and horde survival fans** and players who need a game that fits **a five-minute gap or a long sitting**, is the profile a publisher would compare with its own.

## Limits partner copy should respect

Two limits, stated on the app's page on this site, keep partner copy accurate. The player controls **movement only; there is no manual aiming**, so marketing should not show aiming. And **ads support the free version, with optional in-app purchases**, so promotion should not suggest that progress requires spending.

## Tone and presentation

Zombie games range from cartoonish to grim. The listing's own language, a dead city, infected zones, swarms and the undead, sets the register, and a publisher or collaborator would match it rather than push it further. Store images and marketing would show the real game: a survivor moving through a horde with weapons firing automatically, not cinematic scenes the game does not contain.

## A worked example: balancing a new upgrade

A collaborator proposes a new special ability, a short-range shockwave. Before release, it would be tested against each upgrade category it can combine with: weapon boosts, damage increases and survival upgrades. If the team tests it alongside 4 representative builds in each of those 3 categories, that is 4 × 3 = 12 combinations to play through, each across several stages, to make sure the shockwave does not make other choices pointless. That testing plan would be part of the co-development agreement.

## Transfer requirements

Google Play completes a sale through its **developer-account transfer**. According to Google's Play Console help, the receiving account must be registered and active, the request needs both registration transaction IDs, and a game with in-app products needs an **active payments profile** on the receiving side. Google notes that the move carries **users, statistics, ratings, reviews and subscriptions**, while **promotions and test groups** stay behind, and that **ad SDK integrations are updated** after the transfer so ad traffic is credited to the correct account.
