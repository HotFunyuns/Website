A starting pitcher has two jobs, as MLB's glossary puts it: prevent runs and get outs. The **quality start** is a one-line test of whether he did both. It is simple and, as its critics point out, a little blunt. This guide explains the rule, its history and its limits.

## The six-innings, three-earned-runs rule

MLB's glossary defines it plainly: a starting pitcher records a quality start when he **pitches at least six innings and allows three earned runs or fewer**.

| Start | Innings | Earned runs | Quality start? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 6 | 3 | Yes, the minimum |
| B | 7 | 2 | Yes |
| C | 8 | 4 | No: too many runs |
| D | 5⅔ | 0 | No: not enough innings |
| E | 9 | 4 | No, despite a complete game |

Only **earned** runs count. MLB's glossary defines an earned run as any run that scores against a pitcher without the benefit of an error or a passed ball, often a judgment by the official scorer about whether the run would have scored without the defensive mistake. Runners a pitcher leaves on base still count against him if they later score earned runs, which is why a starter can lose a quality start after leaving the game. [Baseball stats explained](/blog/baseball-stats-explained-for-beginners/) covers the scorebook basics.

## John Lowe's 1985 invention

MLB's glossary credits **John Lowe**, then a sportswriter for the *Philadelphia Inquirer*, with coining the term in **1985** as a way to judge whether a pitcher did his job. Lowe added two companion terms:

- **Tough loss:** a quality start in which the pitcher took the loss.
- **Cheap win:** a non-quality start in which the pitcher earned the win.

The pair separates how well the starter pitched from whether the box score credited him with the win or the loss.

## The 4.50 ERA criticism and the complete-game gap

Earned run average is earned runs per nine innings; MLB's glossary gives the formula as 9 × earned runs ÷ innings pitched. The minimum quality start, three earned runs in six innings, is therefore a **4.50 ERA** for that game: 3 ÷ 6 × 9 = 4.50. Wikipedia describes this as an early criticism, first made by Moss Klein in *The Sporting News*, and quotes Tim McCarver calling it "Rubbish". The criticism has persisted: in 2022, a writer for *The Athletic* called the stat "garbage" and proposed alternatives.

A second criticism runs the other way. A pitcher who goes **nine innings and allows four earned runs** has a 4.00 ERA for the game, better than the minimum quality start, yet does not get one. The stat ignores how deep a pitcher goes beyond six innings. Wikipedia quotes the former pitcher Carl Erskine: "in my day, a quality start was a complete game".

## What the 1.91 average ERA says in its defence

The defence comes from the data. Wikipedia records that Bill James argued in his 1987 *Baseball Abstract* that the exact six-innings, three-runs case was extremely rare among quality starts, and that computer analysis of all quality starts from 1984 to 1991 found an **average ERA of 1.91** in them. On that evidence, the typical quality start was much better than the minimum.

**A worked comparison.** Over three starts, a pitcher goes 6 innings and 3 runs, 7 innings and 1 run, and 8 innings and 0 runs. All three are quality starts. Together: 4 earned runs in 21 innings, an ERA of 4 ÷ 21 × 9 ≈ **1.71**, far from 4.50.

## Quality starts beside other pitching stats
