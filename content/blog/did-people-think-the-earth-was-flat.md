Educated people did not think the earth was flat, and had not for a very long time before 1492. The argument for a spherical earth was made from ordinary observation in the fourth century BCE, and the story of Columbus overturning a flat-earth consensus was largely constructed in the nineteenth century. This article separates what the ancient sources actually say from where the modern story came from.

## What Aristotle actually argued

In *On the Heavens*, Book II, Aristotle gives two observational arguments. The first concerns eclipses: "in eclipses the outline is always curved: and, since it is the interposition of the earth that makes the eclipse, the form of this line will be caused by the form of the earth's surface, which is therefore spherical."

The second concerns travel. "our observations of the stars make it evident, not only that the earth is circular, but also that it is a circle of no great size. For quite a small change of position to south or north causes a manifest alteration of the horizon." He notes that "There are some stars seen in Egypt and in the neighbourhood of Cyprus which are not seen in the northerly regions."

Neither argument requires instruments, mathematics or authority. Both require only paying attention, which is why they travelled so well and lasted so long.

## The ancient and medieval consensus

Jeffrey Burton Russell, the historian whose work on the subject prompted the modern correction, put his conclusion bluntly in a paper given at a conference in 1997: "with extraordinary few exceptions no educated person in the history of Western Civilization from the third century B.C. onward believed that the earth was flat." He dates the settled consensus to the era of Eratosthenes in the third century BCE, writing that by then "the sphericity of the earth was accepted by all educated Greeks and Romans."

That is one historian's formulation, and it is worth treating as an argument rather than as a decree — Russell is making a case about the weight of the evidence, not reporting a vote. But it is a case that has held up, and it is checkable in a way most historiographical claims are not: the medieval texts survive, and many of them are online. Fordham University's Internet Medieval Sourcebook, edited by Paul Halsall, publishes medieval primary texts organised by period and topic, including a section on intellectual life, and reading in it is a faster route to what medieval writers thought than reading about what they are said to have thought.

## Where the story came from

Russell traces the modern flat-earth narrative to the 1830s and to two independent origins. The French scholar Antoine-Jean Letronne, whom Russell describes as holding strong antireligious prejudices, published a misrepresentation of medieval cosmology in 1834. Separately, the American writer Washington Irving invented the dramatic scene in which Columbus stands before the Council of Salamanca and is told by theologians that the earth is flat. Russell's verdict on that scene is one word: "moonshine."

The story was then generalised. John William Draper and Andrew Dickson White, writing later in the nineteenth century, folded it into a much larger thesis about a permanent war between science and religion, and from there it entered textbooks. That is how a novelist's invention acquired the authority of a school fact.

Two things are worth separating here. Whether there was ever a general "war between science and religion" is a live scholarly argument with serious participants on more than one side. Whether educated medieval Europeans believed the earth was flat is not; that claim was manufactured, and the manufacture is documented.

## Checking it outdoors

This is a rare case where a reader can inspect the evidence directly. The Royal Observatory's guide to lunar eclipses explains that an eclipse of the moon occurs when the earth lies directly between the sun and the moon and the moon passes into the earth's shadow, and it distinguishes the umbra, the darkest part of that shadow, from the fainter penumbra around it.

During a partial eclipse the boundary of the umbra is visible on the moon's face, and it is curved — which is precisely the observation Aristotle built on. Very few arguments from antiquity can be re-run from a back garden.

## What the episode teaches about studying history

The flat-earth myth is a case study in how a claim survives. It has a vivid scene, a hero, named villains and a moral. The correction has a bibliography. Guess which one is easier to repeat.

Three habits follow. First, trace a memorable scene to its earliest source; if the earliest source is a nineteenth-century popular book, you have found a retelling rather than a record. Second, notice when a story is doing argumentative work — this one exists to make a point about religion and science, and it was shaped to fit that point. Third, read the period's own texts where you can, which is what distinguishes a primary source from an account of one, as [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) sets out in full, and which the procedure in [analysing a primary source](/blog/how-to-analyse-a-primary-source/) puts into steps.

It also helps to know roughly where things sit in time. "Third century BCE", "the 1490s" and "the 1830s" are three different worlds, and a survey of [the major eras of world history](/blog/major-eras-of-world-history/) is the quickest way to stop them collapsing into an undifferentiated past.

## The short answer

Educated opinion held the earth to be a sphere from antiquity onward, on evidence anyone could check. The dramatic scene in which that consensus is overturned by a single voyage was invented by a nineteenth-century novelist, popularised by polemicists, and repeated for well over a century afterwards. The myth is not really about the middle ages at all. It is about the nineteenth century.
