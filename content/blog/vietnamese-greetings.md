*Xin chào* is the usual direct translation of "hello", but Wiktionary's usage notes say it is not how everyday greetings are normally made. Everyday greetings are built from *chào* plus a word that places the other person relative to you, which means a greeting is also a choice of pronoun. This guide covers hellos, the polite yes, thanks, apologies and goodbyes, with notes from Wiktionary on how each is actually used. Words are written in standard Vietnamese spelling with tone marks; see [Vietnamese tones explained](/blog/vietnamese-tones-explained/) for what the marks do.

## Why xin chào sounds like a textbook

Wiktionary breaks *xin chào* into *xin*, "to ask for", and *chào*, "to greet", and says it appears to be a modern coinage, not attested in pre-modern documents. Its usage note is blunt: the phrase is **rather artificial** as a greeting, often used as a direct translation of English "hello", and very common in interface labels. In formal settings speakers say **kính chào**; newsreaders and television hosts often use the softer **xin kính chào**. On the phone, Wiktionary adds, the default greeting is **a-lô**.

So *xin chào* is safe but slightly stiff. For daily use, learn the pattern below.

## Chào plus a kinship term: the everyday hello

Wiktionary states that everyday greetings use **chào followed by a kinship term**. Which term depends on the person, and Wikipedia's article on Vietnamese pronouns sets out the common ones used for people who are not relatives:

| Greeting | Say it to | Literal sense of the term |
| --- | --- | --- |
| chào anh | a man a little older than you | older brother |
| chào chị | a woman a little older than you | older sister |
| chào em | someone a little younger | younger sibling |
| chào cô | a woman a little younger than your parents; a female teacher | father's younger sister |
| chào chú | a man a little younger than your parents | father's younger brother |
| chào bác | someone a little older than your parents | parent's older sibling |
| chào ông, chào bà | an old man, an old woman | grandfather, grandmother |

Wikipedia notes that in formal contexts only a few terms are used, based on how young or old the stranger appears, and that the plural marker **các** makes a group: *các anh*. For an audience, terms are strung together, as in *các anh chị em*, or *quý vị*, "esteemed guests", which appears in Wiktionary's formal greeting *Xin kính chào quý vị và các bạn*. The logic behind these choices is covered in [Vietnamese pronouns explained](/blog/vietnamese-pronouns-explained/).

## A worked example: four greetings, four relationships

Imagine you are 25 and greet four people in turn.

1. **A 30-year-old man**: *Chào anh.* He is a little older, so he is *anh*.
2. **A 20-year-old woman**: *Chào em.* She is younger, so she is *em*.
3. **Your friend's 55-year-old father**: *Chào bác.* He is a little older than your own parents might be, the use Wikipedia gives for *bác*.
4. **An 80-year-old woman**: *Cháu chào bà.* Wikipedia notes that a young speaker addressing a very old person generally uses *ông* or *bà* for them and *cháu* or *con* for themselves.

Wiktionary's own example has the same shape: *(Cháu) chào chú (ạ)*, where the speaker names themselves *cháu*, greets the listener as *chú*, and may add a polite *ạ* at the end. The 30, 20, 55 and 80 are illustrations; in real life the choice is a judgement about relative age, and Wikipedia notes it may be made by asking or by guesswork.

## Dạ and vâng: answering politely

Wiktionary lists **dạ** as a "yes" that answers a question and as an initial particle that expresses politeness and respect. It also works as "yes?" when someone calls you, and as a sign that you are listening. **Vâng** is labelled northern and polite. They combine in *Dạ vâng*, which Wiktionary glosses "Yes, Sir / Yes, Madam". Regional differences like this one are covered in [northern vs southern Vietnamese](/blog/northern-vs-southern-vietnamese/).

## Cảm ơn or cám ơn, and xin lỗi

"Thank you" has two spellings. Wiktionary records that **cảm ơn** was adopted as the standard form in a 1992 dictionary from Hanoi's Institute of Linguistics, but that **cám ơn** remains the predominant spoken form, especially among Central and Southern speakers, if not nationwide. Either is understood.

**Xin lỗi**, literally "to beg for one's mistake", covers both "I'm sorry" and the polite "excuse me" before a question: *Xin lỗi, cho hỏi đây có phải là nhà anh Tiến không ạ?*, "Excuse me, is this Mr Tiến's address?". For "I'm sorry" in the sense of "sorry about your loss" or "sorry for the inconvenience", Wiktionary notes that translators may use *rất tiếc*, "that's a pity", as in *Rất tiếc, chúng tôi hết phòng đôi rồi ạ*, "I'm sorry, we have run out of twin rooms".

## Goodbyes: chào again, not tạm biệt

Wiktionary notes that **chào** means both "hello" and "goodbye", and its example *Chào mọi người!* can mean "Hello, everybody!" or "Bye, everybody!". The phrasebook goodbye **tạm biệt** is, in Wiktionary's words, almost never used in conversation; it is mostly seen in translations and in language teaching. Everyday alternatives are *chào* or *bai*, preferably with the final particles **nhé** or **nha**.

The same caution applies to *hẹn gặp lại*, "see you later", which Wiktionary describes as more a translation of the English phrase than a natural Vietnamese one. It suggests *gặp lại sau nhé* or simply *sau nhé*.
