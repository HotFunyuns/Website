**Short daily practice is not a compromise for busy people. For the same weekly total, it works better.** That is the useful thing to know, because it means the sustainable option and the effective option are the same option.

## Why distributed practice wins

Spreading study over time substantially outperforms massing it into one session — the effect synthesised across a large body of research by [Cepeda and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/), and one of the techniques rated most effective in the [Dunlosky review of learning methods](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/).

The practical translation: **twenty minutes a day beats two and a half hours on Sunday**, at the same weekly total. Seven opportunities to encode, retrieve and consolidate rather than one.

That reframes the whole problem. You are not choosing between "ideal" and "realistic". Daily short sessions are the ideal.

## Design for your worst day

The most common way a practice habit dies is that it was designed for a good day.

Someone sets a forty-minute daily session, manages it for two weeks, hits a bad week, misses four days, and concludes the habit has failed.

**The fix is a floor you can always hit.** Five minutes. Ten if five feels insulting.

Then:

- **On ordinary days**, do more if you want to. The floor is a minimum, not a target.
- **On bad days**, do the floor. It counts.
- **The floor must be small enough that "no time" is never true.** If it is not, it is a target pretending to be a floor.

The value of the floor is not the five minutes of practice. It is that the habit survives to the following week.

## Remove the decision

The failure point is rarely the practice itself. It is deciding to start.

The reliable fix is to attach practice to something you already do, at a fixed point:

- After a specific meal
- With the first coffee
- On a particular leg of a commute
- Immediately after a fixed daily event

Attaching it removes the decision — you are not choosing to practise, you are doing the thing that comes after breakfast. Removing the decision removes the failure.

## What streaks are good and bad at

Most learning apps use streaks, ours included, and they are worth being clear about.

**Good at:** prompting the first few weeks. A visible run is a genuine motivator while the habit is forming and has no momentum of its own.

**Bad at:** surviving a break. A streak assigns value to an unbroken run, so losing it feels like losing the accumulated progress. People frequently stop entirely after breaking a long streak, which is the opposite of what the mechanic is for.

**How to use one sanely:**

- Treat it as a prompt, not a score.
- Decide in advance that a missed day means doing the next session, not restarting a project.
- If a broken streak would make you quit, stop looking at it.

The underlying point: **the run is not the progress. The practice is.**

## What to do after a missed day

Do the next session.

That is the complete answer. One missed day has negligible effect on learning. The effect that matters is the interpretation — treating it as evidence that the habit has failed, and acting accordingly.

There is a version of this failure that is worth naming, because it is so common: missing a day, then avoiding the app because opening it would show the broken streak, then missing a week. The avoidance costs far more than the original miss.

## When the returns get thin

Practice habits are hardest to maintain when progress stops being visible — which happens in almost every skill at the intermediate stage.

In language learning this is the plateau, covered in [the language learning plateau](/blog/language-learning-plateau/). The same shape appears in arithmetic, music and most other practised skills: rapid early improvement, then a long stretch where the same effort produces less visible change.

The habit has to carry you through that stretch, which is exactly when the floor matters most.

## A workable setup

1. **Pick a floor you can always hit.** Five to ten minutes.
2. **Attach it to a fixed daily event.**
3. **Use retrieval, not review.** Test yourself rather than re-reading — see [memory techniques that actually work](/blog/memory-techniques-that-work/).
4. **Let the app handle scheduling** where it does. [Spaced repetition for language learning](/blog/spaced-repetition-for-language-learning/) covers what review systems do and do not automate.
5. **Decide your missed-day policy in advance**, while you are calm.
