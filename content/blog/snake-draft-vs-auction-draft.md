Both formats hand out the same players to the same number of managers, and they produce completely different rosters. The difference is not cosmetic and it is not about pacing. It is that they ration a scarce resource in two incompatible ways, and the skill each one measures barely overlaps with the other.

## The difference stated once, plainly

In a **snake draft**, the scarce resource is **turn order**. Managers pick in sequence, that sequence reverses each round, and you may only take a player nobody has taken yet.

In an **auction draft**, the scarce resource is **money**. Every manager gets an identical notional budget, players are put up one at a time, and each goes to whoever bids highest.

Everything else follows from that one substitution. Which is why the useful question is not "which is better" but "what does each one make impossible".

## Snake drafts: order is the currency

### The reversal, and why it exists

Round one runs 1 through *n* in slot order. Round two runs the same managers in reverse. Round three reverts to the original order, and so on.

With ten managers, that means:

| Slot | Round 1 pick | Round 2 pick | Round 3 pick |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1 | 20 | 21 |
| 5 | 5 | 16 | 25 |
| 10 | 10 | 11 | 30 |

Add the first two picks for each and the totals are 21, 21 and 21. That is the whole design. Without the reversal, the manager at slot 1 would take picks 1, 11 and 21 while slot 10 took 10, 20 and 30, and the gap would widen every round.

Note the asymmetry the reversal creates as a side effect: slot 1 waits nineteen picks between selections, slot 10 waits one. Equal total, very different rhythm.

### What snake drafting rewards

**Reading the board.** Because you cannot choose who is available, most of your skill goes into predicting who *will* be. Recognising that a position is about to dry up and taking it a round early is the core competence.

**Discipline under a clock.** Snake drafts move. The manager who prepared tiers in advance makes better decisions at speed than the one comparing rows in real time.

**Accepting good enough.** You will not get the player you wanted. The format is largely about being unbothered by that.

### What snake drafting takes away

You have no mechanism for wanting something more than someone else. If two managers value the same player, the one picking earlier gets them, full stop. Enthusiasm is not expressible in the format.

That also means every roster in a snake draft ends up structurally similar. Everyone gets one early pick, one late-early pick, and so on down. The spread between the best and worst roster is real but bounded.

## Auction drafts: money is the currency

### How a round works

A player is nominated. Managers bid in increments. Highest bid wins, the amount is deducted from that manager's budget, and the next nomination begins. This repeats until every roster is full.

The critical property: **nobody is out of reach**. If you want the best player in the pool badly enough to spend a third of your budget, you can have them, regardless of where you sit at the table.

### The arithmetic you must hold in your head

Auctions have one hard constraint that beginners break constantly. You must be able to fill every roster slot, so some of your budget is already spoken for.

Your **maximum legal bid** on any player is:

```
remaining budget − (slots left to fill after this one × minimum bid)
```

With a 200-unit budget, 12 roster slots and a 1-unit minimum: your first-player ceiling is 200 − 11 = **189**. After spending 60 on that player, you hold 140 with 11 slots left, so your next ceiling is 140 − 10 = **130**.

That is a ceiling, not a plan. The plan is the second calculation: your **average remaining spend**, which is simply remaining budget divided by remaining slots. Start with 200 and 12 slots and you average 16.7 per slot. Spend 90 on two players and you have 110 for 10 slots, or 11 each. Watching that average fall is how you feel a roster going top-heavy while there is still time to stop it.

### Nomination is a strategic act

Because you nominate players as well as bid on them, you get a tool that does not exist in a snake draft: you can put up a player you do not want, specifically because other managers do, and watch their budgets shrink.

This is legitimate and it is most of what separates experienced auction drafters from new ones. The reflex to nominate your own favourite first is exactly backwards — it invites everyone else to bid you up on the player you least want to lose.

### What auction drafting rewards

**Valuation.** You have to convert "how good is this player" into "how many units is this player worth", for the whole pool, before the format starts charging you for being wrong.

**Patience.** Budgets deplete. The manager who has kept powder dry while others spent can buy well in the last third at prices nobody could pay earlier.

**Structural intent.** You can genuinely decide what shape your roster will be — three expensive players and nine cheap ones, or twelve solid ones — and then execute it. In a snake draft, the shape is largely imposed on you.

### What auction drafting takes away

Time and forgiveness. An auction runs several times longer than the equivalent snake draft, and every manager has to stay engaged the entire way. A single overbid early does not cost you one slot; it lowers the ceiling on every remaining slot for the rest of the draft.

## Side by side

| | Snake | Auction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scarce resource | Pick order | Budget |
| Can you get any player? | No | Yes, at a price |
| Main skill | Reading the board | Valuing the pool |
| Cost of one bad decision | One slot | Every later slot |
| Roster shapes produced | Similar across managers | Highly variable |
| Length | Short | Long |
| Extra strategic tool | None | Nomination |

## So which should you play?

If you are learning to draft, snake. The feedback loop is faster, the decisions are cleaner, and you will run ten drafts in the time one auction takes. Almost everything you learn about tiers, scarcity and replacement value transfers to auctions later.

Choose an auction when the thing you actually want is control over roster shape — when the interesting question to you is *how much is too much for one player*, rather than *who will still be here in two rounds*.

And be honest about the group. Auctions are unforgiving of a table where half the managers have not thought about valuation, because the prices stop meaning anything and the format's whole premise collapses.

## Where our games sit, precisely

It is worth being exact here rather than approximate.

[Basketball Draft GM Franchise](/apps/pro-basketball-draft-gm-mode/) ships eight draft formats: Unlimited Draft, Quick Draft, Blind Draft, Franchise Draft, Salary Cap Draft, VS Computer, Battle Mode and a Daily Challenge. **Salary Cap Draft** is the budget-constrained one. Every pick has a price as well as a rating, and you have to complete a roster inside a limit — which exercises the same valuation muscle an auction demands. It is not an auction: the catalogue describes drafting under a cap, not live bidding against other managers.

[162-0 Baseball Draft & GM Team](/apps/baseball-draft-gm-team/) includes payroll-focused drafting alongside Unlimited Draft, a ten-pick Limited Refresh Draft with no rerolls, VS Computer, Battle Mode, a Daily Challenge and Baseball IQ. Its Franchise GM Mode carries payroll review past draft day.

If you want the auction skill without an auction, cap formats are the closest practice available: they force you to answer "what is this player worth in currency" on every pick, which is the question a snake draft never asks you.

One thing to keep clear in both: the money is a simulation value. In-game salaries, payrolls and budgets have no real-world worth, and the record a simulator returns is a score for how well you built the roster — not a prediction about anything outside the app.
