Ibn Battuta left Tangier in 1325 as a young man of legal training intending to make the pilgrimage to Mecca, and did not settle back in Morocco for roughly twenty-nine years. The book that resulted is the single richest surviving description of the fourteenth-century Islamic world from inside it. It is also dictated from memory long after the fact, shaped by a professional editor, and in places demonstrably borrowed from someone else's book. Both of those statements are true at once, and learning to hold them together is what makes the *Rihla* such a good place to learn source criticism.

## What actually happened, as far as we can tell

The outline is not seriously disputed. He set out in 1325 in his early twenties. He crossed North Africa to Egypt, reached Mecca, and then kept going: Iraq and Iran, the Arabian coast, down the East African coast as far as Kilwa, up through Anatolia and across the steppe, into Central Asia, and then to India, where he spent years in the service of the Delhi Sultanate as a judge. He was sent onward as an envoy, was shipwrecked, spent a long spell in the Maldives — again as a judge — and by his own account reached China before turning back. He returned to Morocco around 1354, and afterwards made the journey across the Sahara to the Mali empire.

That is an extraordinary life by any standard, and the reason to be careful is not that any of it is implausible. It is that the record of it was produced under conditions that introduce specific, identifiable distortions.

## Who actually wrote the book

Ibn Battuta did not sit down and write a travel book. At the end of his travelling life the Marinid sultan of Morocco directed him to give an account of them, and he dictated it to Ibn Juzayy al-Kalbi, a scholar and literary secretary from Granada. Ibn Juzayy finished the text in 1355. Its full title translates roughly as *A Gift to Those Who Contemplate the Wonders of Cities and the Marvels of Travelling*, which is a mouthful, so it is universally called the *Rihla* — "the journey", a recognised Arabic literary genre with its own conventions.

Three consequences follow, and they matter more than any single disputed episode.

**It is a memory document.** He was recalling journeys that had begun thirty years earlier, without, so far as we know, a running diary to work from. Long-delayed recall reorders events, merges similar places and sharpens anecdotes.

**It is an edited document.** Ibn Juzayy says plainly in the text that he abridged and polished the material he was given. Where the prose is most elegant, we are furthest from dictation.

**It is a genre document.** A *rihla* was expected to do certain things: record the scholars and judges the traveller met, describe holy sites, demonstrate the unity of the Islamic world. Material that served those expectations had a better chance of surviving into the final text than material that did not.

And then there is the borrowing. Scholars comparing the *Rihla* with Ibn Jubayr's twelfth-century account of his own pilgrimage have found passages where the later book reproduces the earlier one. The descriptions concerned are among the most frequently quoted in the whole work. They are not eyewitness reporting, and anyone quoting them as such is quoting the wrong century.

## Which parts specialists doubt, and why

Doubt in this literature is specific rather than general. The scholarly biography by Ross E. Dunn, published by the University of California Press, is the standard English-language discussion and works precisely by sorting section from section.

The recurring problem areas are the stretches where the chronology will not fit the itinerary — where the time available does not accommodate the journey described — and the stretches where the detail thins out into generalities that could have come from other people's reports. A side journey north toward Bulghar, and parts of the China narrative, have both attracted that kind of scrutiny. The sceptical case is not that he was inventing a career; it is that a traveller dictating in 1355 may have folded hearsay about places he did not reach into an account of places he did.

That is a more useful kind of doubt than "is it true or false", and it is the same posture recommended in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/): treat a document as a mixture of evidentiary qualities rather than as a single verdict. For the general method of weighing claims about an individual, [how we know what historical figures actually did](/blog/how-we-know-what-historical-figures-did/) sets out the ladder this article is climbing.

## What the Rihla is uniquely good for

Now the other half, because scepticism that produces nothing is just a pose.

For the fourteenth-century Mali empire, Ibn Battuta's account is close to irreplaceable. He travelled across the Sahara in the early 1350s and spent months in the empire under Mansa Sulayman, and his description of court protocol, the administration of justice, food, social conduct and the conditions of the journey is the fullest eyewitness material that survives from any outsider. Historians of West Africa read it alongside Arabic geographers and the later Timbuktu chronicles, and they read it critically — he was frequently unimpressed and sometimes plainly disapproving — but they read it. UNESCO's account of Timbuktu, which places the city's height in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries and links the Djingareyber Mosque to Mansa Musa's return from pilgrimage, describes the world he was moving through.

For the Swahili coast he is similarly valuable: his visits in the early 1330s give a picture of ports whose material remains at Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara — which UNESCO describes as handling gold, silver, pearls, Arabian and Persian wares and Chinese porcelain between the thirteenth and sixteenth centuries — otherwise have to speak for themselves.

For the Delhi Sultanate he is an insider rather than a visitor. He worked in its judicial apparatus, knew the people involved, and had reason to understand how the system operated. That is a different and stronger evidentiary position than tourism.

And for the whole Afro-Eurasian world he is proof of something structural: that a Moroccan jurist could find employment, hospitality, shared law and shared language from West Africa to the Indian Ocean. The connectivity that [the Silk Road](/blog/the-silk-road-for-beginners/) represents for the overland routes had a maritime and legal counterpart, and one man's employment record demonstrates it better than any map.

## About those distance figures

You will see a number attached to him — 75,000 miles is the figure given on the University of California, Berkeley teaching resource, and other sources give something close to it. Treat these as reconstructions, not measurements. They are produced by plotting the itinerary described in the *Rihla* onto a modern map and adding up the legs, which means every doubtful section is inside the total. The number is a way of saying "an enormous amount", and that is all it can honestly be.

The same applies to the country count. Modern national borders did not exist, and counting them tells you about cartography today rather than travel then.

## How to read him well

Ask of any passage: is this somewhere he stayed, or somewhere he passed through, or somewhere he heard about? Is the detail administrative and specific, or is it the kind of scene a reader would expect? Does the chronology work? Is this a description that also appears in Ibn Jubayr?

Applied honestly, those questions leave a great deal of the *Rihla* standing, and what is left standing is remarkable. A single fourteenth-century jurist, whose book has to be argued with page by page, is still the best window anyone has into how vast a world could feel to one person who crossed it on foot, by camel and by ship.
