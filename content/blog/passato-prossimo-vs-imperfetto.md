Passato prossimo vs imperfetto is a choice Italian asks you to make almost every time you talk about the past. In English, "I went to school by bike" can describe one trip or, with the right context, a regular habit. Italian splits them: the **passato prossimo** for an event that happened and finished, the **imperfetto** for what used to happen, what was going on, and how things were. This comparison sets out what each tense is for, how each is built, and how to choose when you are telling a story.

## Two tenses, two kinds of past

Wikipedia's article on Italian conjugation gives the clearest division.

| | Passato prossimo | Imperfetto |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Used for | single actions or events; changes of state | habits; repeated actions with no specific time; ongoing or interrupted actions; past states |
| Example | *stamattina **sono andato** a scuola*, "I went to school this morning" | *andavo in bicicletta a scuola ogni mattina*, "I used to go to school by bike every morning" |
| Change of state vs repetition | *si è arrabbiato quando gliel'ho detto*, "he got angry when I told him" | *si arrabbiava ogni volta che qualcuno glielo diceva*, "he got angry every time someone told him" |

The last row shows the trap. Both English sentences say "got angry", but Italian picks the passato prossimo for the one occasion and the imperfetto for the repeated pattern.

Wikipedia lists three uses of the imperfect: **repeated or habitual actions** in the past; **ongoing actions**, including ones that are eventually interrupted; and **states and conditions** in the past, specifically including weather, time and age. Its Italian grammar article glosses *facevo* as both "I used to do" and "I was doing", which captures the first two in one form.

## How each tense is built

**The passato prossimo** is a compound tense: the present of *avere* or *essere* plus a past participle. Wikipedia's tables give *ho fatto*, "I have done" or "I did". Regular participles end in *-ato*, *-uto* or *-ito*: *parlato*, *creduto*, *finito*. Many common verbs are irregular: *fatto*, *detto*, *letto*, *preso*, *visto*.

Which auxiliary a verb takes is its own topic. In short, Wikipedia says all transitive verbs and most intransitive verbs use *avere*, while *essere* goes with verbs of motion such as *andare*, *venire* and *arrivare*, with *essere* itself and with reflexive verbs. [Essere vs avere](/blog/italian-essere-vs-avere/) works through the rules and the agreement they trigger.

**The imperfetto** is a simple tense. Wikipedia describes it as the stem plus the thematic vowel plus *v*, then endings: *-vo, -vi, -va, -vamo, -vate, -vano*.

| | parlare | credere | finire | essere |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| io | parlavo | credevo | finivo | ero |
| tu | parlavi | credevi | finivi | eri |
| lui / lei | parlava | credeva | finiva | era |
| noi | parlavamo | credevamo | finivamo | eravamo |
| voi | parlavate | credevate | finivate | eravate |
| loro | parlavano | credevano | finivano | erano |

Wikipedia says *essere* is the only irregular verb in the imperfect. Verbs with shortened infinitives use their longer stems: *dire* gives *dicevo*, *fare* gives *facevo*, *bere* gives *bevevo*.

## When one verb means two things

Aspect can change what a verb means, not only when. Wikipedia's example is *sapere*, "to know":

- *Sapevo la verità*: "I knew the truth", having the knowledge.
- *Ho saputo la verità*: "I found out the truth", the moment of getting it.

The imperfect describes the state; the passato prossimo marks the change into it. The same logic is why the passato prossimo handles "he got angry" on one occasion: it is a change of state at a point in time.

There is also a progressive version of the imperfect. Wikipedia's article on the imperfect notes that *parlavo* can also be said *stavo parlando*, using the imperfect of *stare* plus the gerund, and compares the difference to English "I eat" and "I am eating".

## Telling a short story, tense by tense

Here is a five-verb story, with the reason for each choice from the uses above.

> Ieri **pioveva** ed **ero** stanco. **Leggevo** quando **è arrivato** Marco, e **siamo usciti** insieme.

"Yesterday it was raining and I was tired. I was reading when Marco arrived, and we went out together."

1. *pioveva*: weather, so imperfetto.
2. *ero*: a state (being tired), so imperfetto.
3. *leggevo*: an ongoing action that gets interrupted, so imperfetto.
4. *è arrivato*: the single event that interrupts it, so passato prossimo, with *essere* because *arrivare* is a verb of motion.
5. *siamo usciti*: a single completed event, so passato prossimo; *uscire* is another verb of motion, so it takes *essere* and the participle *usciti* agrees with "we".

The tally is three imperfetti for the background and two passati prossimi for the events, 3 + 2 = 5 verbs. The imperfetti describe the background and the passati prossimi carry the events, matching the uses Wikipedia lists.

## Where the passato remoto fits

Italian has a third past, the **passato remoto** (*feci*, "I did"). Wikipedia's article on the preterite describes it as a past for an action completed far in the past, against the imperfetto's repeated or continuous past and the passato prossimo's recent completed past. It adds that colloquial use varies by region: northern Italians and Sardinians use the passato prossimo for any completed action, while southern Italians may use the passato remoto even for recent events. This comparison sticks to the passato prossimo and imperfetto, the pair that everyday spoken Italian in the north relies on.
