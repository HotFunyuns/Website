Reach matters less than the tale of the tape implies. A five-year study of 2,229 professional MMA bouts found that armspan separated winners from losers only at heavyweight; in every other division it made no detectable difference, and age turned out to be the more consistent factor. In boxing, an analysis of 182 world heavyweight title fights found that winners did have slightly longer reach — but that effect was smaller than the study's height effect and below every punch-output comparison it reported.

None of that means reach is irrelevant. It means reach is a tool whose value depends on how a fighter uses it, which is a more useful thing to know before a fight than a number in centimetres.

## What reach measures

Reach is the distance from fingertip to fingertip with both arms stretched out horizontally — the same measurement as armspan or wingspan. It is not arm length, which runs from one shoulder to one fist. A longer reach lets a boxer land jabs and straight punches while staying at a distance a shorter opponent struggles to answer, which is why it is treated as a tactical asset.

Two cautions apply to the numbers you see. First, many published figures come from listings rather than from measurements a study controls: the boxing study discussed below took its height and reach data from BoxRec and Wikipedia instead of measuring anyone, so its numbers are only as good as those listings. Second, reach travels with height — in that same study, median reach exceeded median height by 5 cm for winners and 7.5 cm for losers — so a reach gap and a height gap tend to arrive together.

## The MMA evidence: armspan barely registered

The study is Kirk's analysis in *Research Quarterly for Exercise and Sport* (2024), which looked at age, stature and armspan across 2,229 professional bouts over five years, testing the whole cohort, each year and each of 13 divisions separately. It used Bayes factors — a measure of how strongly the data favour a real difference over no difference — and counted a result only when the evidence favoured a difference by more than three to one.

What it found:

- **Age was the consistent difference.** Winners averaged 29.8 years and losers 30.7; on average, winners were about 0.82 years younger than their opponents. That held across the whole cohort, in four of the five years and in four of the 13 divisions.
- **Armspan helped only at heavyweight.** There, winners averaged 198.4 cm of armspan against 196.1 cm for losers — a gap of about 2.3 cm in averages, with a spread of roughly 7 cm around each.
- **Long arms for your height were, if anything, a slight drawback in one division.** In women's strawweight, a greater ratio of armspan to stature was associated with losing.
- **Nothing predicted how a fight was won.** None of the variables affected the method of victory.

The author's conclusion is blunt: the effect of body shape in MMA is greatly overstated and appears irrelevant in most divisions. The usual limits apply. This is an observational study of who won, not an experiment; it reports differences between groups, not what any one matchup will do; and the abstract does not tell us how the body measurements were collected.

## The boxing evidence: a small edge at heavyweight

The boxing data come from Finlay's study in *PLOS One* (2022), which covered 182 world heavyweight championship bouts between April 1991 and January 2022, using height, reach and fight data from BoxRec and Wikipedia.

| Comparison | Winners | Losers | Effect size reported |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Height (median) | 196 cm | 190.5 cm | d = 0.35 |
| Reach (median) | 201 cm | 198 cm | d = 0.23 |
| Punches thrown and landed | Higher | Lower | d = 0.27 to 0.73 |
| Accuracy | About 8 percentage points higher | — | — |

Winners were taller and had greater reach, and both differences were statistically significant. But look at the last column. The reach effect is smaller than the height effect, and below every punch-output comparison — total punches, jabs and power punches, thrown and landed. What set the winners of these title fights apart most clearly was that they threw and landed more.

The paper flags its own limits: there is no reliability data for its punch-output figures, so those results "should be interpreted with caution"; the analysis covers a single division; and punch statistics were missing for 41 bouts.

## Why heavyweight stands out

Both studies found a size effect at heavyweight — the only division in which the MMA study found an armspan effect, and the only division the boxing study examined — and the weight tables suggest why it might appear there. Heavyweight is a far wider band than the divisions below it: 40 lb, from 225 to 265, under the ABC's unified MMA rules, where most lighter divisions span 10 lb or less, while in boxing heavyweight has no upper limit at all — Nevada's schedule, for example, makes it everything over 195 lb. It is where very different bodies can legally meet. Neither study tested this explanation, but it fits both findings.

## Why a visible advantage shows up so faintly

Several explanations are consistent with the evidence, although none was tested directly:

1. **Weight classes do most of the equalising.** Two fighters at the same limit are already similar in mass, and [MMA weight classes](/blog/mma-weight-classes-explained/) cover how narrow most bands are.
2. **Fighters build styles around their frames.** Out-boxers are generally taller fighters with long reach, and pressure fighters are often shorter with shorter reach, though not always. A shorter fighter usually is not trying to win the long-range exchange in the first place.
3. **Skills move the distance.** A short-armed fighter relies on footwork, head movement and inside fighting to get into range, so skill can offset part of the gap.
4. **The measurements may be noisy.** Reach figures copied from listings rather than measured under controlled conditions can be imprecise, and measurement error blurs small effects towards zero.
5. **Elite samples are filtered.** Fighters who reach title level have usually found ways to compensate for what they lack, which shrinks the visible differences.

## When a reach gap deserves attention

Turned into a working rule, the evidence says: start from the assumption that reach is a minor factor, then upgrade it only when the circumstances give it something to do.

- **The longer fighter actually fights long.** Reach is worth something only to a fighter who keeps the distance and uses the jab. A long-armed fighter who prefers to brawl has thrown the advantage away.
- **It is a heavyweight fight.** The bands are widest there, and both studies found their size effect there.
- **The shorter fighter has no plan to close distance.** Pressure, timing and, in MMA, takedowns are the usual answers; without them, range belongs to the longer fighter.
- **Stance changes how it is used.** In an open-stance fight the jabs meet on the outside rather than finding a clean line, which changes what a long lead hand is for — see [southpaw vs orthodox](/blog/southpaw-vs-orthodox/) — though the longer arm still reaches further.

And in boxing, judges reward clean, effective punches that land, not the distance they were thrown from — [how judges score a round](/blog/boxing-scoring-explained/) — so a reach edge only pays if it turns into landed punches.

In [the 40-0 MMA and boxing draft](/apps/mma-boxing-fight-draft/), reach is one of several attributes — alongside power, defence, cardio, grappling and fight IQ — that feed a result, and a stylistic edge can outweigh a ratings edge. The research suggests a sensible way for any fight model to treat reach: as a modest input whose value depends on whether a fighter fights at range, not as a flat bonus for longer arms.
