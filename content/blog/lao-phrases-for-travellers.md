Most travel phrase lists are ranked by how impressive they sound. This one is ranked by how often you will reach for it, which produces a very different order — and puts "I did not understand that" near the top, above anything you might want to order in a restaurant.

The reason is structural. A phrase you say is half a conversation. The other half comes back at you at natural speed, and the phrases that let you handle *that* are the ones that keep you in the exchange.

## The repair phrases, first

- **ບໍ່ເຂົ້າໃຈ** — *baw khao jai* — I don't understand.
- **ເຂົ້າໃຈ** — *khao jai* — I understand. Useful on its own as a signal to continue.
- **ກະລຸນາເວົ້າຊ້າໆ** — *kaluna wao sa sa* — please speak more slowly.
- **ກະລຸນາເວົ້າໃໝ່** — please say that again.
- **ກະລຸນາຈົດລົງໄປ** — please write it down. Underrated. Numbers and place names survive being written far better than being heard.
- **ຂ້ອຍບໍ່ຮູ້** — *khoi baw hu* — I don't know.

Note ບໍ່ (*baw*) doing the work in three of these. It is the general negator, placed in front of whatever it negates, and the same word appears at the end of a sentence to turn it into a yes-no question. Two jobs, one small word, learned once.

## Greetings and the social minimum

**ສະບາຍດີ** (*sabaidee*) covers hello at any hour. **ຂອບໃຈ** is thank you. **ໂຊກດີ** (*sok dee*) is good luck and works as an informal goodbye; **ໄປດີ** (*pai di*) is "go well", said to someone departing.

Do not skip the politeness particles. ແດ່ on the end of a request softens it; ດອກ does the same job for a negative. These are not decoration — Lao encodes register through particles and pronoun choice rather than through vocabulary, and a request without one is blunter than you intend. [Lao greetings and politeness](/blog/lao-greetings-and-politeness/) sets out how the particle system works and where it differs from the Thai one people assume it copies.

## Meeting people

- **ເຈົ້າຊື່ຫຍັງ?** — *jao seu nyang* — what's your name?
- **ຂ້ອຍຊື່ ...** — *khoi seu ...* — my name is ...
- **ເຈົ້າມາແຕ່ໃສ?** — *jao ma tae sai* — where are you from?
- **ດີໃຈທີ່ຮູ້ກັບເຈົ້າ** — pleased to meet you.
- **ເຈົ້າເວົ້າພາສາລາວໄດ້ບໍ່?** — do you speak Lao? Worth knowing so you recognise it when it is asked of you.

Lao drops pronouns freely, so many of these are shorter than their English equivalents and are complete as they stand. Resist the urge to pad them.

## Money, markets and numbers

This is where a traveller's Lao actually gets used, and where vocabulary without numbers is useless. *How much* is a question you can ask on day one; understanding the answer requires the numbers, and the numbers require a little work.

Prices in Laos run into large figures, so the higher number words matter more here than they would in most languages. Practise them as prices rather than as a counting sequence, because sequence recall and random recall are different skills and only one of them helps at a stall.

## What you eat, and what you cannot

Enough to state what you do not eat, and enough to recognise the staples on a menu. **ເຊີນແຊບ** (*soen saeb*) is the Lao equivalent of *bon appétit*.

The genuinely useful category here is allergies and restrictions, which is dull to learn and is the vocabulary you will most regret not having. It is also the material most likely to be needed in a hurry, which is an argument for drilling it to the point of automaticity rather than merely recognising it.

## Getting somewhere, and writing it down

Place names, "where is", "here", "there", and the ability to confirm a price before getting in. Writing a destination down — hence the phrase above — is more reliable than pronouncing it, particularly with tonal place names where a small error changes the word.

A little script knowledge earns its keep here. Signage outside tourist areas is often Lao only, and decoding a place name is a much smaller job than reading fluently. [The Lao alphabet explained](/blog/lao-alphabet-explained/) covers what the writing system asks of you.

## The group nobody wants and everybody remembers

Pharmacy, hospital, doctor, help, pain. Nobody enjoys learning this group and everybody who has needed it remembers whether they had it.

## How the course groups these, and what we removed

The travel material in [Learn Lao Language Fast & Easy](/apps/learn-lao/) is organised by situation — restaurants, hotels, airports, markets, conversations with local speakers — rather than by grammatical topic, because retrieval under pressure is cued by context. You are standing in a market, and the market unit is what comes back.

Two decisions inside that are worth explaining.

**We cut the small-talk unit.** An early version had a group built around extended social conversation: weather, hobbies, opinions, the sort of material that fills the middle of a general course. It came out because of a mismatch we could not argue away. Those phrases are only useful if you can follow the reply, and a traveller three weeks into Lao cannot. Teaching a learner to ask a question they cannot understand the answer to produces a specific and unpleasant failure: an exchange that starts well, collapses, and leaves both people embarrassed. The repair phrases at the top of this article went in as part of the same decision — they are what the small-talk unit's slot became.

**Numbers sit next to the travel unit rather than in a grammar section.** In a general course, numbers are a vocabulary set that lives near counting and time. For a traveller they are the thing that makes the market unit work at all, so they were moved to sit beside it and the drills use prices and quantities rather than counting sequences.

The honest limitation of a situation-first structure is that it produces gaps a systematic course would not have. Our Lao course is stronger on the language of arriving, buying and getting somewhere than on the language of describing your day, and a learner who wants Lao for a relationship rather than a trip will feel that. It was the right trade for who actually opens this course, and it is a trade rather than a free win.
