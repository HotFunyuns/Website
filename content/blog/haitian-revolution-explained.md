The Haitian Revolution matters because it is the only large-scale revolt of enslaved people in modern history that ended in an independent state built on the abolition of slavery. It began in the French colony of Saint-Domingue in August 1791 and ended with a declaration of independence on 1 January 1804. Twenty-one years later, France demanded payment from the country it had lost, and Haiti spent most of the nineteenth century paying.

That last sentence is the part most commonly missing from summaries, and it is not a footnote. It is the mechanism by which a revolution that won became a state that started its life in debt.

## What happened, and in what order

Sequence first. Saint-Domingue was, by the late eighteenth century, the most profitable colony in the world. The historian Ada Ferrer, writing in the *American Historical Review*, records that it produced roughly half of the sugar and coffee consumed in Europe and the Americas. That output rested entirely on the labour of enslaved Africans and their descendants, worked under a plantation regime whose mortality rates required constant replacement by further trafficking.

In August 1791 the colony's enslaved population rose. Ferrer describes it as the largest and best-coordinated rebellion of enslaved people the world had yet seen; more than a thousand plantations were destroyed within weeks. The scale is the point. This was not a local disturbance that grew: it was organised in advance across estates.

Abolition came after the revolt, not before it. Colonial officials began issuing emancipation decrees in August 1793, and in February 1794 the French National Convention abolished slavery throughout the French colonies. Emancipation was, in other words, conceded to an insurrection that had already won militarily in much of the colony.

France then tried to reverse it. A timeline maintained by the Haitian Revolution project founded at Brown University marks 1801–1802 as the period of General Charles Leclerc's expedition to Saint-Domingue — the French attempt, under Napoleon Bonaparte, to restore colonial authority. It failed, and on 1 January 1804 independence was declared. Ferrer notes what that made Haiti: the second independent nation in the Western Hemisphere, and the only one founded by formerly enslaved people and without slavery.

Popular retellings tend to compress all of this into a duel between Toussaint Louverture and Napoleon Bonaparte. That framing is not wrong about either man's importance, but it is a poor description of a process that ran for more than twelve years and involved colonial officials, a metropolitan legislature that abolished slavery and then had its decision contested, and an expeditionary campaign that failed. Claims about what any individual leader personally decided need their own evidence, and that evidence is thinner and more contested than the chronology of events.

## The 1825 indemnity, and what it actually cost

For two decades France refused recognition. Then, on 17 April 1825, Charles X issued an ordinance setting a price: 150 million francs, payable in five annual instalments, in return for France recognising Haitian independence. The Bibliothèque nationale de France puts that sum at roughly fifteen per cent of annual French state revenue at the time — and at least ten times Haiti's.

Haiti could not pay it out of revenue, so it borrowed. In November 1825 it raised a 30 million franc loan on the French market at six per cent; after bank commissions, only 24 million actually reached the country. This is the origin of what is often called the "double debt": an indemnity, plus the borrowing required to service the indemnity.

The arithmetic did not work, and France eventually conceded as much. In 1838 it recognised Haitian independence unconditionally, reduced the indemnity to 90 million francs, and restructured it into thirty annual instalments — while simultaneously cutting the compensation paid to former colonists by forty per cent. The independence debt itself was cleared in 1878, financed by a further French loan raised in 1875. The external loans were not fully repaid until 1887–88 and 1921–22. That final date sits inside the United States occupation of Haiti, which ran from 1915 to 1934.

None of this requires editorialising. The dates and sums are in the record, and they describe a state paying, for most of a century, for the act of ceasing to be a slave colony.

## Why the evidence was thin for so long

For two hundred years, historians arguing about what the Haitian Declaration of Independence said were working largely from reprints. No original printing was known to survive in Haiti. In 2010 Julia Gaffield, then a doctoral student at Duke University, identified an eight-page printed pamphlet dated 1 January 1804 among British archival holdings; a second printed version was subsequently located. Duke's account of the find notes what it established beyond the text itself — that the new government had a working printing operation and was communicating with other powers within weeks of independence.

This is a useful case for anyone learning how historical claims are actually built, and it pairs directly with the distinction set out in our guide to [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/). The pamphlet is a primary document. The two centuries of reprints were not, and the difference turned out to matter.

It is also a reminder that absence of evidence in one archive is not absence of evidence. The document was in a British collection the whole time, catalogued but unremarked.

## Where the disagreements are

Historians do not divide neatly here, but several genuine arguments persist. One concerns causation: how much weight to give the French Revolution's own upheavals versus the organisation and objectives of the enslaved insurgents themselves. Ferrer's framing — that the rising *forced* slavery onto the agenda in Paris — sits against older accounts in which metropolitan ideas radiated outward to the colony. A second concerns the revolution's effect elsewhere: whether it accelerated abolition in the Atlantic world or hardened slaveholding regimes against it. Both happened, in different places, and the balance is still argued.

A third is Haitian historiography's own long-standing objection: that the revolution was for a century treated in European and North American scholarship as marginal, or as an atrocity narrative rather than a political event. UNESCO's Routes of Enslaved Peoples programme, launched in 1994, was explicitly founded to address that silence around slavery and the slave trade in public history. Reading Haitian scholarship alongside French and Anglophone accounts is not a courtesy; it changes which questions look important.
