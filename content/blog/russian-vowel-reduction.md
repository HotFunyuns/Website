You learn that **о** is "o". Then you hear *хорошо* and it comes out roughly "khuh-rah-SHAW", with only the last vowel sounding like the letter you were taught. Nothing has gone wrong. Russian gives its stressed syllable a full vowel and squeezes every other vowel toward a neutral sound, and the spelling does not record it.

This is called **vowel reduction**, and it is the single largest reason Russian words do not sound the way they look.

## The one rule that explains the most

**Unstressed а and о merge.** The phenomenon has a name, *akanye*, and it means that in an unstressed syllable the two letters no longer represent different sounds.

How far they merge depends on where the syllable is relative to the stress:

- **Immediately before the stress** (the first pretonic syllable), both are realised as a sound written [ɐ] — an open, slightly centralised "ah". *Паром*, ferry, is [pɐˈrom].
- **Everywhere else unstressed**, both reduce further to a **schwa** [ə], the neutral vowel of English *about*. *Облако*, cloud, comes out [ˈobləkə] — one clear о under the stress, two schwas after it.

So Russian is not being inconsistent. It has two degrees of reduction, and the position of the syllable relative to the stress decides which one applies.

Once you have this, a large amount of previously baffling listening resolves. *Молоко*, milk, is [məɫɐˈko]: schwa, then [ɐ], then the full stressed о. Three identical letters, three different sounds, and a completely regular reason for it.

## The second merger

Front vowels have their own version. The main feature is **ikanye**: unstressed **е** merges with **и**, realised as [ɪ].

*Семена*, seeds, is [sʲɪmʲɪˈna]. Both unstressed е sounds have become [ɪ].

This one matters disproportionately for grammar, because Russian puts a great deal of its grammatical information in unstressed endings. When several endings reduce toward the same neutral sound, hearing the difference between them becomes difficult in a way that reading them is not — which is why learners can conjugate flawlessly on paper and still fail to catch a case ending in speech.

## What does not reduce

The high vowels **у** and **и** are usually described as undergoing no reduction in quality. They are phonetically centralised in unstressed position, and in rapid speech they can drift toward a schwa, but they do not participate in the mergers above.

That makes them useful. If you can hear a clear у or и in a word, you have an anchor that has not been modified, and you can often work out the stress by elimination from the vowels that have been.

## The part nobody can fix for you: stress is unmarked

Here is the awkward fact underneath everything above. **Reduction depends entirely on where the stress is, and Russian does not tell you where the stress is.**

Ordinary printed Russian — books, newspapers, signs, messages — carries no accent marks. Dictionaries and learner materials print them as a teaching aid, and they disappear the moment you read anything real. Stress is not predictable from spelling, it can move between forms of the same word, and in a handful of pairs it is the only thing distinguishing two different words.

The consequence is that **stress is part of the word, and must be learned with it.** Writing down a new Russian noun without its stress is like writing down a German noun without its gender: you have recorded most of the word and omitted the part that decides how it behaves.

The practical habit that follows: whenever you record a new word, record the stress, and say it aloud with the reduction applied rather than spelling it out letter by letter. A word learned as a spelling has to be converted to a sound every time you use it. A word learned as a sound is just available.

## Why this makes listening harder than reading

Most learners find Russian reading progresses faster than Russian listening, and reduction is a large part of why.

Written Russian is fully explicit: every letter is there. Spoken Russian compresses everything outside the stressed syllable, merges vowels that are distinct on the page, and leaves the listener to reconstruct the word from a stressed nucleus plus context. That reconstruction is a vocabulary problem as much as an ear problem — you can only recover a word you already know.

This is also why over-enunciated learner audio can be actively counterproductive. Audio that pronounces every vowel as written teaches a version of the word that no Russian speaker produces, and the learner then has to unlearn it on first contact with a real speaker. The [Cyrillic beginner's guide](/blog/learn-russian-cyrillic-beginners-guide/) covers the letter-to-sound mapping; this article is the systematic exception to it, and both have to be held at once.

## The order worth learning it in

1. **Learn that unstressed о and а are the same sound.** This alone resolves most confusion.
2. **Learn the two degrees**: [ɐ] immediately before the stress, schwa everywhere else.
3. **Learn that unstressed е goes toward и.** Then stop adding rules.
4. **Record stress with every new word, permanently.**
5. **Practise on words you already know**, saying them with reduction rather than spelling them out. Reduction on familiar vocabulary is a pronunciation exercise; reduction on unfamiliar vocabulary is a guessing game.
