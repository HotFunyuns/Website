Basketball Career Sim is the Reign Creative game that tells one player's story instead of a franchise's, and that single-athlete focus is what makes it worth a conversation with sports publishers, basketball communities and youth programmes. This overview sets out what the app does today, which kinds of partnership fit it, and how a sale or licence would actually proceed. Nothing here is an offer or a valuation; it is an invitation to talk.

## A created player, from youth ball to a legacy score

According to its Google Play listing, the game starts with a player you build: position, height, weight, wingspan, jersey number, playing style, archetype and attributes. The career then runs through youth basketball, high school, recruiting and college, the draft and the professional seasons, and the app's page notes that early decisions still show up seasons later.

Matches are played through **Live Sim**. Rather than steering players around the court, you choose what happens on key possessions: shoot or move the ball, defend your man or call for help, play safe or gamble. Those calls feed coach trust, team chemistry, reputation and the reactions of teammates, rivals, media and fans. Off the court sit training camp, attribute upgrades, signature moves, contracts, brand deals and lifestyle purchases, all paid for with fictional in-game money.

Three practical facts matter to any partner. The listing says the game supports offline play and needs no account. It is free to download, supported by ads, and an optional premium purchase removes ads and unlocks themes. And every league, team and player is fictional. The full consumer walkthrough is in the [Basketball Career Sim guide](/blog/basketball-career-sim-guide/), and the app page is at [/apps/pro-basketball-my-career-sim/](/apps/pro-basketball-my-career-sim/).

## Why publishers, communities and coaches are the natural fit

The app's page on this site describes its audience as fans who care about "the story behind the stat line", players who prefer a career sim to arcade controls, and people who need a game that works offline. That points to three partner types.

- **Sports publishers** looking for a decision-led basketball title they can market, localise or bundle, without taking on an action game's control scheme.
- **Basketball communities and creators**, whose audiences already argue about recruiting choices and player archetypes, topics the site covers in pieces such as [how college recruiting works in career mode](/blog/college-recruiting-in-basketball-career-mode/) and [basketball player archetypes](/blog/basketball-player-archetypes/).
- **Coaching and youth programmes** that want a low-friction game to discuss decision-making with young players. We make no claim that the game improves anyone's basketball; it is entertainment that happens to put choices in a sequence.

## Licensing ideas grounded in what the game already does

A useful test for any proposal is whether it builds on features that exist. Some candidates:

1. **A branded edition.** Because every team is fictional, a partner's name, colours or community identity could replace placeholder branding where that is technically feasible. Using a real league, school or athlete would need that rights holder's own licence; the game has none today.
2. **Co-developed career content.** New recruiting storylines, archetypes or signature moves written with a coaching partner who knows the youth pathway.
3. **Distribution in a new region,** with a publisher handling store presence and translation while development stays with us.
4. **Cross-promotion** with a complementary app whose audience overlaps the app page's "decisions with long-term consequences" group.

As a worked planning example, a programme that wanted to walk players through the five career stages the listing names (youth, high school, college, draft, pro) over a ten-week season could give each stage two weeks: 10 ÷ 5 = 2. That is the sort of structure a first call can sketch, not a promise of outcomes.

## What a first call with Reign Creative covers

Start with the business inquiry form on the [partnerships page](/partnerships/) or an email to ReignCreativeSupport@gmail.com. Say who you are, which app you mean and what you have in mind in a few sentences. Please leave confidential, financial or personal information out of that first message. If there is a fit, a call explores goals on both sides and whether the app can support them; non-public detail is shared only with qualified parties and, where appropriate, under an NDA. Every discussion is nonbinding until both sides sign terms after due diligence.

## How Basketball Career Sim would change hands on Google Play

If the conversation becomes an acquisition, the move between developer accounts runs through Google's own process. Google's [transfer help page](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/6230247) says both accounts must be registered and active, and the original account and the app must comply with policy. Users, download statistics, ratings and reviews, content ratings and the store listing move with the app; bulk export, payout and earnings reports do not.

Two details are specific to an app like this one. Because it carries in-app products, the target account needs an active payments profile, and Google notes an in-app-purchase app is unpublished after transfer until the new owner verifies prices and publishes again if the default currency differs. Because it shows ads, Google says ad SDK integrations, including AdMob, must be updated in the app so traffic is credited to the new account. After submission, the target developer reviews and approves the request, and Google's support team replies within two business days. What any sale actually includes beyond the Play listing would be set out in the written agreement.
