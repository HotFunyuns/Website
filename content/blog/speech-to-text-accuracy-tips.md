Speech-to-text software can get the bulk of a recording right and still miss the words that matter. Several of the causes Wikipedia lists are things you can control before the recording ever reaches the software. This guide explains what makes recognition fail, how accuracy is measured and the recording habits that make the biggest difference.

## What makes recognition hard

Wikipedia's article on speech recognition sets out the problem. Voices vary in **accent, pronunciation, articulation, roughness, dialect, nasality, pitch, volume and speed**, and speech is **distorted by background noise, echoes and recording characteristics**. It also lists conditions that change accuracy:

| Factor | Easier | Harder |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vocabulary | Small, distinct words | Large or easily confused vocabulary |
| Speaker | A system built for one speaker | A system meant for any speaker |
| Style | Read speech | Spontaneous speech, with "um"s and false starts |
| Speech | Isolated words | Continuous, naturally spoken sentences |
| Conditions | Quiet room | Environmental noise, echoes, room acoustics |

You cannot change who is speaking, but you can change almost everything in the last row.

## How accuracy is measured

Accuracy is usually expressed as **word error rate** (WER). Wikipedia explains that it compares the recognised text with what was really said, counting three kinds of mistake:

- **substitutions**, where one word is replaced by another;
- **deletions**, where a spoken word is missing;
- **insertions**, where an extra word appears.

WER is the total of those three, divided by the number of words actually spoken. A WER of 0 is a perfect match; Wikipedia notes it can even exceed 1 if a system inserts many extra words.

**A worked example.** Someone says "Send the report to Ana by Friday", seven words. The software writes "Send a report to Anna Friday". That is two substitutions ("a" for "the", "Anna" for "Ana") and one deletion ("by"): three errors out of seven words, a WER of about **43%** for that sentence. Notice which errors matter: "a" for "the" is harmless, but the name and the missing "by" could cause real confusion. A low average WER does not protect the words you care about most.

## Recording habits that matter most

1. **Get the microphone close.** Google's help for its Live Transcribe app gives the same advice: hold the device microphone **near the person or sound** you want to capture. Being closer makes the voice louder relative to room noise and echo.
2. **Choose a quiet room.** Turn off fans, televisions and music; close windows.
3. **Avoid echo.** Soft furnishings absorb sound; bare rooms with hard surfaces reflect it.
4. **One voice at a time.** Two people talking over each other are hard to untangle when you check the transcript against the audio.
5. **Speak at a steady pace.** Wikipedia lists speed among the ways voices vary; clear, unhurried speech gives the software less to guess.
6. **Spell unusual names and terms** aloud once, or note them as you go.

## Where to put the phone

The same principle, closer is better, looks different in each setting:

| Setting | Phone placement |
| --- | --- |
| Voice memo | Held in front of you, speaking towards the microphone, away from wind |
| One-to-one interview | On the table between you, nearer the person whose words matter most |
| Meeting | In the middle of the table, away from laptops, cups and keyboards |
| Lecture | As near the speaker as you are allowed, never at the back of the room |
| Phone or video call | Recorded at the source where possible, rather than from a speaker across the room |

Wherever it goes, put the phone on something soft, such as a folded cloth, so table knocks and vibrations do not reach the microphone.

## Before you start a long recording

Make a ten-second test recording and transcribe it. If the test transcript is poor, move the phone closer or change room before you record an hour of audio. It is much easier to fix the setup than the transcript.

## After transcription: check where it counts

Even a good transcript needs a targeted check:

- **names** of people, places and products;
- **numbers**, dates, prices and quantities;
- **technical terms** and acronyms;
- anything you will **quote**.
