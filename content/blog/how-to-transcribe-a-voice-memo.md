A voice memo catches an idea when typing is inconvenient: on a walk, in the car, the moment a thought arrives. The catch is that audio is hard to search and slow to review. Turning a memo into text gives you both: the speed of speaking and the usefulness of writing. This guide covers recording memos that transcribe well, converting them and editing the result.

## Why transcribe a voice memo

A memo is a capture tool; a transcript is a working tool. Once a memo is text you can **search** it, **copy** parts of it into a note or email, **skim** it in seconds and **restructure** it into something finished. The listing for our transcription app describes exactly this use: voice notes becoming a written starting point for a journal entry, a plan or a first draft.

## How much text a memo makes

Wikipedia's article on words per minute notes that audiobooks are recommended at **150 to 160 words per minute**, the range in which people comfortably hear and say words, while slide presentations tend to run at about 100 to 125.

**A worked example.** A five-minute memo spoken at about 150 words a minute produces about **750 words**, the length of a short article. A 90-second idea is about 225 words, a solid paragraph or two. Knowing this helps you plan: if you need a 1,500-word first draft, about ten minutes of focused talking gets you the raw material.

## Recording memos that transcribe well

Speech recognition is sensitive to how a recording is made. Wikipedia notes that recognition is affected by **volume, speed, background noise, echoes and recording characteristics**, as well as by accent and pronunciation. Google's help for its Live Transcribe app gives the core advice for any phone recording: hold the device microphone **near the person or sound** you want to capture.

For memos, that means:

1. **Speak towards the microphone**, at a steady distance.
2. **Pick a quiet spot**: step away from traffic, wind and other conversations.
3. **Speak in sentences**, at an even pace, rather than fragments.
4. **Say names and numbers clearly**, and spell unusual ones.
5. **Pause between ideas**; a short gap makes the transcript easier to split into paragraphs.

## Transcribing the memo

In [Transcribe Audio to Text PRO](/apps/transcribe-audio-to-text/), the steps are the same for any recording: start with the recorded speech, transcribe it, check the details, then use the text. If you only need the gist, a quick read is enough; if the memo contains anything you will act on, such as dates, amounts or names, check those against the audio.

## Editing in two passes

**Pass one: correct.** Read the transcript with the memo playing, fixing misheard words, names and numbers. Do not restructure yet.

**Pass two: shape.** Now turn talk into text:

- **delete repetition**, the "so, as I said" of thinking aloud;
- **group related points** under short headings;
- **turn instructions to yourself into tasks**, such as "call the venue by Friday";
- **keep good phrasing**: a spoken draft can contain lively sentences worth keeping.

How much to tidy depends on the purpose; [verbatim versus clean verbatim](/blog/verbatim-vs-clean-transcription/) explains the options.

## A memo habit that lasts

Memos can pile up, and an untranscribed memo is easy to forget. A simple routine keeps them useful:

1. **Name each memo** as soon as you stop recording, with a word or two about the topic.
2. **Transcribe the same day**, while you still remember what you meant.
3. **Move the text** to where it will be used: a notes app, a task list or a draft document.
4. **Review weekly**: skim the week's transcripts for ideas you have not acted on.
5. **Archive the audio** once the text is final, rather than deleting it straight away.

## Memos that include other people

If a memo records someone else, such as a conversation or a meeting, ask before you record: courtesy expects it, and the rules on recording other people vary from place to place. [Transcribing an interview](/blog/how-to-transcribe-an-interview/) covers consent before recording anyone.

## Memos for journalling and planning

Voice memos suit reflective writing too: talking for three minutes about your day may feel easier than writing it. Keep each memo until its text version is finished. Tone, emphasis and a half-finished thought that did not transcribe well all live in the audio.
