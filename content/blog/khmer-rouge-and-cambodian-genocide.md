Democratic Kampuchea lasted three years, eight months and twenty days. The Khmer Rouge seized Phnom Penh on 17 April 1975; the Vietnamese army entered the city on 7 January 1979. Cambodians use that exact duration as the standard description of the period, and the tribunal that later tried the regime's leaders uses it too.

Precision matters here because popular accounts routinely mis-date and mis-scope the period — extending it backwards into the civil war, forwards into the decade of continued Khmer Rouge insurgency, or treating a single prison as the whole of it. What follows sets out the sequence, what a court established under a legal standard of proof, and where the numbers genuinely remain uncertain.

## Before April 1975

The regime did not appear from nothing. Patrick Heuveline and Bunnak Poch, writing in *Demography*, record that Norodom Sihanouk — who had governed Cambodia since negotiating independence from France in 1953 — was overthrown in a military coup in 1970, and that the country then entered a civil war during which eastern Cambodia, near the Vietnamese border, was subjected to intensive bombing by United States B-52s. Many Cambodians took refuge in Phnom Penh, the city the Khmer Rouge captured on 17 April 1975.

That bombing and that war belong in the account because they are part of how the movement grew and how the country was wrecked before 1975. They are not an exculpation. Establishing a precondition is not the same as excusing what a government then chose to do, and conflating the two is a failure of reasoning rather than a moral position.

## What the regime did

The mechanisms are well established. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum summarises them as forced labour, persecution and execution carried out in the name of an extreme agrarian ideology. The population of the cities was driven into the countryside. People were worked, starved and killed.

The tribunal's findings make the categories precise. In Case 002/02 the crimes charged and found included murder, extermination, deportation, enslavement, imprisonment, torture, persecution on political, religious and racial grounds, enforced disappearances, forced transfer, and forced marriage and rape — alongside grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions covering wilful killing, torture, inhuman treatment and unlawful confinement. Those are legal findings after trial, not descriptions borrowed from a survey of the literature.

## What the tribunal established, and against whom

The Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia was a hybrid court: Cambodian, with international support. Its own summary of its work records ten individuals named and investigated, over a hundred crime sites examined, 219 witnesses and experts heard, and 3,945 civil parties admitted to the proceedings. It describes itself as the first internationally supported domestic tribunal to convict a former head of state of the crime of genocide.

The specific outcomes for the two most senior surviving defendants are these. On 7 August 2014, in Case 002/01, Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan were each sentenced to life imprisonment for crimes against humanity — in Khieu Samphan's case including extermination, political persecution, forced transfer, enforced disappearances and attacks on human dignity. The Supreme Court Chamber affirmed those convictions on 23 November 2016.

On 16 November 2018, in Case 002/02, both were convicted of the much wider set of crimes listed above, with sentences merged into the existing life terms. The genocide findings were narrow and specific: Khieu Samphan was convicted of genocide by killing members of the Vietnamese group; Nuon Chea of genocide by killing members of the Vietnamese and Cham groups. The Supreme Court Chamber affirmed Khieu Samphan's Case 002/02 conviction on 22 September 2022. Nuon Chea died in Phnom Penh on 4 August 2019 during the appeal, and the court accordingly records his Case 002/02 convictions as not final.

That distinction is worth keeping. "Genocide" as a legal finding attached to identified acts against identified protected groups is not the same as "genocide" used as a general description of the period, and a careful account says which sense it is using.

## The death toll is a range, and the range has structure

No single figure is defensible without naming its source and method.

Heuveline and Poch note that published estimates of excess deaths during the Khmer Rouge period range from 500,000 to three million, and adopt 1.5 to 2.0 million — attributing those deaths to political violence, malnutrition, exhaustion from overwork, lack of appropriate medicine and increased exposure to malaria, and describing the total as nearly a quarter of Cambodia's 1975 population. Their method draws on retrospective survey questions about parental survival, comparison with national demographic estimates, and analysis of sex-ratio imbalances in the surviving population.

The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum gives almost two million, describing that as approximately a third of the country's population. The Documentation Center of Cambodia states that two million were killed and five million survived. The tribunal's own summary says over a million people were killed or perished — a lower figure, consistent with a court restricting itself to what it could establish.

These differ both in the count and in the denominator, which is why the same number can be a quarter of the population in one account and a third in another. Anyone quoting a figure should quote the source with it. The general problem — how a historical quantity is produced, and what choices are buried in it — is the subject of our guide to [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/), and applies to counts as much as to chronology.

## Documentation, and why it was built

The Documentation Center of Cambodia was founded in 1995, initially as an adjunct of Yale University, and now operates from Phnom Penh with twenty-one affiliated offices around the country. Its stated purpose is collecting data on the crimes of genocide and other atrocity crimes committed by the regime, and preserving that record for justice and for memory. It received the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 2018.

Much of the evidentiary base for the trials, and for the scholarship, exists because an organisation set out to build it. That is a point about method: in cases where a state destroys or abandons its records, the archive is a later construction, and its choices shape what can subsequently be proved. Our explainer on [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) covers why the provenance of a collection matters as much as its contents.

Survivors and perpetrators are both still alive, and the politics of this period inside Cambodia remain contested. Writing about it as closed history would be wrong, and so would treating the tribunal's verdicts as the end of the argument rather than as its legal baseline.

## Where it sits in a wider chronology

Understanding this period means seeing it against the Vietnam War, the wider Cold War in Southeast Asia, and the decade of continued conflict afterwards — Heuveline and Poch note that Vietnamese troops remained in Cambodia for ten years while Khmer Rouge forces fought on from near the Thai border.
