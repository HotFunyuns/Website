If you want to learn world history without paying for a course, the honest starting point is that the free options are not competing products. They are four different categories — a nonprofit curriculum platform, a teacher-facing course package, an open-university short-course library, and a museum collection platform — plus reference apps, which do a fifth thing entirely. Choosing well means deciding which job you need done first.

**Disclosure before anything else:** World History Timeline Sim is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and it appears in this comparison. Everything below is drawn from what each provider documents officially on its own site, checked on the research date shown at the top of this article. There are no ratings, no popularity figures and no overall winner, because none of those would be honest here.

## How this comparison was made

Only officially documented attributes are compared: what the provider says the resource is, what it costs, who it is aimed at, whether the content comes with teaching materials and documented provenance, and whether it works without a connection. Where a provider does not document something, this article says so rather than guessing.

Providers were excluded where their official documentation could not be opened and verified on the research date. That is why one widely used broadcaster-run revision service does not appear: we could not verify its current documentation directly, and listing a competitor on memory is not acceptable in a comparison.

## What each one is actually for

**Khan Academy** is a nonprofit education platform. Its [about page](https://www.khanacademy.org/about) states the mission as providing "a free, world-class education to anyone, anywhere", describes it as "a 501(c)(3) nonprofit organization" reliant on donations, and says it offers free courses across core academic subjects including social studies, available in many countries and languages. The model is a structured sequence with practice built in, aimed principally at school-age learners. Strongest for someone who wants a guided path and immediate exercises.

**The OER Project** publishes classroom-ready history curricula. Its [World History page](https://www.oerproject.com/World-History) describes "Free, Standards Based Online History Curriculum" and lists several course configurations, including an AP option and versions beginning at different points in the past, along with teaching guides, professional development and a help centre. It is built for teachers first, which means the sequencing and the supporting materials are unusually thorough. Strongest for someone who wants a real course arc rather than a set of topics.

**OpenLearn**, from The Open University, is a library of free short courses and articles. Its front page states that "everything on the multi-award winning OpenLearn is free to everyone", offering "hundreds of courses, articles, videos and interactives" across nine subject areas including History & The Arts. The units are self-contained and vary in length. Strongest for someone who wants university-produced material on a specific topic rather than a full sweep.

**Google Arts & Culture** is a collections platform rather than a course. Its material comes from partner museums and cultural institutions, and it presents artworks, objects, institutions and interactive exhibits. There is no curriculum and no assessment. Strongest for looking closely at objects and places, which is a genuine and under-used way into a period.

**World History Timeline Sim**, ours, is a browsing and revision app: a large corpus of events, people, civilisations, wars and landmarks, filterable by era, region and country, plus decision-based modes and quiz and flashcard material. Everything is bundled locally, so it works without a connection. It is not a course, it sets no syllabus, and it has no assessment tied to a curriculum.

## Where the other providers are stronger than ours

This is the section that matters most, so it is stated plainly.

**Provenance.** The curriculum providers exist as educational institutions or as teaching projects with materials, review processes and professional standards behind them, and Google Arts & Culture's content comes from named partner museums. Our app's historical content was written in-house, carries no citations for any individual claim, and has had no external scholarly review. UNESCO's framing of [open educational resources](https://www.unesco.org/en/open-educational-resources) treats inclusive and equitable *quality* as one of the five action areas member states are asked to support — quality assurance is part of what openness is supposed to mean, and an unsourced app does not meet that bar.

**Sequence and assessment.** A course decides what you study in what order and checks whether you understood it. Our app deliberately does not: it gives filters and a corpus. That is useful for exploration and useless as a syllabus.

**Depth of argument.** University-produced units and curriculum materials make arguments and show their working. Short app entries summarise.

If your priority is any of those three, one of the other providers is the better fit, and you should use it.

## Where a bundled app earns its place

Two things genuinely favour the app category.

**Offline use.** Everything in our app is packaged with the download, so it works on a commute, on a plane or on a bad connection. None of the four web-delivered providers documents offline access on the pages checked, and web courses generally assume a connection. This is a structural difference between delivery models rather than a feature gap.

**Breadth for orientation.** When you do not yet know what you want to study, a filterable corpus answers questions a linear course cannot: what else was happening in this century, what connects to this person, where does this sit relative to that. The [app](/apps/world-history-timeline-sim/) is built for that kind of movement, and [our guide to reading a world history timeline](/blog/world-history-timeline-guide/) covers how to get value out of it without mistaking it for a syllabus.

The honest role for an unsourced app is orientation and revision. It is not a substitute for teachers, scholarly books, archives or primary sources, and nothing in it should be cited.

## Picking by goal

- **You want a structured path from scratch.** Start with one of the curriculum providers. Finish a course before adding anything else.
- **You are teaching or supporting a class.** The teacher-facing curriculum package is the obvious first stop, because the supporting materials are the point.
- **You want one topic in depth.** A university short-course library gives you a self-contained unit without committing to a full sweep.
- **You learn from objects and places.** A collections platform will do more for you than any amount of summary text.
- **You want daily revision and chronological intuition between study sessions.** A browsable app fits here, and only here.

The most common good answer is a pair: one course for structure and one app for the days you have fifteen minutes.
