Italian numbers are built by joining words end to end, so once you know the basic pieces you can say any figure, including a year or a price. Wiktionary's appendix of Italian numbers, the main source here, notes that a cardinal number of any length may be written as one continuous word: 342 is *trecentoquarantadue*. The catch is a set of small sound changes where the pieces meet. This reference covers counting, the joins, large numbers, ordinals and how figures are written.

## Zero to nineteen

| Number | Italian | Number | Italian |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | zero | 10 | dieci |
| 1 | uno | 11 | undici |
| 2 | due | 12 | dodici |
| 3 | tre | 13 | tredici |
| 4 | quattro | 14 | quattordici |
| 5 | cinque | 15 | quindici |
| 6 | sei | 16 | sedici |
| 7 | sette | 17 | diciassette |
| 8 | otto | 18 | diciotto |
| 9 | nove | 19 | diciannove |

Notice where the teens switch. Eleven to sixteen end in *-dici*; seventeen to nineteen start with *dici-* instead: *diciassette*, *diciotto*, *diciannove*. The unit comes last from seventeen onwards, as it does in every decade after.

**Uno** also behaves like the indefinite article when it comes before a noun. Wiktionary gives *un espresso*, *uno scotch* and *una birra*, which follow the same rules covered in [Italian articles and gender](/blog/italian-articles-and-gender/).

## Twenty to ninety-nine, and the dropped vowel

The tens are *venti* (20), *trenta* (30), *quaranta* (40), *cinquanta* (50), *sessanta* (60), *settanta* (70), *ottanta* (80) and *novanta* (90). Add a unit straight on: *ventidue* (22), *trentacinque* (35), *novantanove* (99).

Two rules apply at the join.

- **Before uno and otto, the ten drops its final vowel.** 21 is *ventuno*, not *ventiuno*; 28 is *ventotto*; 41 is *quarantuno*; 88 is *ottantotto*. Wiktionary's tables show the same pattern in every decade.
- **A final tre takes an accent.** Wiktionary notes that numbers ending in 3 from twenty upwards are stressed on the last syllable and written *-tré*: *ventitré*, *trentatré*, *sessantatré*. Plain *tre* on its own has no accent.

## Cento, mille, mila and milione

| Number | Italian |
| --- | --- |
| 100 | cento |
| 200 | duecento |
| 1,000 | mille |
| 2,000 | duemila |
| 10,000 | diecimila |
| 100,000 | centomila |
| 1,000,000 | un milione |
| 2,000,000 | due milioni |
| 1,000,000,000 | un miliardo |

Three details matter here. **Mille becomes mila in the plural**, so 1,000 is *mille* but 3,000 is *tremila*. For **cento**, Wiktionary lists both *centouno* and *centuno* for 101, and both *centootto* and *centotto* for 108; 180 is *centottanta*. And **milione** and **miliardo** are not adjectives. They are nouns with plurals (*milioni*, *miliardi*) and take *di* before a noun: Wiktionary's example is *miliardi di lire*.

Wiktionary adds that numbers above a thousand are sometimes written as separate parts, *due mila* for *duemila*, especially with large round numbers.

## Building three numbers step by step

1. **2026**: two thousand is *duemila*, then twenty-six is *ventisei*. Joined: *duemilaventisei*.
2. **1984**: Wiktionary's own example. One thousand (*mille*) plus nine hundred (*novecento*) plus eighty-four (*ottantaquattro*), with the o of *cento* dropped before *ottanta*: *millenovecentottantaquattro*.
3. **860,789**: Wiktionary notes that numbers above a hundred thousand are often broken into groups of three. Say 860 thousand, *ottocentosessantamila*, then 789, *settecentoottantanove*.

As a check on the third, 860 × 1,000 + 789 = 860,789, and 1,000 + 900 + 84 = 1,984.

## Ordinals: primo to decimo, then -esimo

The first ten ordinals have their own forms: *primo, secondo, terzo, quarto, quinto, sesto, settimo, ottavo, nono, decimo*. From eleven on, Wiktionary says, you drop the last vowel of the cardinal and add **-esimo**: *undicesimo* (11th), *ventesimo* (20th), *centesimo* (100th). A final *-tré* keeps its e but loses the accent: *ventitreesimo*.

Ordinals are adjectives, so they agree in gender and number with the noun they describe. Wiktionary also notes that ordinals form fractions (*un quarto*, *tre quarti*), that a half is *mezzo*, and that the days of the month use cardinals: *il trenta di maggio*. Capitalised hundreds name centuries, so *il Trecento* means 1301–1400, the 14th century.

## Writing figures and saying prices

Italian swaps the English separators. A **full stop marks thousands**, so 25.000 is twenty-five thousand, and a **comma marks decimals**, so 3,5 is read *tre virgola cinque*, "three comma five". Arithmetic uses *più* (plus), *meno* (minus), *per* (times) and *diviso* (divided by): *tre per sette uguale ventuno*.

Prices are in euros. Wikipedia notes the lira was Italy's currency until the euro replaced it as a unit of account in 1999, with euro coins and notes following in 2002, and that the euro is divided into 100 cents. Wiktionary records a variant form with *e* that is common when quoting prices: *tremila e seicento dollari*, "three thousand six hundred dollars". With this form a following noun stays singular, as in *cento e una pagina*.
