**Deliberate practice is repetition with four things added: a single specific target, immediate feedback, a difficulty just above your current reliable level, and a correction step before the next attempt.** A five-minute drill can satisfy all four, but only if you make choices most people skip — one mode, one difficulty, and a pause to look at what went wrong.

It is also worth knowing how large the effect is before organising your week around it. The honest answer is that deliberate practice matters, it is the part of performance you control, and it explains considerably less than the popular version of the idea suggests.

## The four conditions, stated plainly

Strip the concept back and it is a checklist.

1. **One specific target.** Not "get better at maths" but "two-digit subtraction with a borrow". A target you can name is a target you can check.
2. **Immediate feedback.** You need to know within seconds whether the attempt was right, because the longer the gap, the weaker the link between the attempt and the correction.
3. **Difficulty just beyond your current range.** If you get everything right, the session is a performance, not practice. If you get almost everything wrong, you are guessing rather than refining.
4. **Correction before the next attempt.** The repetition that teaches is the one that follows understanding what went wrong in the previous one.

Most self-directed practice fails on the first and the fourth. People practise the mode they enjoy at a level they already own, and they move straight to the next round without looking at the error.

## What the evidence says about how much it explains

The original framing — that accumulated deliberate practice largely accounts for differences in expert performance — came from a 1993 study of violinists. Two later pieces of work are the reason to state the claim carefully.

The first is a meta-analysis by Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald, [*Deliberate Practice and Performance in Music, Games, Sports, Education, and Professions*](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/macnamara-et-al-2014-deliberate-practice-and-performance-in-music-games-sports-education-and-professions-a-meta-analysis.pdf), published in *Psychological Science* in 2014. Pooling the available studies, it reported that deliberate practice explained "26% of the variance in performance for games, 21% for music, 18% for sports, 4% for education, and less than 1% for professions."

The second is a direct replication. [*The role of deliberate practice in expert performance: revisiting Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993)*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731745/), published in *Royal Society Open Science* in 2019, re-ran the violinist study and found deliberate practice accounted for 26% of the variance in performance, against the 48% reported in the original.

Read those two results together and the sensible conclusion is not "practice does not matter". It is that practice is one substantial input among several, that its share is larger in structured, well-defined domains like games and music than in loosely defined ones, and that nobody can promise you a particular outcome from a particular number of hours. Arithmetic drill is about as well-defined as a task gets, which puts it at the favourable end of that range — and still well short of the whole story.

## Mapping the four conditions onto a short drill

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and it is a convenient worked example because each of the four conditions corresponds to something you either choose or ignore on screen.

**A specific target** is the mode and difficulty you pick. Choosing Subtraction Sprint at a borrowing-heavy difficulty is a target. Opening whatever appeared first is not.

**Immediate feedback** arrives per question, and the untimed accuracy round goes further by attaching an explanation and an optional hint to each item. That untimed format is the closest thing in the app to a deliberate-practice setting, because it removes the clock that otherwise pushes you past the correction step.

**Difficulty just beyond your range** is the ladder. The arithmetic modes carry difficulty labels that change what is actually generated — single digit up through two-digit by two-digit and mixed chains — and the memory modes have seven levels that change sequence length, grid size and how long an item is shown. The rule of thumb worth using is that if you are not getting a noticeable share wrong, you are on the wrong rung; [why practice scores stop improving](/blog/why-practice-progress-plateaus/) covers what to change when they do.

**Correction before the next attempt** is the part no app can do for you. The technique library is the built-in support here: each of its entries carries steps, a worked example, a "best for" note and a named common mistake, so a missed question can be traced to a method rather than filed as bad luck.

A concrete example of what correction looks like. If 47 × 6 comes out wrong, the useful question is which part failed. Splitting it gives 40 × 6 = 240 and 7 × 6 = 42, and 240 + 42 = 282. Checking the other way, 282 ÷ 6 = 47. If the error was in the 7 × 6, that is a fact-recall problem and the fix is the times-table deck. If the error was in adding 240 and 42, that is a carrying problem and the fix is a different drill entirely. A score tells you neither.

## Where a game works against deliberate practice

This is the part most articles on the subject leave out, and it applies to our app as much as to any other.

**A score blurs the diagnosis.** Points totals in drill games typically fold correctness together with speed and streak effects, which means two sessions with the same score can contain very different error patterns. Accuracy and the list of missed items are the informative outputs; [tracking practice progress without fooling yourself](/blog/how-to-track-practice-progress/) goes through which numbers to read first.

**Timers punish the correction step.** Under a clock, stopping to understand an error costs you the next three questions, so the format quietly trains you to skip exactly the thing that makes practice deliberate.

**You do not choose the items.** Generated questions are drawn from a difficulty band, not from your personal error list, so the mode is only as targeted as its narrowest difficulty setting. That is a real limitation, and the honest way around it is to use narrow modes rather than mixed ones when you are working on a weakness — the reverse of the advice in [interleaved versus blocked practice](/blog/interleaving-versus-blocked-practice/), which applies once the method is installed.

**Feeling productive is not evidence.** Soderstrom and Bjork's review [*Learning Versus Performance*](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/11/soderstorm_ra_learningvsperformance.pdf) notes that "people often mistakenly interpret their performance during acquisition as a reliable guide to long-term learning". A session that went smoothly may have been too easy to teach anything.

## A five-minute structure that meets the conditions

One version, built only from short formats and the pause that makes them count:

- **Minute 0–1.** One untimed accuracy round on your weakest operation. Read every explanation, including on the ones you got right.
- **Minute 1–2.** Open the technique matching the error you just made and work its example on paper.
- **Minute 2–4.** Two short timed rounds on that same operation at a difficulty where you expect to miss some.
- **Minute 4–5.** Look at accuracy and median answer time, not the score, and note which item type caused the misses.

That is five minutes with a target, feedback, an appropriate level and a correction step. It will feel less satisfying than five minutes of scoring well, which is rather the point.

The [IES practice guide on organising instruction and study](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1) is the closest thing to official backing for the structural half of this: its recommendations include interleaving worked example solutions with problem-solving exercises and using quizzing to re-expose learners to key content, which is a reasonable description of alternating between a technique's worked example and a drill on the same material.

None of this makes drill a route to general cognitive improvement — the cornerstone guide [do brain training apps work](/blog/do-brain-training-apps-work/) sets out why that claim is weak, and the rest of the [brain training games hub](/blog/topics/brain-training-games/) stays inside the narrower claim. What deliberate practice offers is a better return on the minutes you were going to spend anyway.
