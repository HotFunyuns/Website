A brain age score in a phone app is a performance index with an age label attached. You answer questions, the app combines your accuracy and your response times into a number, and that number is mapped onto an age through a rule the developer wrote. There is no biology in the chain. The same phrase is also used for something entirely different in neuroimaging research, and the collision of the two meanings is why the app version feels more authoritative than it is.

## The research version is an imaging estimate

In neuroimaging, brain age estimation trains a model on brain scans from healthy people of known ages, then applies it to a new scan. The BrainAGE framework, described in a review covering its first decade, derives its estimate from structural T1-weighted MRI: the images are preprocessed, the data reduced, a regression model is trained on healthy reference brains, and the model's estimate for an individual is compared with that person's actual age.

The quantity of interest is the gap, not the estimate. As the review puts it, "The difference between a person's estimated brain age and its chronological age finally identifies the individual deviation from the typical maturation/aging trajectory." Positive values indicate an estimate above chronological age.

That is a research instrument with a defined input, a documented model and an active methodological literature about its limitations, including how prediction error varies across the age range. Whatever its open questions, it begins with a picture of a brain.

## The app version has no such input

An app has two observations about you: whether you answered correctly, and how long each answer took. That is it. There is no scan, no biological sample, no clinical norm sample and no examiner. So any age label the app prints must be produced by the same two observations passed through a mapping the developer chose.

That mapping is a design decision, not a measurement. Two apps given identical performance can print ages a decade apart, because the choice of which index value corresponds to "34" is arbitrary in the sense that nothing external fixes it. It can be tuned to make most users feel good, or tuned to make improvement look dramatic, and neither choice is detectable from the outside.

This is where the questions in [reliability and validity in testing](/blog/reliability-and-validity-in-testing/) earn their keep. Ask what the norm sample is. Ask what evidence supports the interpretation that a lower printed age means anything about you. Ask whether the figure has a published margin of error. For an app-generated brain age, all three questions usually terminate immediately, which is itself the answer.

## Why the number moves, and what that does not prove

Performance indices are noisy, and everything ordinary moves them: sleep, time of day, caffeine, whether you are sitting still, the size of the screen, whether someone spoke to you mid-round. Reaction-time measures in particular are sensitive to all of these.

The largest effect, though, is familiarity. The second time you meet a task format you are faster, because you already know the layout, the rhythm and what the instructions mean, before any change in the underlying skill. An age label that improves over a week of daily play is consistent with getting better at that specific game and nothing else. The general version of this problem — improvement on the trained task, uncertain transfer to anything else — is the substance of [do brain training apps work](/blog/do-brain-training-apps-work/) and of [working memory and training](/blog/working-memory-and-training/).

The scientific consensus statement issued jointly by the Stanford Center on Longevity and the Max Planck Institute for Human Development states the position bluntly: "there is little evidence that playing brain games improves underlying broad cognitive abilities, or that it enables one to better navigate a complex realm of everyday life", and it concludes that claims promoting brain games "are frequently exaggerated and at times misleading."

## Regulators have already addressed this style of claim

The Federal Trade Commission's 2016 action against the maker of Lumosity is the reference case. The FTC announced a settlement including $2 million in consumer redress over advertising that claimed the program would improve performance "on everyday tasks, in school, at work, and in athletics" and would "delay age-related cognitive decline and protect against mild cognitive impairment, dementia, and Alzheimer's disease", together with claims about a range of health conditions.

The agency's own business-guidance post framed the evidentiary question in terms any reader can apply: if a person's game scores go up, "does that establish a boost in memory, focus, and productivity that will translate into tangible day-to-day benefits? Or does it just show that the person got better at playing the game?" The settlement required human clinical testing before the company could make a broad range of cognition claims.

An age label is a compressed version of exactly the claim the FTC examined. It asserts, in one number, that your performance corresponds to a stage of life. Nothing an app can observe supports that inference.

## What this app does, and what it refuses to do

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and it produces no brain age, no cognitive age, no age comparison and no percentile. It contains no IQ test and no cognitive assessment. It is published as an educational game, and the numbers it shows are statistics about your own sessions: score, accuracy, streak, average and median response time, fastest correct answer, and personal bests for each mode.

Those numbers are worth reading for one narrow purpose — comparing your own runs on the same mode at the same difficulty — and they are worth nothing as a statement about you. Arithmetic drills such as Multiplication Rush, Addition Sprint and Mixed Operations get you faster at arithmetic. Memory rounds on numbers, sequences, grids and delayed recall get you better at those rounds. That is the whole claim, and there is no version of it that involves your age.
