**Italian verbs come in three families, defined by the infinitive ending: -are, -ere and -ire.** Each family takes its own set of personal endings, so once you can identify the family, you know which pattern to apply.

That is the mechanical part, and it takes an afternoon. The strategic part — which verbs to learn and in what order — is what actually determines whether you can say anything.

## Why the ending replaces the pronoun

Italian conjugates for person: the verb form is different for I, you, he/she, we, you (plural) and they. Because the ending already identifies who is acting, **the subject pronoun is normally left out.**

This is the first structural adjustment for an English speaker. English relies on the pronoun and has almost no verb inflection; Italian relies on the inflection and drops the pronoun. Pronouns reappear only for emphasis or contrast.

The practical consequence: **you cannot skip conjugation the way you can skip some grammar.** Without the correct ending, the sentence has no subject.

## The awkward truth about frequency

The verbs you need most are the ones that follow the patterns least.

To be, to have, to go, to do, to want, to be able to, to have to, to say, to know — these appear constantly in any conversation, and most are irregular.

This is not an Italian quirk. It is a general property of language: **frequency preserves irregularity.** Rare words get regularised over time because nobody remembers the exception. Common words are rehearsed constantly, so the exception survives. The same is true of English's "go/went" and "be/was".

For a learner, the implication is direct: **you cannot learn Italian verbs by learning patterns and then adding exceptions.** You learn about ten individual verbs first, and the patterns cover everything after that.

## A prioritised order

| Priority | What to learn | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The verbs for "to be" and "to have", present tense | Both are used constantly and both are auxiliaries for compound tenses |
| 2 | Regular present tense for all three families | The pattern that covers most verbs |
| 3 | High-frequency irregulars: go, do/make, want, can, must, say, know | Appear in almost every conversation |
| 4 | The near-past (a compound past built with the auxiliaries) | Lets you narrate |
| 5 | Simple future, or the present used with future time words | Often the present is enough |
| 6 | Everything else | Later |

Steps 1 to 3 are the ones that produce disproportionate returns. A learner with ten verbs conjugated confidently in the present tense can hold a basic conversation. A learner with three tenses of regular verbs and no irregulars cannot.

## Why the auxiliaries come first

The verbs meaning "to be" and "to have" do double duty: they are ordinary verbs *and* they are the auxiliaries used to build compound tenses.

That means learning them is not just learning two verbs. It is learning the machinery for every compound tense you will meet later. Time spent on them early pays back repeatedly.

One complication worth flagging: Italian chooses between the two auxiliaries depending on the verb, and with one of them the past participle agrees with the subject. That agreement rule is a genuine difficulty, and it is a later-stage problem — it is mentioned here only so it does not surprise you.

## The present tense goes further than you expect

Beginners rush toward more tenses. It is usually the wrong instinct.

Combined with time expressions — tomorrow, later, next week, since — the present tense handles a large share of ordinary conversation. Some constructions that require a different tense in English use the present in Italian.

The better use of early study time is:

1. Make the present tense **automatic**, not merely known.
2. Build vocabulary, so you have something to conjugate.
3. Add tenses once the first two are solid.

## What conjugation is not

It is worth putting Italian's grammar in proportion. Conjugating for person is real work, but consider what Italian does *not* have: no case system, no separate formal declension tables for nouns, no tone system.

[Russian cases explained for beginners](/blog/russian-cases-explained-for-beginners/) covers a language where nouns change form according to their role in the sentence — a considerably larger memory load than Italian's verb endings. Every language front-loads difficulty somewhere; Italian's is here, and it is a comparatively contained problem.
