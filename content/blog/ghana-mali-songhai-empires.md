Ghana, Mali and Songhai were three separate states in the West African Sahel, not one empire that changed its name twice. They had different ruling families, different capitals, different centuries and — crucially for anyone trying to study them — radically different bodies of surviving evidence. What they shared was a region and a trading system.

The fastest way to keep them apart is to attach each one to a place and a piece of physical evidence you could go and stand in front of.

## The separation in one table

This table gives the anchor points, each drawn from a UNESCO World Heritage inscription, so every row can be checked against a specific dossier.

| State | Anchor site | What UNESCO's inscription dates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ghana | The Saharan caravan towns of Mauritania | Ouadane, Chinguetti, Tichitt and Oualata founded in the 11th–12th centuries to serve trans-Saharan routes |
| Mali | Timbuktu and Djenné | Timbuktu's economic and cultural apogee in the 15th–16th centuries; Djingareyber Mosque begun under Sultan Kankan Moussa |
| Songhai | Gao | The Tomb of Askia built by Askia Mohamed in 1495 |

The overlap in that table is deliberate. The Mauritanian ksour post-date Ghana's height and were still functioning under Mali; Timbuktu's peak straddles the Mali-to-Songhai transition. Sahelian political history does not divide into clean blocks, and any timeline that shows three tidy consecutive bars is simplifying.

## Ghana: the state we know least about

Ghana is the earliest of the three and the hardest to write about responsibly, because almost nothing survives that its own government produced. What exists is archaeology, and accounts written by North African and Arab geographers who were describing a place most of them had not seen.

What can be said with confidence is structural. The Sahel states sat at the southern terminus of caravan routes crossing the Sahara, and UNESCO's inscription for the four Mauritanian ksour describes towns "originally built to serve the important caravan trade routes that began crossing the Sahara", developed between the twelfth and sixteenth centuries as "a series of stages along the trans-Saharan trade route". These were obligatory stops linking North Africa to the river regions of western Africa and the savanna zone beyond.

That is the economic machine all three empires sat on. A state in this position taxes movement rather than land, which shapes everything about how it is organised.

It also means the comparison people reach for — the Silk Road — is only half right. The goods, the actors and the environmental constraints are different, and the camel caravan and the Saharan well network have no equivalent in [the Silk Road for beginners](/blog/the-silk-road-for-beginners/). Treat them as two distinct systems that happened to share a logic.

## Mali: Timbuktu, Djenné and the scholarship economy

Mali is where the evidence gets thicker, and where the popular story gets least accurate.

UNESCO's Timbuktu dossier gives the town a fifth-century foundation and dates its economic and cultural apogee to the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. At that peak it records the University of Sankore with 180 Koranic schools and 25,000 students, and describes the town as a market "where the trading of manuscripts was negotiated, and salt from Teghaza in the north, gold was sold, and cattle and grain from the south."

Note the order of that list. Manuscripts are named first. Timbuktu's significance is not only that gold passed through it, but that books did — commissioned, copied, bought and sold as commodities.

The architecture records the political history precisely. The Djingareyber Mosque's initial construction, in UNESCO's account, dates to Sultan Kankan Moussa returning from a pilgrimage to Mecca; it was rebuilt and enlarged between 1570 and 1583 by the Imam Al Aqib, Qadi of Timbuktu, who added the southern section and the cemetery wall. The Sankore Mosque was built in the fourteenth century and restored by the same Al Aqib between 1578 and 1582, when he had the sanctuary demolished and rebuilt to the dimensions of the Kaaba. The Sidi Yahia Mosque went up around 1400 and was restored in 1577–78.

Djenné complicates the story usefully. UNESCO describes it as one of the oldest towns in sub-Saharan Africa, and the inscribed property is a serial one: the present town's old fabric plus four archaeological sites — Djenné-Djeno, Hambarkétolo, Kaniana and Tonomba. Excavations in 1977, 1981, 1996 and 1997 revealed a pre-Islamic urban structure with circular and rectangular buildings and a wealth of material remains, which UNESCO dates back to the third century BCE.

In other words, urban life in the Niger inland delta is roughly two millennia older than the empire usually credited with it.

## Songhai: Gao and 1495

Songhai is the last of the three and, at Gao, the most precisely datable. UNESCO records that the pyramidal structure of the Tomb of Askia was built by Askia Mohamed, Emperor of the Songhai Empire, in 1495 — built, specifically, when Gao became the capital and Islam was adopted as the official religion.

The complex comprises the pyramidal tower, two flat-roofed mosques, a necropolis and a white stone square. UNESCO calls it the most important and best conserved vestige of the Songhai Empire, which extended through West Africa in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and describes the architecture as Sudano-Sahelian: rounded earthen forms whose plaster layer is renewed each year after the rains erode it, with permanent timber scaffolding built into the structure.

That annual renewal is worth dwelling on. A building of this kind survives because a community keeps rebuilding it, which makes the monument a record of continuous practice rather than a frozen artefact.

## Three different evidence problems

Here is the part that changes how you read everything above.

For Ghana, the evidence is archaeological plus external testimony, so claims about its politics and religion are inferences with wide uncertainty. For Mali, there is standing architecture, a large manuscript corpus and external accounts, so confidence is much higher — though still uneven, since the manuscripts survive selectively and the external accounts have their own agendas. For Songhai, there is a dated monument, an identified builder and a documented religious policy.

Even the European sources need this treatment. The Bibliothèque nationale de France holds the digitised sea-chart atlas usually called the Catalan Atlas, one of the medieval European documents most often reached for when writing about Sahelian gold — and the library's own catalogue records its authorship as "Abraham Cresques ?", question mark included. If the attribution of a famous source is uncertain enough for its custodian to flag it, the claims built on top of it deserve the same caution.

That variation in confidence is the real lesson, and it is the same one at work in [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/): a date is only as good as the chain of reasoning behind it.

One more framing point. Readers trained on European history tend to look for a collapse narrative, and the arguments rehearsed in [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/) are a useful warning about what that instinct costs. Sahelian states did not so much fall as get reorganised by shifting trade, external military pressure and internal succession disputes, and the transitions between Ghana, Mali and Songhai are better understood as changes in who taxed the routes than as three separate endings.
