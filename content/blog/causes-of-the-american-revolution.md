The causes of the American Revolution are easiest to follow as a chain. A war that left Britain in debt produced new taxes; the taxes produced an argument about who had the right to levy them; the argument produced boycotts and riots; and Parliament's punishment of Boston in 1774 turned a dispute over revenue into a dispute over self-government. The US State Department's Office of the Historian summarises it in one line: the Revolution was precipitated, in part, by a series of laws on trade and taxes passed between 1763 and 1775.

That "in part" is worth keeping. What follows is the sequence, step by step, with the evidence for each link.

## 1763: a victory that left a bill

Britain emerged from the Seven Years' War in 1763, the Office of the Historian notes, "burdened by heavy debts." Wikipedia gives the scale: a national debt of £133 million, with annual debt payments of £5 million out of an £8 million budget. Keeping troops in North America permanently would cost a further £360,000 a year.

The same year brought a second grievance that had nothing to do with money. After Pontiac's War broke out, the Royal Proclamation of 1763 closed the land between the Appalachians and the Mississippi to colonial settlement. Wikipedia records that it angered settlers, fur traders and land speculators, and that it failed to stop westward migration.

## The Sugar Act and the Stamp Act, 1764–1766

The first revenue measure was a tax cut on paper. Wikipedia records that the Sugar Act of 1764 lowered the duty on foreign molasses from 6 pence to 3 pence a gallon, while stepping up enforcement against smuggling and letting customs officials prosecute in vice admiralty courts, where Crown-appointed judges rather than local juries decided cases. The Office of the Historian makes the same point about the effect: duties that had been laxly collected were now enforced, so revenue rose.

Here is the arithmetic of why a cut could still hurt:

| Measure | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Duty on foreign molasses | 6d per gallon | 3d per gallon |
| Change in the rate | | −3d, a 50% cut |
| Duty collected in practice | little, as enforcement was lax | 3d per gallon, now enforced |

A merchant who had paid little under a laxly enforced law could be worse off at half the old rate. The 1764 Currency Act, which the Office of the Historian says forbade colonies from issuing paper currency, made debts and taxes harder still to pay.

Then came the Stamp Act of 1765, which Wikipedia describes as the first direct tax on the colonies, applying to newspapers, legal documents, almanacs and even playing cards. Its reception was the turning point. The Virginia House of Burgesses denied Parliament's authority to tax the colonies; a Boston crowd destroyed the stamp distributor's house; and in October 1765 delegates from nine colonies met in New York. The Office of the Historian calls the act "a common cause to unite the 13 colonies."

## Representation, not the rate

The core dispute was constitutional. Wikipedia states plainly that the colonists did not object that the taxes were high, because they were actually low; they objected to having no voice in the Parliament that passed them. Grenville claimed that Americans paid about 1 shilling a head in imperial taxes against 26 shillings paid by the English, a per-head figure that excludes local taxes.

Parliament's answer was "virtual representation": most people in Britain could not vote either, yet Parliament represented them all. James Otis replied that no member sat for any colonial constituency, so the colonists were represented by no one. Parliament repealed the Stamp Act on 21 February 1766, but the Declaratory Act of March 1766 kept its claim to legislate for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever."

## Townshend duties, a shooting and a tea monopoly, 1767–1773

The Townshend Acts of 1767 put duties on paper, glass and tea, on the theory, Wikipedia explains, that Americans objected only to internal taxes and not to customs duties. Boycotts followed; troops arrived in Boston in 1768; and on 5 March 1770 soldiers fired into a crowd, hitting 11 people, five of whom died. Wikipedia notes the soldiers were tried and acquitted, defended by John Adams.

Most of the Townshend duties were repealed in 1770, except the one on tea. The Tea Act of 1773 let the East India Company undersell colonial traders, and on 16 December 1773 men disguised as Indigenous people dumped the company's tea into Boston Harbor.

## 1774: punishing Boston pulled the colonies together

Parliament's response — the four laws colonists called the Intolerable Acts — closed Boston's port until the tea was paid for, altered the Massachusetts charter and restricted town meetings. Wikipedia notes they were meant to end self-government in Massachusetts but instead increased support for revolution. Colonial legislatures sent delegates to the First Continental Congress in Philadelphia in September 1774, which called a boycott of British goods from 1 December.

The Office of the Historian adds a detail that shows how close the alternatives were: General Thomas Gage, military governor of Massachusetts, recommended suspending the Intolerable Acts, and London removed him from his post instead. Fighting began at Lexington and Concord on 19 April 1775, twelve years after the 1763 peace. The Declaration of Independence followed on 4 July 1776, and its list of grievances — reading it as [a primary source](/blog/how-to-analyse-a-primary-source/) is rewarding — names taxes "without our Consent," standing armies, quartering, trade restrictions and the loss of jury trials.
