Before a single Shanghainese lesson could be written, somebody had to decide how to spell the language in Latin letters. There is no standard to inherit. Wu Chinese has four broad schools of romanisation, several of them with internal variants, and they disagree about something as basic as what the letter *b* is for.

(You will see this written as Shanghainese romanization as often as romanisation, depending on where the source was published. Same thing.)

This is the sort of decision that is invisible in a finished course and determines a great deal about what using it feels like. Here is the comparison we ran, what we picked, and the readability compromise we accepted.

## The four schools

**Common Wu Pinyin** is the most widely used, and it came from amateur language clubs and local learners rather than from an institution. It contains two closely related schemes: Wu Chinese Society pinyin, developed around 2005, and Wugniu pinyin, built on it around 2016. Both adhere to IPA conventions. Wu Chinese Society pinyin generally does not mark tones. Either of them is the default in most learning material, and the name *Wugniu* is itself the Shanghainese pronunciation of 吴语.

**The missionary school** is the historical one — English-based Latin orthographies developed by Western missionaries in the late 19th and early 20th centuries for Bible translation and educational texts. The Edkins romanisation of Shanghainese is the representative example. It is of real documentary value and no longer a practical choice for a learner.

**The Latin Phonetic Method**, also called French-Wu or *Fawu*, exploits similarities between French and Wu phonetics and follows both IPA and French orthographic conventions. It was developed in 2001 by a Shanghai-born surgeon living in Lyon. Its use is in decline.

**Hanyu-Pinyin-derived schemes** are the only school developed by professional linguists, mostly in state-administered universities. More than twenty competing schemes in this family have been published since the 1980s. The most notable is Qian Nairong's Shanghainese Pinyin from 2006, often shortened to Qian's Pin, which appears in formally published dictionaries and textbooks.

## The comparison that actually decided it

Read past the labels and the schools split on one question: **are Latin letters being assigned by international phonetic convention, or by what they already mean to a Mandarin pinyin reader?**

Shanghainese has a three-way stop contrast — voiceless unaspirated, aspirated, and voiced. Mandarin has a two-way one. Here is how the schemes handle the three sounds:

| Sound | Wu Chinese Society | Wugniu | French-Wu | Qian's Pinyin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Voiceless unaspirated (as in 巴, 百) | p | p | p | b |
| Aspirated (as in 怕, 捧) | ph | ph | ph | p |
| Voiced (as in 旁, 别) | b | b | b | bh |

Look at the two ends of that table. In the first three columns *b* is a voiced sound, which is what *b* means in most languages written in Latin script. In Qian's scheme *b* is a voiceless unaspirated sound, which is what *b* means in Mandarin pinyin, and the voiced sound has to be written *bh*.

Neither is wrong. They are optimising for different readers. Qian's scheme is friendlier to someone arriving with Mandarin pinyin already in their head; the IPA-aligned schemes are friendlier to someone arriving from any other language, and truer to what the letters mean outside the Chinese-teaching world.

## What we chose

We use the Common Wu Pinyin conventions — the IPA-aligned side of the table — and we show them alongside characters on every line.

The reasoning came down to three things.

**It is what the learner will meet elsewhere.** Either Wu Chinese Society pinyin or Wugniu is the default in most Shanghainese learning material. A learner who finishes our course and goes looking for more should not have to re-learn the notation to read the next thing they pick up. Choosing the locally optimal scheme and stranding the learner at the end of it would be a poor trade.

**It does not require Mandarin.** Qian's scheme is legible to a Mandarin reader and opaque to everyone else. Our course is explicitly built for people who want Shanghainese rather than Mandarin, including heritage learners with no Mandarin literacy at all, and choosing a notation that assumes pinyin would have quietly contradicted the premise of the whole thing.

**It keeps the voiced series visible.** Shanghainese has voiced obstruent initials, a feature rare outside the Wu and Xiang groups, and it is the contrast a Mandarin ear most reliably fails to hear. A notation that writes the voiced sound as *b* keeps it looking like what it is. A notation that writes it as *bh* — a digraph, visually a modified version of something else — makes it look like a footnote on a sound the learner already knows. It is not.

## The compromise we accepted

Here is the cost, stated plainly: **for a learner arriving with solid Mandarin pinyin, our romanisation reads wrong for the first week.**

They see *p* and produce an aspirated sound, because that is what *p* means in pinyin. They see *b* and produce a voiceless unaspirated one. Both are wrong in our scheme and both feel completely natural. Until the mapping is rebuilt, a fluent pinyin reader is a *worse* reader of our romanisation than someone who has never seen pinyin at all.

That is a real disadvantage imposed on a real and substantial part of the audience, and we did not talk ourselves out of it. We chose to accept it rather than design it away, because the alternative — optimising the notation for Mandarin readers — has a cost that is smaller-looking and permanent: it builds a Mandarin frame into the course at the level of the alphabet, in a course whose entire argument is that Shanghainese should not be taught through Mandarin.

A one-week relearning cost is recoverable. A structural assumption is not.

The mitigation is explicit rather than clever. The pronunciation lessons state the letter values up front and name the pinyin conflict directly, rather than leaving the learner to discover it by sounding wrong. Beyond that, the audio is the authority: [Learn Shanghainese Wu Chinese](/apps/learn-shanghainese/) records whole phrases from Wu speakers, and the romanisation is a reminder of something you have heard rather than the source of truth for a sound you have not.

## If you are reading a different scheme

You will be, sooner or later. The rule is short: work out which school a source belongs to before you trust a spelling, and never mix two inside one study session. A letter's value is only defined inside its own system.

Tone marking varies too. Because the yin-yang split and the checked tones are conditioned by the syllable's own structure, many schemes mark only the dark level tone, often with an acute or grave accent, and leave the rest unmarked — [Shanghainese tones and tone sandhi](/blog/shanghainese-tones-and-tone-sandhi/) explains why that is defensible rather than lazy. Wu Chinese Society pinyin generally does not mark tone at all.
