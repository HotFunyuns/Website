Here is the thing nobody mentions when they teach you the five positions: they are not in the rulebook. FIBA, which publishes the Official Basketball Rules recognised internationally, governs how many players a team may have on the playing court and how the game is conducted. It does not say what any of them is called or what any of them is for.

Positions are a vocabulary that grew up around the sport to describe recurring jobs. That is why they can shift over a decade without a single rule changing, and why arguments about whether a player "is" a four or a five are arguments about description, not about legality.

Understanding them is still worth your time — you cannot talk about basketball without them — but understanding what kind of thing they are is what stops you being confused later.

## Why the numbers run 1 to 5

The numbering is not arbitrary and it is much easier to remember than the names once you see the logic: **it runs from the player who operates furthest from the basket to the player who operates closest to it.**

1. Point guard — furthest out, brings the ball up
2. Shooting guard — perimeter, primarily a scorer
3. Small forward — the middle, on the wing
4. Power forward — inside the arc, works the interior
5. Centre — closest to the rim

That single organising idea also explains the groupings you hear constantly: 1 and 2 are the **guards**, 3 and sometimes 2 and 4 are the **wings**, 4 and 5 are the **bigs**.

## The five, one at a time

### 1 — Point guard

The point guard's job is decision-making. They bring the ball up against pressure, organise the offence, choose which action the team runs, and are usually the last person to touch the ball before a shot goes up.

**Core skills:** ball-handling under pressure, court vision, passing accuracy and timing, decision speed.

**Typical build:** traditionally the smallest player on the floor, because the job rewards handling and agility over size.

**How the job goes wrong:** turnovers under pressure, and — more subtly — a point guard who dominates the ball so much that four teammates stand still.

### 2 — Shooting guard

The two is a perimeter scorer. Where the point guard creates opportunities, the shooting guard is usually the one converting them: coming off screens, spotting up, attacking a closeout.

**Core skills:** shooting from range, movement without the ball, finishing after the catch, and — often overlooked — defending the opponent's best perimeter scorer.

**Typical build:** taller than the point guard, still built for speed rather than strength.

**How the job goes wrong:** a two who only functions with the ball in their hands is doing the one job while occupying a slot designed for the other.

### 3 — Small forward

The three is the sport's generalist. The role sits between the perimeter and the interior and is usually asked to do a little of everything: score inside and out, defend multiple positions, rebound without being a primary rebounder.

**Core skills:** versatility above all, plus the physical range to guard both smaller and larger opponents.

**Typical build:** the balance point of the roster — big enough to hold up inside, quick enough to survive on the perimeter.

**How the job goes wrong:** a three with no defined strength ends up merely adequate at everything, which is different from being versatile.

### 4 — Power forward

The four works inside the arc. Rebounding on both ends, defending in the paint, setting screens, and scoring in the mid-range and around the rim.

**Core skills:** rebounding, physical strength, screening, interior finishing — and increasingly, shooting from range.

**Typical build:** large and strong, built for contact.

**How the job goes wrong:** a four who cannot function away from the basket shrinks the floor, because the defender guarding them has no reason to leave the paint.

### 5 — Centre

The five is the anchor. Defensively they protect the rim and are the last line when a drive gets past everyone else. Offensively they finish close to the basket, screen, and collect offensive rebounds.

**Core skills:** rim protection, positioning, rebounding, finishing in traffic.

**Typical build:** usually the tallest and longest player on the roster.

**How the job goes wrong:** a five who cannot move laterally becomes a target the moment the opposition forces them to defend away from the basket.

## The three groupings that actually organise a roster

If you take one structural idea away from this article, make it this one. Most modern thinking about lineups uses three bands rather than five slots:

- **Guards** — handle the ball, create advantages, initiate.
- **Wings** — score and defend across the perimeter and the space just inside it.
- **Bigs** — protect the rim, rebound, screen, finish inside.

The bands are useful because they describe what a lineup *needs* rather than what players are called. A five-player unit has to be able to bring the ball up against pressure, space the floor so the paint is not congested, and protect the rim. If a lineup cannot do all three, it does not matter what positions are listed next to the names.

## Positionless basketball, accurately described

The phrase gets used to mean "positions no longer exist", which is not what happened.

What happened is that the sport got better at recognising that these responsibilities can be distributed differently. A tall player who can handle the ball can initiate offence. A big who can shoot from range drags a rim protector away from the rim. A team can switch every screen if all five players can defend across a wide range of opponents.

The vocabulary followed: **combo guard** for a player between the one and the two, **point forward** for a forward who initiates, **stretch four** for a power forward who shoots from range, **small-ball five** for a team playing a forward at centre to gain speed at the cost of size.

Notice that every one of those terms is defined *by reference to the traditional positions*. That is the clearest evidence the framework has not been discarded. It has been demoted from an identity to a description — a shorthand for what a player is doing in a given possession rather than what they are.

There is also a genuine cost to the flexible approach, which usually goes unmentioned: a lineup where everyone can do a bit of everything can end up with nobody who does any one thing well enough to build around. Versatility is a strategy with trade-offs, not a free upgrade.

## Position, role and assignment are three different things

Worth separating, because they get conflated:

- **Position** is the label on the roster.
- **Role** is what the team asks this player to do — primary scorer, defensive stopper, floor spacer, connector.
- **Assignment** is who they are guarding on this possession, which can change several times within one trip down the floor.

A player can be listed at one position, occupy an entirely different role, and take assignments that match neither. When you hear that a team "plays two point guards", this is the distinction being described: two players whose *position* is the one, sharing a *role* usually held by a single player.

## What it means when you are building a roster instead

From the front-office side, positions stop being identities and become **coverage**. The question is not "do I have a small forward" but "can this lineup bring the ball up, space the floor and protect the rim". That is the subject of [how to build a balanced basketball roster](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-basketball-roster/), and it is the reason drafting five high-rated players at overlapping positions produces a worse team than drafting four and a specialist.
