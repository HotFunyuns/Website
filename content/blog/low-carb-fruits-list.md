Fruit is where low-carb eating gets complicated. All fruit contains sugar, but the amount, and how much of the carbohydrate is fibre, varies widely. Below, common raw fruits are ranked by **net carbs**, with carbohydrate and fibre alongside, for a usual portion and for 100 g. The figures come from USDA FoodData Central, using the SR Legacy database and its portion weights (for example "Apples, raw, with skin", 182 g for a medium apple), and each table gives both the per-portion and the per-100 g figure.

## Reading the tables

"Net" means carbohydrate after fibre is taken away, the figure a net-carb tracker displays. On packaged fruit, the FDA label gives you Total Carbohydrate and Dietary Fiber but leaves that subtraction undone; [net carbs explained](/blog/net-carbs-explained-keto-tracking/) goes through the definition. Everything here is raw, whole fruit.

## Lowest: berries and citrus

| Fruit | Portion | Carbohydrate | Fibre | Net | Net in 100 g |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lemon, without peel | 1 fruit (58 g) | 5.4 g | 1.6 g | 3.8 g | 6.5 g |
| Blackberries | 1 cup (144 g) | 13.8 g | 7.6 g | 6.2 g | 4.3 g |
| Raspberries | 1 cup (123 g) | 14.7 g | 8.0 g | 6.7 g | 5.4 g |
| Plum | 1 fruit (66 g) | 7.5 g | 0.9 g | 6.6 g | 10.0 g |
| Strawberries | 1 cup halves (152 g) | 11.7 g | 3.0 g | 8.6 g | 5.7 g |
| Kiwifruit, green | 1 fruit (69 g) | 10.1 g | 2.1 g | 8.0 g | 11.7 g |

Raspberries and blackberries stand out for **fibre**: about 8 g a cup, more than half their total carbohydrate. That is why they are the lowest common fruits per 100 g.

## Middle: melons and stone fruit

| Fruit | Portion | Carbohydrate | Fibre | Net | Net in 100 g |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Watermelon | 1 cup diced (152 g) | 11.5 g | 0.6 g | 10.9 g | 7.1 g |
| Cantaloupe | 1 cup cubes (160 g) | 13.1 g | 1.4 g | 11.6 g | 7.3 g |
| Peach, yellow | 1 medium (150 g) | 14.3 g | 2.3 g | 12.1 g | 8.0 g |
| Orange | 1 fruit (131 g) | 15.4 g | 3.1 g | 12.3 g | 9.4 g |

Melons are low per 100 g, but with little fibre almost all their carbohydrate counts.

## Highest: everyday sweet fruits

| Fruit | Portion | Carbohydrate | Fibre | Net | Net in 100 g |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Blueberries | 1 cup (148 g) | 21.4 g | 3.6 g | 17.9 g | 12.1 g |
| Apple, with skin | 1 medium (182 g) | 25.1 g | 4.4 g | 20.8 g | 11.4 g |
| Cherries, sweet | 1 cup without pits (154 g) | 24.7 g | 3.2 g | 21.4 g | 13.9 g |
| Pear | 1 medium (178 g) | 27.1 g | 5.5 g | 21.6 g | 12.1 g |
| Mango | 1 cup pieces (165 g) | 24.7 g | 2.6 g | 22.1 g | 13.4 g |
| Banana | 1 medium (118 g) | 27.0 g | 3.1 g | 23.9 g | 20.2 g |
| Grapes | 1 cup (151 g) | 27.3 g | 1.4 g | 26.0 g | 17.2 g |

Blueberries are a berry, but USDA's figures put them closer to apples than to raspberries: about 17.9 g of net carbs a cup against 6.7 g.

## Serving size matters as much as the fruit

Per 100 g, a plum (10.0 g) is only a little lower than an apple (11.4 g) and a kiwifruit (11.7 g) is slightly higher; they look low-carb in the table mainly because they come in smaller pieces. **A worked example:** a medium apple has about 20.8 g of net carbs. Half a cup of raspberries, about 61.5 g, has about 3.3 g, and a cup of halved strawberries about 8.6 g. Choosing berries over an apple saves roughly 12 to 18 g, while halving an apple saves about 10 g. Either change moves a low-carb day more than the choice between, say, a peach and an orange.

## Fruit and blood sugar

Net carbs are only one measure. How quickly a food raises blood glucose depends on more than its carbohydrate count; [glycemic index and load](/blog/glycemic-index-and-load/) explains those measures. [Dietary fibre explained](/blog/dietary-fiber-explained/) covers why fibre is subtracted at all, and [low-carb vegetables](/blog/low-carb-vegetables-list/) gives the same figures for vegetables.

## Logging fruit

USDA's portion weights are averages, and the fruit in your kitchen may be larger or smaller. Weigh a typical piece once and log by weight.
