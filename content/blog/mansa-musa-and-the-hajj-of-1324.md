Mansa Musa ruled the Mali Empire in the early fourteenth century, and his pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 made him the most widely reported West African ruler in the medieval Mediterranean world. What is genuinely documented about him is narrower and more interesting than the internet version: a set of Arabic accounts written by men who were not there, a European map drawn half a century later, and an economy whose scale contemporaries described in impressions rather than in figures. The claim that he was the wealthiest individual in history is not a finding. It is a modern currency conversion applied to a society where the conversion does not work.

## What is actually documented

The secure outline is this. Mali in this period controlled the southern end of the trans-Saharan trade, where West African gold was exchanged for Saharan salt and for goods from North Africa and beyond. The mansa did not personally own the goldfields; the state's position rested on controlling and taxing the exchange and on tribute from a wide territory. In 1324 Musa undertook the hajj, travelling through Egypt with a very large retinue, and his passage through Cairo left a strong impression on Egyptian officials. He is also associated with building programmes and with patronage of scholars on his return.

Almost everything beyond that outline is either reported at second hand or inferred.

## How close were the sources?

The main witnesses are Arabic writers working in Egypt and North Africa, and none of them saw the events of 1324.

Al-Umari, an Egyptian administrator, wrote in the 1330s and is explicit that he is recording what officials in Cairo told him about a visit that had taken place years earlier. Ibn Khaldun, writing later in the fourteenth century, worked from Malian and North African informants and is the main source for the dynastic sequence. Ibn Battuta reached Mali in the 1350s, which makes him a genuine eyewitness to the kingdom — under a later ruler, roughly a generation after Musa.

That is a respectable evidence base by medieval standards and a thin one by modern ones. It means the famous details — the size of the caravan, the quantities of gold distributed, the effect on Cairo's gold market — are reports of reports, made by writers with an interest in a striking story. The right form of words is "al-Umari was told that," not "it is a fact that." The general principle is set out in our guide to [how we know what historical figures actually did](/blog/how-we-know-what-historical-figures-did/), and this is one of the cleanest illustrations of it.

## The Catalan Atlas is a picture, not a measurement

The image most often used to illustrate Musa comes from the Catalan Atlas, a Majorcan world map of 1375 that shows a crowned West African ruler seated with a gold object in his hand, facing a mounted traveller. It is a marvellous object and it is not evidence of Malian finances.

It was made in Europe about half a century after the pilgrimage, by cartographers working from merchant and travellers' report. What it documents is Mali's reputation in the Mediterranean world — which is itself historically significant, because it shows that a West African state had become a fixed point on the European mental map of wealth. It documents nothing about quantities.

## Why the superlative cannot be computed

Rankings of the richest people in history work by taking a reported quantity of gold, converting it at a modern gold price, and adjusting for inflation. Every step of that fails here.

Modern inflation adjustment assumes a continuous price series and a stable basket of goods; neither exists between fourteenth-century Mali and the present. Gold's purchasing power was radically different in a society where it was locally abundant and in one where it was scarce. And the underlying category does not transfer: a mansa's command over gold was an attribute of the office and of a tributary state, not a portfolio he could liquidate. Asking how many dollars he was worth is like asking the horsepower of a river.

There is a better question, and the sources can answer it: what did his resources let him do? Finance a pilgrimage across the Sahara with a large retinue, distribute gifts on a scale that outsiders remembered for decades, recruit scholars and craftsmen, and build. That is a description of capability, which is what the evidence supports.

If you want a method for testing claims of this kind generally, the dating and measurement techniques in [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) apply directly: ask what was actually measured, by whom, and against what baseline.

## Timbuktu, Sankoré and what the manuscripts show

Timbuktu is the other half of the popular story, and it needs a chronological correction. The Library of Congress, whose 2003 exhibition *Ancient Manuscripts from the Desert Libraries of Timbuktu* brought the corpus to a wide audience, places the city's foundation roughly nine centuries before that exhibition and dates the manuscripts it displayed to the sixteenth through eighteenth centuries, describing them as covering "every aspect of human endeavor" and as evidence of the level of civilization attained by West Africans during the Middle Ages.

Read carefully, that is a caution as well as a celebration. The surviving libraries mostly post-date Musa by generations. They are excellent evidence for a long, continuous West African scholarly tradition in law, astronomy, medicine, grammar and theology; they are not direct documentation of his reign. Conflating the two is the single most common error in popular accounts.

Sankoré deserves the same care. It was a mosque around which teaching circles gathered, with scholars taking students and certifying them in particular texts — a real and sophisticated educational culture organised on its own terms rather than on those of a chartered European university. Applying the wrong institutional vocabulary to it invites a sceptic to knock down a claim nobody should have made.

The manuscript corpus is now the subject of substantial cataloguing and digitisation work, including at the Hill Museum & Manuscript Library, which holds Islamic manuscripts in Arabic, Persian, Turkish and Urdu from Africa and Asia and reports continuing work on Malian cataloguing. UNESCO's Memory of the World programme, running since 1995 with several hundred inscriptions, is the register on which documentary heritage of this kind is recorded. For the broader historiographical project of writing African history from African sources, UNESCO's eleven-volume *General History of Africa*, produced by more than 550 historians and specialists under a committee that was two-thirds African, is the standard reference point.
