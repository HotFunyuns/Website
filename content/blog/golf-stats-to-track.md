If you want to know where your strokes go, track six things on every hole: your score, whether you hit the fairway on par 4s and par 5s, whether you hit the green in regulation (GIR), how many putts you took, whether you got up and down after missing a green, and how many penalty strokes you added. Each takes a second to note, and together they show which part of your game is costing you.

What they cannot do is measure size. Each one is a yes-or-no count: it tells you that a fairway was found or a green was hit, not how much the shot helped. Measuring that is the job [strokes gained](/blog/strokes-gained-for-club-golfers/) was built for, and it is why the traditional stats work best read together rather than one at a time.

## A scorecard line you can actually keep

One row per hole is enough. A filled-in example:

| Hole | Par | Score | Fairway | GIR | Putts | Up and down? | Penalties |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7 | 4 | 5 | Missed left | No | 2 | Tried, failed | 0 |
| 8 | 3 | 3 | — | Yes | 2 | — | 0 |
| 9 | 5 | 7 | Yes | No | 2 | Tried, failed | 1 |

Notice what hole 9 shows that the score alone would hide. The drive found the fairway, the green was missed, there was a penalty stroke somewhere in between, and the short game did not rescue it. That is the start of a diagnosis rather than just a 7.

## Definitions, precisely

Golfers argue about these stats largely because they define them loosely. Here is what each one means and where the edges are.

### Green in regulation

A green is hit in regulation when any part of the ball is on the putting surface after a number of strokes at least **two fewer than par**: one stroke on a par 3, two on a par 4, three on a par 5. The "two fewer" assumes two putts, which is the same assumption built into par.

Three edge cases:

- **The fringe is not the green.** The Rules define the putting green's edge as where the specially prepared area visibly begins, and the fringe or collar is mown slightly higher than the green. A ball resting entirely on the fringe is not a green in regulation. Under Rule 13.1a of the 2023 Rules of Golf, a ball is on the putting green when any part of it touches the green, so a ball overlapping the edge does count.
- **Penalty strokes count.** Your score includes penalty strokes, so a par 3 where you found water off the tee, dropped and then hit the green has taken three strokes to get there. That is not a green in regulation.
- **Driving the green counts.** Reaching a par 4 in one, or a par 5 in two, is a green in regulation with strokes to spare.

### Fairway hit

A fairway is counted as hit when any part of the ball is touching the fairway after the tee shot on a **par 4 or par 5**. Par 3s are excluded, which is why a course with four par 3s offers only 14 fairway chances a round.

There is an oddity here worth knowing. "Fairway" is not one of the defined terms in the Rules of Golf: under Rule 2.2 the fairway and the rough are both simply the general area. "Fairway hit" is a convention that depends on where the greenkeeper mowed, not a Rules concept.

### Putts

The golf glossary defines a putt as a shot played on the green. Count the strokes you take once the ball is on the putting surface. A stroke from the fringe with a putter is not a putt under that definition; if you prefer to count it, do so every round, because consistency matters more than which convention you choose.

### Up and down, scrambling and sand saves

- **Up and down:** holing out in two strokes from off the green. The first shot gets the ball up onto the green and the putt gets it down.
- **Scrambling:** missing the green in regulation but still making par or better. Your scrambling percentage is the share of missed greens on which you manage it.
- **Sand save:** an up and down for par from a greenside bunker, which makes it a subset of scrambling.

Note the overlap: on a par 4 where you miss the green with your second, a chip and a putt for par is both an up and down and a scramble.

### Penalty strokes

Most stat sheets leave this one out, which is a mistake. A penalty stroke goes on your score without a shot being played, and it is the one stat that points straight at decisions rather than technique. Every penalty has a cause you can name: a tee shot aimed at the trouble, a carry that was never on, a ball lost in rough you chose to flirt with.

## What each stat hides

| Stat | What it records | What it hides | Read it with |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Green in regulation | Reached the putting surface in par minus two | How close: a tap-in and a putt from the far edge of the green count the same | Putts |
| Fairway hit | Tee shot finished on fairway grass | Distance, and how bad the misses were | Greens in regulation, penalties |
| Putts | Strokes taken on the green | Where the first putt started; missing greens and chipping close lowers it | Greens in regulation |
| Scrambling | Par or better after a missed green | Whether the chip or the putt did the work | Putts on scrambled holes |
| Sand saves | Up and down from greenside bunkers | How often you are in bunkers in the first place | Approach misses |
| Penalty strokes | Strokes added under the Rules | Nothing about technique; everything about choices | Fairways hit |

The putts row is the classic trap. A golfer who misses most greens and chips well will have low putt totals, because many of their putts start close. A golfer who hits most greens from a long way out will have higher putt totals while playing better golf. Putts per round, read alone, can rank those two the wrong way round.

## Reading the numbers together

Four patterns are worth recognising:

1. **Many greens hit, many putts.** Your approach shots are finding the green but not getting close, or your putting from distance needs work. Look at how far your first putts start.
2. **Few greens hit, few putts, decent scores.** Your short game is carrying you. Scrambling will be high, and the bigger gain is probably in approach play.
3. **Plenty of fairways, few greens.** The tee shot is not the problem. Approach distances, and whether you have a club for them, are the next thing to check; [club distance gapping](/blog/club-distance-gapping/) covers that.
4. **Few fairways and frequent penalties.** That is a strategy problem before it is a swing problem, and [golf course management basics](/blog/golf-course-management-basics/) is the place to start.

## How many rounds before a number means anything

Small samples swing hard. With 14 fairway chances, one extra fairway moves your percentage by about seven points (1 ÷ 14 is a little over 0.07). A single round can make a good driver look erratic or a poor one look steady. Treat any one round as a note, and look for patterns across several before changing your practice.

The same caution applies to your handicap. A Handicap Index averages the best 8 of your most recent 20 score differentials, not all of them, so it summarises your better golf rather than your typical round; [golf handicaps for beginners](/blog/golf-handicap-for-beginners/) explains why it moves the way it does. Stats explain where the strokes went; a handicap measures what you have shown you can score.

## Where strokes gained fits

The traditional stats are free and need nothing but a pencil. Their weakness is that they are yes-or-no and say nothing about how close to the hole each shot left you. Strokes gained values each shot by the change in position it produced, which needs a reference set of recorded shots that most club golfers do not have. The practical answer is to use both: let the counts tell you where to look, and ask the strokes-gained question about the shots you find there. [How golf scoring works](/blog/golf-scoring-explained/) covers the scoring terms these stats are built on.
