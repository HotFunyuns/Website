Delayed recall means being asked for something after a gap rather than immediately, and the gap has a name: the retention interval. It is the most consequential dial in short-term memory research, because what fills it changes the result far more than how long it lasts. An empty three seconds costs you almost nothing. Three seconds of counting backwards can cost you the whole item. This article explains why, and what that tells you about holding an answer in real life.

## The interval is the experiment

Immediate recall and delayed recall ask different questions. Immediate recall asks what you are still holding. Delayed recall asks what survived a gap you did not control.

The difference matters because the honest version of "how good is your memory" almost always involves a gap. You rarely get to answer the instant you finish encoding. You work out a total and then reach for your phone; you read a code and then walk to another room; you hold a partial product while you compute the next one. Every one of those is a retention interval with something in it.

Nelson Cowan's review of memory terminology is useful background here, since the whole reason short-term memory and working memory are separate terms is that some tasks impose work during the interval and others do not. We treat that split in detail in [our article on short-term versus working memory](/blog/short-term-versus-working-memory/), and the capacity underneath it in [the working memory guide](/blog/working-memory-and-training/).

## Filled and unfilled delays are not the same thing

Here is the finding that reorganises everything else. Berman, Jonides and Lewis, testing verbal short-term memory across different intervals, report that "both conditions with a filled interval led to worse memory performance, but the difficulty of the intervening task had no effect."

Read that second clause twice. It was not that hard interruptions hurt more than easy ones. **Occupation was what mattered, not effort.** A trivial verbal task during the gap did roughly as much damage as a demanding one.

The reason is the thing you are doing without noticing. The same authors observe that rehearsal "happens so habitually that it is often not noticed and is difficult to disengage." In an unfilled delay you are not idly waiting; you are quietly refreshing the material, which is why the delay feels easy. Fill the gap with anything that uses the same channel and the refreshing stops.

## The classic demonstration

The standard way to show this is the Brown-Peterson task. Hanover College's psychology department hosts a public demonstration of it, and their description is exactly the canonical one: a three-letter string and a three-digit number are presented, and during the retention interval the participant counts backwards by threes from that number. The instruction is explicit — focus on the counting and do not repeat the letter string — because the counting exists solely to block rehearsal.

The result is steep. As the demonstration notes, "as the retention interval increased, retention accuracy decreased over the first 15 seconds about until the results seemed to be at a chance level." Fifteen seconds of arithmetic can erase three letters you had no trouble with at the moment of presentation.

It is worth being careful about what that famous curve proves. Berman and colleagues observe that attributing the effect to a decay mechanism "is, however, suspect", because retroactive interference from the counting task plausibly contributed most of the forgetting. Their broader conclusion is that the mere passage of time does not cause forgetting in short-term memory. The curve is real; the standard interpretation of it is contested.

## What this changes about how you work

Three practical consequences follow, and none of them is "concentrate harder".

**Protect the gap, not the item.** If you have a number to carry from one place to another, the useful action is refusing to read, speak or listen during the transit. Effort spent trying to hold the item more firmly is less effective than effort spent keeping the channel clear.

**Expect the cost to be flat.** Because interruption difficulty barely mattered in the filled-interval comparison, a "quick glance" at something verbal is not a cheaper interruption than a long one. There is no small interruption.

**Write it down when the gap is unavoidable.** This is not defeatism. If the interval is going to be filled, an external record is the correct engineering solution, and the research is the reason why.

The classical account of the verbal store gives the mechanism behind all three: Robert Hughes describes the traditional model as a store holding phonological representations for around two seconds before decay, sustained by "an active articulatory control process that supports articulatory rehearsal." Occupy the articulatory process and you have removed the maintenance.

## What the app's delayed rounds are, and are not

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and it ships a delayed-answer drill that makes this concrete: you solve a problem, hold the result through a blank pause, and type it once the pause ends. Its memory difficulty levels also include a hold duration as a real mechanical parameter, running from no hold at the Beginner level up to a pause of more than a second at the hardest level.

That is a genuinely unusual dial to have exposed in a consumer app, and it is useful for feeling the difference this article describes — an unfilled pause of a second is trivial, and the same pause with a distraction in it is not. What the app does not have is any assessment. It runs no delayed recall test, produces no clinical measure, and makes no statement about what your performance means beyond the score, accuracy, streak and response-time figures it records for your own runs. Standardised memory assessment is something a qualified professional does with a validated instrument under controlled conditions, and a phone game is not a substitute for it in any direction.

Used as practice, the delayed rounds are a good corrective to the habit of answering instantly, which is what the timed sprints train.
