Most language courses are written for someone who has never heard the language. A heritage learner has heard nothing but the language for the first decade of their life, and then stopped. Those are not the same person, and pretending otherwise is why so many people who "grew up with" a language bounce off a beginner course within a fortnight.

This article is about what the heritage profile actually looks like, what the research literature means by the term, and how to build a study plan around an uneven starting point rather than an empty one.

## What "heritage learner" means

The term has a fairly settled definition and a deliberately fuzzy edge.

The definition most often cited in language education was formulated by Guadalupe Valdes at Stanford University and describes a student raised in a home where a non-majority language is spoken, who speaks or at least understands that language, and who is to some degree bilingual in it. A Heritage Briefs paper by Ann Kelleher of the University of California, Davis, published by the Center for Applied Linguistics, sets out that definition and explains why it is useful to teachers: it puts developed functional proficiency at the centre, which is the thing instruction actually has to work with.

The same paper is careful to note that this is not the only use of the term. In indigenous community contexts — the examples given are Navajo, Hawaiian and Arapaho — any member of the community studying the language may be considered a heritage learner, regardless of proficiency, including children from homes where the language is not spoken at all. There the instruction is oriented toward preservation and cultural transmission rather than toward closing a proficiency gap.

Both senses matter for a language like Shanghainese, because families in that situation are usually doing both things at once: an adult reconnecting with a language they half-hold, and a child being given one they do not hold yet.

The National Heritage Language Resource Center at UCLA, which researches the linguistic profiles and pedagogical needs of heritage speakers, frames the population plainly: a large share of households use a language other than the majority one at home, and the speakers who result often have native-like fluency in some respects while lacking literacy or formal register in the same language.

## The profile is uneven, not shallow

This is the part that generic advice gets wrong. A heritage learner is not "a beginner who is a bit ahead". The skills are distributed differently.

What is typically strong:

- **Listening.** Years of ambient exposure produce sound discrimination, rhythm and prosody that a classroom learner spends a long time acquiring.
- **Household and kinship vocabulary.** Food, family terms, weather, instructions, affection and telling-off.
- **Pragmatic instinct.** Knowing which register sounds rude without being able to explain why.

What is typically weak:

- **Production.** Recognising a sentence is a different operation from building one.
- **Literacy.** Reading and writing usually were not part of the home exposure at all.
- **Formal and public register.** The language of work, officialdom, argument and abstraction.
- **Explicit grammar.** Rules were absorbed, not taught, so they are unavailable for correction.
- **Vocabulary breadth outside the home.** Anything the household did not talk about.

Two failure modes follow. Starting at unit one bores you through the listening material and leaves you underprepared for the script. Skipping to intermediate assumes literacy you never built. The correct answer is neither: **audit each skill separately and rejoin the path in a different place for each one.**

## Why Shanghainese is a particularly sharp case

A heritage learner of Shanghainese usually carries a second complication: they know some Mandarin, and they expect it to help more than it does.

Glottolog classifies Shanghainese as a dialect reached through a chain of Sinitic groupings — Wu, Wu Chinese, Taihu, Suzhou — rather than as a variety of Mandarin. Shared characters and a large shared vocabulary stock mean meaning often transfers. Pronunciation largely does not, and the tone behaviour is genuinely different in kind rather than in detail.

The site's existing piece on [whether Shanghainese is a dialect or a language](/blog/is-shanghainese-a-dialect-or-a-language/) works through the classification question and why the answer is partly political. For the purposes of a study plan, the operative fact is narrower: treating Shanghainese as accented Mandarin produces a learner who is understood by nobody and frustrated quickly.

The other sharp edge is tone sandhi — the way tones change when syllables run together. Shanghainese relies on it heavily, which means a heritage learner who can hear correct Shanghainese but has never produced it will find that word-by-word practice sounds wrong even when every individual word is right. [Shanghainese tones and tone sandhi](/blog/shanghainese-tones-and-tone-sandhi/) covers the mechanism, and it is the single highest-value thing for a heritage learner to work on early, because it is the gap between recognition and production.

## Building the plan

A practical sequence for someone with the heritage profile:

1. **Test your listening honestly.** Not against a family member who slows down for you — against natural-speed material. This tells you how much of your advantage is real.
2. **Start writing at unit one and mean it.** Script and romanisation are the part where you are a genuine beginner, and the fastest area to make visible progress.
3. **Convert recognition into production deliberately.** Say the things you already understand, out loud, before adding new vocabulary. This is the bottleneck, not vocabulary count.
4. **Work tone behaviour before word lists.** In a language with heavy sandhi, learning more isolated words builds a bigger wrong-sounding vocabulary.
5. **Fill the register gaps on purpose.** Household vocabulary is covered; work, transport, health and officialdom are usually not.
6. **Use family as input, not as instruction.** Relatives are excellent exposure and unreliable teachers, because fluent speakers rarely know the rules they follow.
