A lecture moves at the lecturer's speed, and notes taken in the room always miss something. A recording catches everything, and a transcript makes that everything usable: searchable, skimmable and easy to turn into revision material. This guide covers recording a lecture properly, transcribing it, and what to do with the transcript afterwards, with an eye on what a major review of study techniques found.

## Step 1: get permission

Before recording, check the rules. Your institution and your lecturer may each have a policy on recording, and your course may already provide an official recording. Recording without permission can breach those policies, and recording laws vary by place. Ask the lecturer or check the course information first, and use recordings only for your own study unless told otherwise.

## Step 2: record from the right place

Wikipedia's article on speech recognition notes that speech is distorted by **background noise, echoes and recording characteristics**, and a large room can add all three: distance from the speaker, hard surfaces and fan noise. Google's Live Transcribe help gives the basic remedy for any recording: keep the microphone **near the person speaking**.

- **Sit near the front**, close to the lecturer or the room's speaker, away from the projector.
- **Put the phone on something soft** so desk knocks do not reach the microphone.
- **Silence notifications** and turn on aeroplane mode if it does not affect the recorder.
- **Check after five minutes** that the recording is actually running and audible.

## How long the transcript will be

Wikipedia's article on words per minute cites **100 to 125 words a minute** as a comfortable pace for slide presentations, and 150 to 160 for audiobooks.

**A worked example.** A one-hour lecture at 100 to 125 words a minute produces roughly **6,000 to 7,500 words**, a lot to reread before every exam. That is why the transcript is the start of studying, not the end.

## Step 3: transcribe and correct the key terms

Transcribe the recording, then correct the words that matter most, not every "um". Lectures can be full of **technical terms, names and formulas**, and Wikipedia notes that recognition error rates rise as the vocabulary grows larger. Check them against your slides, the reading list or the audio. A transcript where "mitosis" became "my toes is" is a transcript you cannot search.

## Step 4: turn the transcript into study material

A 2013 review of ten study techniques by Dunlosky and colleagues rated **practice testing** and **distributed practice** as high utility, and **rereading**, **summarisation** and **highlighting** as low utility, though it notes summarisation helps some students on some tasks. So build the transcript into things you can test yourself with:

1. **Questions**: turn key points into questions you can answer later without looking, and come back to them over several days.
2. **A term list**: every new term with a one-line definition and where it came up.
3. **Short section summaries** if they help you, in your own words, as a map rather than the main method.
4. **Gaps**: anything still unclear becomes a question for the lecturer or tutor.

The listing for our transcription app describes this use: reviewing recorded explanations alongside lecture notes and picking out unfamiliar terms or points to ask about.

## Line the transcript up with the slides

Lectures make most sense when words and visuals sit together. As you correct the transcript, add the slide number at each change, such as "[slide 14]", so every explanation points to its diagram or equation. Where the lecturer says "as you can see here" or "this one", note what "this" refers to while it is fresh; a transcript full of unexplained pointers is hard to revise from weeks later.

## Use timestamps to jump back

Mark the transcript with times at each new topic, so a confusing section is one tap away in the recording; [adding timestamps to a transcript](/blog/how-to-add-timestamps-to-a-transcript/) explains how often and in what format. For study, a clean transcript without fillers is easier to read than a strictly verbatim one; [verbatim versus clean verbatim](/blog/verbatim-vs-clean-transcription/) explains the difference.

## Live captions in the room

If following speech in real time is the difficulty, live captioning may help alongside recording. Google's Live Transcribe, available on Pixel phones and some other Android phones, shows speech as text as it happens; its help says it needs an internet connection unless you use one of its offline languages. If you need formal accessibility support, ask your institution what it offers.
