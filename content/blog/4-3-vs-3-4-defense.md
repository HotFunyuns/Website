A 4-3 defence lines up four down linemen with three linebackers behind them. A 3-4 lines up three down linemen with four linebackers. Both keep four defensive backs, so the whole difference sits in the front seven. The NFL's own Football Operations explainer reduces it to where the pass rush comes from: in a 4-3 it "is primarily generated by the down linemen", while in a 3-4 it "comes primarily from the linebackers as the defensive linemen occupy multiple offensive linemen."

That makes the choice less a question of which front is better than of which players you need. A 4-3 asks for linemen who win their own matchups. A 3-4 asks for linemen who soak up blockers and for linebackers who can reach the quarterback. And the base front is only part of the picture: when an offence adds receivers, a defence can trade a front-seven player for an extra defensive back.

## The two fronts, side by side

| | 4-3 | 3-4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Down linemen | Four: two ends, two tackles | Three |
| Linebackers | Three | Four |
| Defensive backs | Four | Four |
| Main source of the pass rush | The down linemen | The linebackers |
| What the linemen are there to do | Win their own blocks and get into the backfield | Occupy more than one blocker each |

The position names come most naturally from the 4-3. The league's glossary describes ends lined up at each outside end of the line, trying to force their way into the backfield to pressure the quarterback or stop the running back, and two tackles set opposite the guards. Behind them, three linebackers stand three to five yards off the ball with separate jobs. The middle, or "mike", relays the call and sets the alignment. The strong-side "sam" lines up opposite the tight end. The weak-side "will" pursues from the other side and contains runs away from the tight end.

The 3-4 rearranges the same seven players. In the league's diagram, three linemen sit across the middle and four linebackers stand in one row behind them, with the outside pair set wider than the defensive ends. That is where an edge rush has to start, and it matches the league's description of a 3-4 whose pass rush comes mainly from linebackers rather than linemen.

## What each front asks of a roster

| Roster question | 4-3 | 3-4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where the premium rusher plays | Defensive end, on the line | Outside linebacker, off the edge |
| What interior linemen must do | Penetrate and make plays themselves | Hold ground against two blockers so others are free |
| What linebackers must do | Three distinct jobs: call, tight end, pursuit | Rush the passer as well as tackle and cover |
| Starters by unit | 4 linemen, 3 linebackers | 3 linemen, 4 linebackers |
| Hardest player to replace | A lineman who wins one-on-one | A linebacker who can rush and still drop into coverage |

The table hides the consequence that matters most: the same athlete changes value when the front changes. A 4-3 end's job, in the glossary's words, is to pressure the quarterback or stop the running back. Move a similar player to a 3-4 and he becomes a linebacker, and the linebacker job description adds dropping into pass coverage. A rusher who cannot do that is a narrower asset in a 3-4 than his rushing alone suggests.

The reverse applies inside. Much of a 3-4 lineman's work is invisible on a stat sheet, because occupying two blockers frees someone else to make the tackle. Judging those players by their own tackles and sacks undervalues them in exactly the front that needs them most. A 4-3 tackle, by contrast, is expected to make some of those plays himself.

None of this makes one front cheaper. A 4-3 concentrates its difficulty in four linemen who must each win; a 3-4 concentrates it in the interior lineman who can absorb a double team and the outside linebackers who rush. [Football positions explained for drafting](/blog/football-positions-explained-for-drafting/) covers what each position does in general; the point here is that the front decides which of those positions is scarce.

## Base fronts and the nickel package

Both fronts are base defences: what a team plays when the offence shows the personnel it was built against. When the offence adds wide receivers, the league's explainer says teams may use a nickel package, which replaces a linebacker or defensive lineman with an extra defensive back. The glossary describes the same move from the other direction: a fifth defensive back brought on to cover receivers, usually at the expense of a linebacker.

That is why the 4-3 versus 3-4 argument settles less than it seems to. The more often an opponent spreads the field with extra receivers, the more of the game a defence plays in a nickel package, and the less often either base front is on the field at all. The choice still matters, because the nickel is built from the same players. If the extra defensive back usually comes on at the expense of a linebacker, as the glossary says, a 4-3 can keep its four-man line intact, while a 3-4 has to choose among the linebackers it relies on for its rush.

Labels describe personnel, not alignment. A linebacker can stand on the line of scrimmage like a lineman, and an outside linebacker in a 3-4 often rushes from the edge, so a four-man line and a three-man line with an edge rusher can look much the same from the stands.

## What changes for a GM

For anyone building a roster, real or simulated, the front is a budget decision before it is a tactical one.

1. **Choose the front before you rank players.** It decides whether your premium rushers come from the defensive-line pool or the linebacker pool. Drafting the wrong kind leaves an expensive player out of position.
2. **Budget for the fifth defensive back.** Against offences that use extra receivers, the nickel package puts him on the field in place of a linebacker or lineman, so a player who only fits the base front can end up closer to part-time than his depth-chart line suggests. [Depth charts explained](/blog/depth-charts-explained/) applies the same playing-time logic to backups.
3. **Count each rusher once.** A 4-3 end and a 3-4 outside linebacker can be the same athlete. Pencilling him into both plans double-counts your pass rush.
4. **Spend where the front concentrates its difficulty.** In a 4-3 that means linemen who win alone; in a 3-4, the interior lineman who survives double teams and the linebackers who rush. Paying for the visible statistics instead of the scarce job is the classic overspend.
