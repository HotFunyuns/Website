Great Zimbabwe was built by Shona-speaking Iron Age communities, over roughly three and a half centuries, between about 1100 and 1450 CE. UNESCO's inscription states this flatly: scientific research has proved that the site was founded in the eleventh century by a Bantu population of the Iron Age, the Shona, on ground only sparsely inhabited before. Zimbabwe's own National Museums and Monuments describes it the same way.

The question gets asked anyway, and the reason it gets asked is part of the history.

## What is actually there

The monument sits about 30 kilometres from Masvingo, at roughly 1,100 metres, and the property extends over almost 800 hectares. UNESCO divides it into three groups.

The **Hill Ruins** occupy a granite mass and were continuously inhabited from the eleventh to the fifteenth centuries, with layered traces of settlement throughout. Rough granite rubble blocks form enclosures reached by narrow, partly covered passageways. This acropolis is generally read as a royal city: the west enclosure as the residence of successive chiefs, the east enclosure — where six steatite posts topped with carved birds were found — as ritual space.

The **Great Enclosure** lies south of the hills, dates to the fourteenth century, and is the structure in every photograph. It is elliptical, built of cut granite blocks laid in regular courses, and contains daga-hut living quarters, a community area, and a narrow passage leading to a high conical tower. Daga is a building material made from granitic sand and clay; huts stood inside the stone walls, with internal walls marking off each family's area.

The **Valley Ruins** are a series of living ensembles across the valley, and UNESCO dates them to the nineteenth century — much later than the rest. That single detail should shape how you read the site: it was not built once and then left.

The masonry carries chevron and chequered decoration executed to a high standard. Zimbabwe's National Museums and Monuments records that Great Zimbabwe is the largest of more than 300 dry-stone-walled structures of this type across Zimbabwe and southern Africa. It is the biggest example of a widespread regional tradition, not a unique anomaly — and that fact alone disposes of any argument that it must have been imported.

## The attribution problem, stated plainly

UNESCO's own short description opens by calling the ruins "the capital of the Queen of Sheba, according to an age-old legend". That a World Heritage dossier still has to mention the legend is a measure of how durable it has been.

The outsider-origin story — Phoenicians, Arabs, Sabaeans, anyone but the people living there — was not a neutral error. A colonial administration in southern Africa had an obvious stake in the conclusion that Africans had not built the largest stone structures on the subcontinent, and that conclusion survived in official and popular accounts long after excavation pointed the other way. The correction came from stratigraphy, from dated material in sealed contexts, and from the regional distribution of the same building tradition.

It is worth being precise about what settled it, because "archaeologists disagreed with the colonists" is too vague. The evidence is the same kind described in [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/): datable imports in undisturbed layers, radiocarbon from occupation deposits, and a construction sequence that can be read directly from the walls.

## The economy: local production, long-distance trade

For a long time the interpretation ran one way — Great Zimbabwe mattered because gold and ivory flowed out of it to the coast. Recent archaeological science has made the internal picture much richer.

New excavations at the Eastern Ridge Ruins and adjacent areas recovered fragments of over a hundred gold-processing vessels, both reused pottery and purpose-made crucibles, from stratified contexts. Compositional analysis showed these technical ceramics were used to refine and collect gold at high temperature, most likely producing not only standardised ingots but finished objects. The gold prills set in the crucible slag are consistent with natural, unalloyed gold, and the variability in silver levels points to several different sources.

The authors' conclusion is the interesting part: local agency and local gold consumption were much more significant than generally assumed, with production occurring in homesteads throughout the settlement rather than being concentrated for export. They end with an explicit warning against oversimplified narratives of globalisation.

The external connections are nonetheless real and well evidenced. UNESCO records excavated glass beads, porcelain from China and Persia, and gold and Arab coins from Kilwa. Work published in 2025 pushes the timeline back further: Persian Gulf ceramic sherds and Asian glass beads recovered from the Letaba region of north-eastern South Africa show communities up to 400 kilometres inland already participating in Indian Ocean Rim trade by the tenth century, with the distribution of such ceramics concentrated along the Limpopo's main tributaries.

This is a different network from the Eurasian overland routes described in [the Silk Road for beginners](/blog/the-silk-road-for-beginners/), operating on monsoon sailing rather than caravan staging, and it deserves its own name rather than a borrowed one.

## Why the capital emptied

UNESCO's reconstruction is specific. Around 1450 the capital was abandoned because the hinterland could no longer furnish food for the overpopulated city and because of deforestation. The resulting migration benefited Khami, which became the most influential city in the region — though UNESCO reads that shift as a sign of waning political power rather than a simple transfer.

Two cautions. First, this is a reconstruction from environmental and archaeological evidence, not a recorded event; nobody wrote down why people left. Second, the richer picture of internal gold production described above suggests the site's economy was less dependent on a single export relationship than older models assumed, which matters for any account of why it failed.

Readers who want the general form of this argument will recognise it from [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/): when a field offers several partial explanations and no decisive one, the responsible output is the set of competing accounts with their evidence attached. Environmental strain, political fragmentation and shifting trade routes are not rival theories so much as three pressures that plausibly operated together.

## What came after: Torwa, Khami and Mutapa

The story does not stop when the famous walls empty.

Khami, 22 kilometres from Bulawayo, became the capital of the Torwa dynasty, which UNESCO dates to 1450–1650 and describes as arising from the collapse of the Great Zimbabwe kingdom. It was abandoned during Ndebele incursions in the nineteenth century. Architecturally it is a development rather than a copy: revetments and retaining walls appear there for the first time in the sub-region's building history, and Khami still has the longest decorated wall anywhere in the area.

UNESCO places the whole sequence in a chain — initiated at Mapungubwe in what is now South Africa, extending to Great Zimbabwe, and continuing through the later states. The imported material at Khami includes fifteenth- and seventeenth-century Spanish porcelain, Rhineland stoneware and Ming porcelain, much of it displayed at the Museum of Natural History in Bulawayo.

The other successor is the one most readers have heard of under a garbled name. When the Portuguese settled at Sofala in 1505, UNESCO records, the region was divided between the rival powers of the kingdoms of Torwa and Mwene-Mutapa. European writers rendered that second name as Monomotapa and built a good deal of fantasy on top of it; the underlying state was a real regional power in a landscape with more than one.
