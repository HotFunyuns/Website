In a survival block-building game, the first real project is a base. It does not need to be beautiful. It needs to keep you safe, hold what you gather and give you somewhere to come back to. This guide covers how to plan one, drawing on Wikipedia's descriptions of survival and sandbox games, with Minecraft, which Wikipedia calls the most successful example of a sandbox game, as the reference point.

## What a first base is for

Wikipedia's article on sandbox games describes Minecraft at its core as a survival game in which players collect resources to build tools and **construct shelters to protect them from hostile creatures**. Its article on survival games adds that players start with crude shelters for protection and, as they gather more resources and survive more readily, go on to build massive structures. A starter base, then, has three jobs:

1. **Shelter**: a safe place when the world turns dangerous.
2. **Storage**: somewhere to keep what you gather.
3. **A return point**: a fixed place to come back to between trips.

Everything else can wait.

## Choosing a site

Before placing a block, think about the first few trips. Build close to the resources you will need most at the start, usually wood and stone, so early runs are short. Choose ground you can recognise from a distance, so that you can find your way back after exploring. Flat ground is quicker to build on than a slope, while building into a hillside can save on wall blocks. None of these is a rule; they are trade-offs between time, materials and safety.

## Sizing the first shelter

Small is the right size for a first base, because every block must be gathered. A useful benchmark:

| Part | Blocks |
| --- | --- |
| Walls: a 5 × 5 square, 16 blocks per layer, three layers | 48 |
| Flat roof over the 5 × 5 square | 25 |
| Minus a doorway two blocks high | −2 |
| **Total** | **71** |

That leaves a 3 × 3 floor inside, enough for a few storage spots and a place to craft. If materials are short, a 4 × 4 square with three-block walls takes 12 blocks per layer, 36 for the walls and 16 for the roof: 52 blocks, or 50 with the doorway, for a 2 × 2 interior.

Size pays off later. A 7 × 7 hut built the same way takes 24 blocks per wall layer, so 72 for the walls and 49 for the roof: 119 blocks with the doorway. That is about 1.7 times the materials of the 5 × 5 hut for a 5 × 5 interior, almost three times the floor space. Bigger bases give more floor per block, which is why expanding makes sense once gathering is easy.

## Storage from day one

Plan storage early. Whatever you cannot carry on a trip has to live somewhere, so a base is also a warehouse. Group things by type, such as building blocks, tools and food, so you can find them when you return in a hurry.

## Light and the night

In Minecraft, Wikipedia explains, monsters spawn in darker areas outside a radius around the player, depending on the difficulty setting, which is why players build shelter to get through the night. That makes **light** part of base design there: a lit interior and lit surroundings make the area around a base safer. Not every block-building game uses the same rules, so check how monsters appear in the game you are playing before relying on any one trick.

## Growing in stages

Once shelter and storage work, expand in steps:

1. **Shelter**: the first hut.
2. **Workshop**: room to craft and store more.
3. **Projects**: the larger builds that, Wikipedia notes, often model real or fictional buildings.

Each stage should pay for the next: gathering trips made easier by a better base bring back the materials to improve it further. [How crafting systems work](/blog/how-crafting-systems-work/) explains why that loop is so compelling.
