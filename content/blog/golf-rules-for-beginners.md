The Rules of Golf run to 25 Rules and more than 70 defined terms, but a beginner's round is decided by a small slice of them. Play the ball where it lies and the course as you find it. Count every stroke and every penalty yourself. Know which situations cost a stroke — taking relief from a penalty area, declaring a ball unplayable, and losing a ball or hitting it out of bounds, which costs you the distance as well. Know when relief is free. And keep moving. This guide covers that slice in roughly the order you will need it.

It follows the 2023 edition of the Rules of Golf, written jointly by The R&A and the USGA, which is still the edition in force on 2 October 2026; The R&A has said the next edition will be published on 1 January 2028. Rule numbers below refer to the 2023 edition.

## Two principles, and you are the referee

Rule 1.1 states the game in two lines: for each stroke you play the course as you find it and play the ball as it lies. Almost everything else in the book is either a restatement of those principles or a defined exception to them — places where you may improve conditions, such as repairing a pitch-mark on the green, or play from somewhere other than where the ball lies, such as when taking relief.

That gives a beginner a dependable default. If you do not know whether you may move something, or move the ball, assume you may not, play it as it lies and ask afterwards.

The second principle is responsibility. Under Rule 1.3b, players apply the Rules to themselves and are expected to recognise their own breaches. A player who knows they have incurred a penalty and deliberately leaves it off is disqualified, and so are players who agree to ignore a Rule they know applies. Elite events have referees; at a club, your own honesty is the referee.

## Three sizes of penalty

Rule 1.3c(2) sets three levels. Knowing which is which tells you whether a mistake is a nuisance or the end of your round.

| Level | When it applies | Typical beginner examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One penalty stroke | A minor potential advantage, or taking penalty relief to play from somewhere else | Relief from a penalty area; an unplayable ball; causing your ball at rest to move |
| General penalty | Most breaches: two strokes in stroke play, loss of the hole in match play | Playing a wrong ball; touching the sand right behind your ball in a bunker |
| Disqualification | Serious misconduct, or an advantage too large for the score to stand | Returning a hole score lower than you took; failing to hole out and not correcting it |

Penalty strokes go on your score for the hole. They are not optional, and they do not depend on whether anyone saw.

## Where the ball lies decides which Rules apply

The Rules divide the course into five areas. The general area is everything outside the other four, so "fairway" and "rough" are both simply general area as far as the Rules are concerned.

| Area | What changes there |
| --- | --- |
| Teeing area | The ball may be teed. The area is two club-lengths deep behind the front of the tee-markers, and in stroke play playing from outside it costs two strokes and must be corrected (Rule 6.1b). |
| General area | The default Rules apply, including free relief from abnormal course conditions and for a ball embedded in its own pitch-mark. |
| Bunkers | You may remove loose impediments such as leaves, but you must not touch the sand with your club right in front of or behind the ball, in a practice swing or in your backswing (Rule 12.2b). |
| Penalty areas | Marked red or yellow. Play the ball as it lies, or take relief outside for one stroke (Rule 17). |
| Putting green | You may mark, lift and clean your ball, repair ball-marks and spike marks, and putt with the flagstick in or out. Accidentally moving your ball there costs nothing. |

## Six situations every new golfer meets

### The ball is in water or behind red or yellow stakes

That is a penalty area. You may play the ball as it lies, with no special restrictions on how you do so (Rule 17.1b), or take relief outside the area for one stroke. A yellow area gives two relief options: replay from where you last played, or drop on a line going straight back from the hole through the point where the ball last crossed the edge. A red area adds a third: a drop within two club-lengths of that crossing point, no nearer the hole. Water the course has not marked counts as red.

### You cannot find it, or it went past the white stakes

A ball is lost if it is not found within three minutes of starting to search, and it is out of bounds only when all of it lies outside the boundary edge, which should be marked with white stakes or lines. Either way the remedy is stroke and distance: one penalty stroke, and you play again from where you hit the previous shot (Rule 18.2). A tee shot out of bounds means your next stroke from the tee is your third.

The time-saver is a provisional ball. If your shot might be lost outside a penalty area or out of bounds, tell someone you are playing a "provisional" and hit another before walking forward. Some clubs also adopt Model Local Rule E-5, which lets you drop near the fairway for two penalty strokes instead of walking back. It is intended for casual rounds and golfers' own competitions, and the R&A's guidance says it is not appropriate for professional or elite amateur events, so check the local rules before relying on it.

### It is somewhere you cannot play it from

Anywhere except a penalty area, you alone may declare your ball unplayable (Rule 19). For one penalty stroke you may replay from where you last played, drop back on a line from the hole through the ball, or drop within two club-lengths of the ball, no nearer the hole. In a bunker the two dropping options must stay in the bunker, unless you pay two strokes in total to drop back on the line outside it.

### A cart path, sprinkler head or puddle is in the way

Paved and other artificially surfaced paths, sprinkler heads, puddles, ground under repair and animal holes are abnormal course conditions, and Rule 16.1 gives free relief when the ball lies in or on one or it physically interferes with your stance or swing. In the general area, find the nearest point where that interference ends, no nearer the hole, and drop within one club-length of it. Boundary stakes are not obstructions, so there is no free relief from them.

### The ball is plugged in its own pitch-mark

If part of the ball is below the level of the ground in the general area, Rule 16.3 gives free relief: drop within one club-length of the spot right behind it, no nearer the hole. Some courses restrict this relief by Local Rule, for example to areas cut to fairway height or less.

### You hit the wrong ball, or moved your own

Playing a ball that is not yours costs the general penalty in stroke play, and you must go back and play your own (Rule 6.3c) — the reason experienced players put an identifying mark on theirs. Causing your own ball at rest to move costs one stroke and the ball goes back (Rule 9.4b), but there is no penalty when it happens accidentally while you are searching for it or on the putting green.

Whenever a Rule tells you to drop, the definition is specific: let go from knee height so the ball falls straight down, without throwing or spinning it.

## Your bag and your scorecard

**Fourteen clubs at most.** Rule 4.1b limits you to 14 clubs during a round. In stroke play the penalty is two strokes for each hole where the breach happened, capped at four for the round. How to fill those slots sensibly is covered in [club distance gapping](/blog/club-distance-gapping/).

**Hole out, and get the card right.** In regular stroke play you must hole out on every hole (Rule 3.3c). After the round your marker certifies your hole scores and you certify and return the card (Rule 3.3b). Return a hole score higher than you took and it stands; return one lower and you are disqualified, unless the only error was a penalty you genuinely did not know about. Adding up the total is the Committee's job, not yours. [How golf scoring works](/blog/golf-scoring-explained/) explains what the numbers mean once they are on the card.

## Ready golf and pace of play

Rule 5.6b says a round is meant to be played at a prompt pace. It recommends making each stroke within 40 seconds of being able to play, adds that most players should usually be quicker, and encourages groups to let faster groups through.

"Ready golf" is the stroke-play permission in Rule 6.4b(2) to play out of turn in a safe and responsible way — when you are ready and the player farther away is not, for instance, or when someone wants to finish a very short putt. It only works if nobody is endangered, distracted or interfered with, and if the player whose turn it is wants to go first, the others should generally wait. Match play is different: there, an opponent may cancel a stroke played out of turn unless they agreed to it (Rule 6.4a).

## Etiquette is written into Rule 1

Golf's etiquette is not a separate code. Rule 1.2a expects every player to act with integrity; to show consideration for others, which includes prompt pace, watching out for safety, not distracting other players and an immediate shout of "fore" if a ball might hit someone; and to take care of the course by replacing divots, smoothing bunkers and repairing ball-marks. Falling short carries no stroke penalty under the Rules themselves, but a Committee may disqualify a player for serious misconduct, and a club may add penalties through a Code of Conduct adopted as a Local Rule (Rule 1.2b).

## A friendlier format for your first competitions

Regular stroke play makes every blow-up hole count in full. Maximum Score (Rule 21.2) caps each hole at a figure the Committee sets — such as twice par, a fixed number or net double bogey — and players are encouraged to pick up once they reach it, so a lost ball or two cannot wreck the round or hold up the course. Stableford does something similar with points, and [golf tournament formats](/blog/golf-tournament-formats/) compares the two. If you start keeping a handicap, the Rules of Handicapping cap hole scores too: at par plus five while you are establishing a Handicap Index, and at net double bogey once you have one. [Golf handicaps for beginners](/blog/golf-handicap-for-beginners/) explains how the handicap itself is worked out.
