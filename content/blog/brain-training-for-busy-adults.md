Ten minutes is enough for a practice session that does something, as long as you spend it on four different activities rather than ten minutes of the same drill. The route below takes about that long, uses only things that exist in one app, and claims only the narrow thing practice actually delivers: you get better at what you practise.

It will not raise your intelligence, protect you from anything, or spill over into skills you never trained. Four things are worth keeping apart before you start. **Practice** is repeating a task to get better at that task. **Entertainment** is playing because it is enjoyable. **Educational activity** is learning a method you did not previously know. **Standardised psychological assessment** is a normed instrument administered under controlled conditions by a qualified professional. A ten-minute routine is the first three at once. It is never the fourth.

## Why the unit is ten minutes

Short sessions are not a compromise forced on busy people; they are close to what the scheduling evidence supports. The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide on organising instruction lists "space learning over time" among its recommendations, and the Bjork Learning and Forgetting Lab at UCLA describes distributing repetitions across time as one of the most robust results in cognitive psychology. Both point the same way: ten minutes on six days beats an hour on one.

The second reason is more practical. Arithmetic decay in adults is rarely a knowledge problem. National Numeracy defines numeracy as the confidence to use basic maths at work and in everyday life, and notes that many adults avoid maths out of low confidence rather than inability. Ten minutes is short enough that you will actually do it, and doing it is most of the battle. At population level, adult numeracy is surveyed by large studies such as PIAAC, run in the United States by the National Center for Education Statistics — a useful reminder that measuring a skill is a separate discipline from practising one.

## The four-part route

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and the slots below map onto modes it actually ships. The shape of the routine matters more than the product, though; any app with the same four ingredients will do.

### Minutes 1–2: one timed sprint on your worst operation

Pick the operation you least want to do. Run one 60-second sprint — Multiplication Rush, Addition Sprint, Subtraction Sprint or Division Dash — at a difficulty you can hold, not the hardest one on the list.

Two minutes of clock pressure is the whole point of this slot. It separates the facts you genuinely know from the ones you quietly reconstruct, because reconstruction is what runs out of time first. Do not chase the score here. The sprint is a diagnostic.

### Minutes 3–5: one accuracy set, no clock

Switch to Accuracy Trainer: ten questions, no timer, feedback and an explanation after each answer, hints available. This is the slot that teaches. Having just found the thing you are slow at, work ten questions on it without a clock pushing you to guess.

If you do only one part of this routine on a bad day, do this one.

### Minutes 6–8: one technique, read and used

Open the technique library, read one method end to end, then take its two-question mini quiz. Each entry carries steps, a worked example, a note on what it is best for, and a named common mistake — and that last field is the useful part, because most half-remembered shortcuts fail on exactly one case.

Choose a method that matches the sprint you just ran. Slow on the nines? Read the method for multiplying by 9 and 99 and watch what it does with a carry. Bogged down in borrowing? Read the counting-up approach to subtraction. Our [mental math practice guide](/blog/mental-math-training-guide/) groups these methods into three underlying moves — round and compensate, break apart and recombine, swap the operation for an easier equivalent — which makes them far easier to hold than a bag of unrelated tricks.

### Minutes 9–10: one memory round set

Finish with one short memory round set — number recall, a sequence, a grid, or a delayed-recall round. It uses a different kind of attention from arithmetic, which is part of why it works as a closer: you are not asking the same tired system for one more effort.

The seven memory difficulty levels change real mechanics rather than a score multiplier — how many items appear, how large the grid is, how long the reveal lasts — so a level that feels impossible is genuinely a different task, not the same task marked harshly.

## The order is not arbitrary

Sprint, then accuracy set, then method, then memory. The sprint finds the weakness while you are freshest; the accuracy set repairs it while the miss is still in mind; the technique gives you something to carry into tomorrow; the memory round ends the session on a task that does not feel like more of the same.

Running it the other way round — method first, sprint last — tends to produce a pleasant reading session followed by two rushed minutes, which is a different and much less useful activity. If your sessions have stopped producing anything, that ordering problem is worth ruling out before you conclude the practice itself has stalled.

## Judging whether it is working

Hold the mode and the difficulty constant, then watch accuracy before score. Score is a composite: it moves when the difficulty label moves, and a bigger number at an easier setting is not improvement. Accuracy at a fixed difficulty is the honest line, and average response time is the second one.

Expect noise. A single bad session means you were tired, not that you regressed.
