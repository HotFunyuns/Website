Arguments about contested historical figures are usually four arguments happening at once. What did the person actually do? What was known and objected to at the time? What does the current evidence show? And whom should a society choose to honour now? Separating those four makes most of these disputes legible, because people who appear to disagree about history often agree about it and disagree about commemoration. This article is about how to tell them apart. What the evidence for an individual life can and cannot establish is covered in [how we know what historical figures actually did](/blog/how-we-know-what-historical-figures-did/).

## Question 1: What did the person do?

This is an evidential question and it frequently has firm answers. Enslavement, deportation, massacre and famine policy leave records — shipping ledgers, court proceedings, correspondence, censuses, company accounts — and the existence of a public argument about a figure does not make those records uncertain.

It is worth being blunt about this, because the two get conflated. A society arguing over a statue is not thereby arguing over whether the documented acts occurred. Treating heated commemoration debates as evidence that the underlying history is unsettled is a category error, and it tends to work in one direction only.

The professional standard is clear on the limit. The American Historical Association requires historians to "honor the integrity of the historical record" and warns that "the desire to score points as an advocate should never tempt a historian to misrepresent the historical record." That applies to defenders and critics alike.

## Question 2: What was contested at the time?

"You cannot judge the past by today's standards" is offered as though it closed the conversation. It is better treated as a testable claim, because contemporaries very often did object, and their objections survive.

Where they do, they are evidence of two things: that alternatives were conceivable within the period, and that the decision was made in the knowledge that others disagreed. Where they do not survive, that absence is itself worth explaining — records can be missing because objection was absent, or because objectors had no access to the page.

This is the reconstruction work that makes explanation possible, and it is genuinely incompatible with judging from hindsight. If you want to know why a decision was made, you need the options as they appeared then.

## Question 3: What does the evidence now show?

Interpretations move because evidence moves. Archives open, provenance research is funded, previously excluded scholars ask different questions, and reputations built on a narrow evidence base do not always survive a wider one.

Institutional examples are easiest to check because the institutions publish their own reasoning. The Rijksmuseum has published material on slavery built around ten personal histories from Brazil, Suriname, the Caribbean, South Africa and Asia — enslaved people, enslavers, beneficiaries and abolitionists — and frames the subject as "an inextricable part of our history". The Royal Museum for Central Africa in Tervuren publishes research on the provenance of its collections and has mounted exhibitions examining colonial-era image-making, including one presenting the 1913 Congo Panorama as colonial illusion.

Who is doing the research matters as well. The Royal Historical Society's 2018 report on race, ethnicity and equality in UK history found that 96.1 per cent of UK-national university historians were White, with Black historians under one per cent of university-based history staff, and argued that curriculum and workforce reinforce one another. A discipline's composition shapes the questions it thinks to ask, which shapes which legacies come up for reassessment at all.

## Question 4: Whom should we commemorate?

This is the only one of the four that is not a question about the past. A statue, a building name or a banknote is a present-tense decision about public honour, made by living institutions, and it is settled politically rather than evidentially.

Historians can inform it — by establishing the record, by describing how and when the commemoration was created and by whom — without that expertise settling it. Noting that a monument was erected decades after the person died, during a particular political moment, is a historical finding. Deciding what to do about it is not.

Keeping question four separate from question one is the single most useful move available in these arguments, because it lets two people agree entirely about what happened and still disagree about what a city should do.

## The presentism argument, fairly stated

In *Perspectives on History* in 2022, James H. Sweet argued that historians increasingly read the past through present political concerns, warning that "If history is only those stories from the past that confirm current political positions, all manner of political hacks can claim historical expertise," and that although "historical questions often emanate out of present concerns", the past should interrupt and contradict the present unpredictably.

That is an argument rather than a verdict, and it is contested within the discipline it addresses — which is not a weakness in the example but the point of it. The AHA's own standards describe disagreement as productive, stating that "Disagreements and uncertainties enrich our discipline" and that "Multiple, conflicting perspectives are among the truths of history." Reading a contested-legacy argument well means identifying which of the four questions each side is answering, rather than deciding who sounds more confident. The habit of separating perspective, selection, framing and distortion in a text is covered in [how to spot bias in history writing](/blog/how-to-spot-bias-in-history-writing/).

## A caution about "debated" labels

Software increasingly labels topics as contested, and the labels are worth reading sceptically. Our own app is an example, so the disclosure belongs here: World History Timeline Sim is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and it carries an uncertainty label that marks entries as debated.

That label marks *topics with contested legacies* rather than *claims that historians dispute*, and the two are not the same. In practice it is applied to figures whose reputations are argued over — among them Columbus, Churchill, Aurangzeb, Cixi, Marcos and Suharto — including alongside statements about them that nobody disputes. A reader who takes the label to mean "historians disagree about whether this happened" will draw the wrong conclusion. We are describing the behaviour rather than defending it, and it should not be cited as evidence of scholarly disagreement about anything.

The general lesson outlives our app. Any interface that compresses "this reputation is argued about", "the sources conflict" and "the dating is uncertain" into one badge has thrown away the distinction that matters. The app's content carries no citations and has had no external review, and no app replaces a teacher, a scholarly book, an archive or a primary source — its own disclaimer screen says so. More on the underlying evidence problem appears in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/), and the attribution question for monuments specifically in [who built the great monuments](/blog/who-built-the-great-monuments/).

## Using the four questions

Next time an argument about a historical figure gets loud, try sorting each claim into one of the four boxes. Most of the heat comes from claims in box four being answered with evidence from box one, or from box-one claims being treated as though they were opinions.

Where a government maintains an official position that historians outside it reject, describe the disagreement rather than adjudicating it in your own voice: say who holds which position, what each rests on, and what would settle it. That is slower than taking a side, and it is the only version that survives contact with a reader who already knows the case.
