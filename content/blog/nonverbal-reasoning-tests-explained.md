A nonverbal reasoning test asks you to infer a rule from shapes instead of from words or numbers. A row of figures changes in some systematic way; you pick the option that continues the change. The selling point attached to these tests — that removing language removes culture — is where the claim outruns the evidence. Dropping the reading load genuinely helps a candidate whose first language is not the test's language. It does not make the item neutral, because a diagram is a convention that people learn, and the published research on cross-cultural testing is much less comfortable than the marketing.

## What the items actually look like

Three formats cover most of what you will meet. A **figural series** shows a sequence of frames and asks for the next one. A **matrix** shows a grid with one cell missing and asks which option belongs there. An **odd-one-out** shows several figures governed by a shared rule and asks which breaks it.

The rules themselves are a short list applied in combination: rotation, reflection, translation, addition or removal of an element, change of shading, change of size, change of count, and progression along any of those. Difficulty rises when rules are combined, when two rules operate on different features at once, or when the relevant feature is not the visually loudest one.

University careers services group these with numerical and verbal tests under selection testing, note that they are timed and have right and wrong answers, and observe that logical and diagrammatic formats are used particularly often for technical roles. That last point is worth holding on to: in employment settings these tests are chosen for predictive convenience, not because anyone believes shapes are a purer window onto thought.

## What removing language removes

Exactly one thing, reliably: the requirement to read fluently in the test's language under time pressure. That is a real and worthwhile gain, and it is why nonverbal formats exist at all. The nonverbal Army Beta of 1919 was built for that reason, as the history of [who invented the IQ test](/blog/who-invented-the-iq-test/) sets out.

What it does not remove is the construct question — [what IQ tests actually measure](/blog/what-iq-tests-actually-measure/) is the same question whether the items carry words or shapes. Nor does it remove everything else the candidate brings. Familiarity with two-dimensional representations of objects, the convention that a sequence runs left to right, the expectation that exactly one answer is correct, the habit of working quickly under a clock, prior exposure to puzzle-shaped items — all of these vary with schooling and with what a person has been asked to do before, and none of them is language.

## The evidence points both ways, and the detail is the point

Work in cross-cultural neuropsychology has repeatedly found that the assumption of neutrality is unsafe. One study of cognitive testing across cultural groups reported that "the most striking, consistent biases are seen on tests of basic visuoperception as well as executive tasks utilizing visual stimuli... rather than on tasks with a prominent language component", and noted that this "runs counter to common clinical practice in neuropsychology which emphasizes the use of non-verbal tests, presumed to be less biased than verbal tasks". The same paper points out that many instruments were developed and normed in a narrow set of populations, which builds the comparison group's assumptions into the scale itself.

A different study, comparing South African and British university students on a Wechsler battery, found that the battery "lacks measurement invariance between the two groups, suggesting that it may be tapping different constructs in each group" — but also that the subtests "tapping WM and fluid reasoning (Matrix Reasoning) showed no cross-cultural effects", while knowledge-loaded subtests did. So the honest summary is not "nonverbal tests are biased" and not "nonverbal tests are fair". It is that fairness is a property of a particular test used with a particular group for a particular decision, and it has to be demonstrated rather than assumed from the absence of words.

That is also how the professional standards frame it. The *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, published jointly by the American Educational Research Association, the American Psychological Association and the National Council on Measurement in Education, treat fairness as a technical issue of test development and use rather than a feature you can read off the item format.

## Practising without being misled

If you are preparing for a real selection test, the useful work is narrow: learn the rule vocabulary, practise spotting which feature is varying, and practise under the clock so that scanning becomes automatic. The sister article on [how abstract reasoning items are built](/blog/abstract-reasoning-tests-explained/) goes through item anatomy and distractor design in more detail, and [spatial reasoning and number sense](/blog/spatial-reasoning-and-number-sense/) covers a related question people conflate with this one.

What will not help is treating a practice score as a measurement of yourself. Repeated exposure to a format changes your score on that format, which is one reason [working memory and training](/blog/working-memory-and-training/) is worth reading before drawing conclusions from any improvement, and why the evidence review on [brain training apps](/blog/do-brain-training-apps-work/) applies here too.
