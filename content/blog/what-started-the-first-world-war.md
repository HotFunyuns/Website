The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 started the First World War in the sense that a match starts a fire. It is the trigger. It is not the cause, and treating it as one leaves you unable to explain why a political murder in a provincial capital produced a four-year continental war rather than a diplomatic incident. The preconditions — interlocking alliance commitments, a sustained arms build-up, a Balkan region destabilised by Ottoman retreat, and mobilisation plans that compressed the time available for decisions — are what turned the trigger into a catastrophe.

## Trigger and precondition, kept apart

The distinction is not pedantry; it is the entire analytical content of the question.

A trigger is the event that begins a specific sequence. A precondition is the structure that determines what that sequence can become. The same assassination in 1890 or 1930 does not produce the same result, because the surrounding architecture is different. This is the same reasoning that makes the debate over [what caused the Bronze Age collapse](/blog/what-caused-the-bronze-age-collapse/) productive rather than circular: invasion, drought and earthquake are candidate triggers, and the systemic fragility that let local shocks cascade is the precondition.

So the useful question is not "what started it" but "what made a crisis of this kind escalate this far, this fast".

## The architecture of 1914

Four structures did the work.

**The alliance system.** Fordham University's collection of pre-1914 documents groups its material under a "Path to War" heading, indexing treaties including the Dual Alliance of 1879, the Three Emperors League of 1881 and the Anglo-Russian Entente of 1907. Each was defensive in its own language. Together they meant that a confrontation between any two great powers automatically implicated others.

**The arms race.** Naval and land build-ups over the preceding two decades produced enormous standing capacity and a widespread belief that windows of relative advantage were closing — an incentive to act sooner rather than later.

**Balkan instability.** Ottoman retreat from south-eastern Europe left contested territory, competing national claims, and two Balkan wars immediately before 1914. Austria-Hungary, a multinational empire, read Serbian nationalism as an existential threat to its own structure rather than as a border dispute.

**Mobilisation timetables.** Continental mobilisation plans were built around railway schedules and could not easily be partially executed or reversed once begun. That converted a political decision into a logistical commitment, and it shrank the negotiating window from months to days.

None of these four is a cause on its own. All four together describe a system in which a sufficiently sharp trigger produced war.

## The July crisis, in outline

Through July 1914 the crisis moved in steps: Austro-Hungarian consultation with Berlin about support, an ultimatum to Serbia with terms shaped to be difficult to accept in full, a Serbian reply that conceded most but not all of it, an Austro-Hungarian declaration of war on Serbia, Russian mobilisation in support of Serbia, German declarations against Russia and France, and the German advance through Belgium. The Australian War Memorial records the end point of that chain plainly: "Britain and Germany went to war on 4 August 1914."

The documentary record of those weeks is unusually rich, because every foreign ministry involved later published selections of its own papers to support its case. That is worth knowing before reading any of them: these collections were assembled as arguments. The Bundesarchiv in Germany, The National Archives in the United Kingdom and the US National Archives all hold the underlying material, and reading a published collection as a neutral record is the mistake our guide to [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) is written to prevent.

## The war-guilt argument, with the positions named

This is a hundred-year-old dispute with national stakes, and our job is to describe it accurately rather than to settle it.

The 1919 peace settlement assigned responsibility to Germany and its allies, and that clause became both a legal basis for reparations and a political wound. Through the 1920s German scholarship and the German foreign ministry mounted a very large documentary campaign against the verdict, publishing diplomatic papers at scale; parallel publication efforts followed in other countries. In the 1960s the German historian Fritz Fischer argued that German leadership had pursued expansionist aims and bore primary responsibility, which reopened the question inside German historiography itself and produced a long controversy. Later scholarship has tended to distribute responsibility across several capitals, emphasising miscalculation, misread signals and the mobilisation dynamics described above, while other historians continue to argue for asymmetric responsibility.

Readers usually meet whichever version their national curriculum teaches, and mistake it for the settled answer. The accurate statement is that the professional dispute is live, that it is partly about evidence and partly about what "responsibility" means when several governments each took a step, and that any single-sentence verdict is a position rather than a finding. Our article on [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) covers a related habit: knowing which choices a confident summary has quietly made.

## What the war contained that a causes article must not absorb

The First World War is also the context in which the Armenian Genocide took place, and it should not be folded into a diplomatic narrative. Doing so tends to convert a deliberate policy of deportation and mass killing into an atmospheric consequence of wartime conditions. It has its own documented sequence, its own evidence base and its own continuing dispute over characterisation, and we treat it separately in [the Armenian Genocide explained](/blog/armenian-genocide-explained/).

The war's human cost is also better seen at a scale smaller than the national one. The National Archives in the United Kingdom holds a set of letters from railway company staff who enlisted, catalogued as RAIL 253/516: of 184 men from a single audit office who joined up, 17 died, and the company's memorial at Paddington eventually recorded 2,524 staff deaths. The Australian War Memorial records that from a population of fewer than five million, 416,809 Australian men enlisted, of whom more than 60,000 were killed. The United States, per its National Archives, remained neutral for three years before entering on 6 April 1917.

## What to take away

**The assassination is the trigger and the alliance-plus-mobilisation system is the precondition.** Any account that gives you only one of those has given you half an answer.

**The documents were published as arguments.** The July 1914 collections are evidence about what governments wanted others to believe as well as evidence about what happened.

**Nobody has settled the responsibility question**, and the honest presentation names the positions, their proponents and their grounds instead of choosing one.
