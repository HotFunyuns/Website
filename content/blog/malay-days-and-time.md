Days, months and clock times are some of the most regular vocabulary in Malay. The days are Arabic loans, the months are recognisably English, and clock time is built from one word, *pukul*, plus a number. The trap is half past, which works differently in Malaysian Malay and Indonesian. This reference covers all three areas with examples from Wiktionary and Wikipedia; spellings are standard Malaysian Malay.

## Isnin to Ahad: the seven days

| English | Malay | Origin, per Wiktionary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Isnin | Arabic |
| Tuesday | Selasa | Arabic |
| Wednesday | Rabu | Classical Malay, from an Arabic form |
| Thursday | Khamis | Arabic |
| Friday | Jumaat | Arabic |
| Saturday | Sabtu | Arabic |
| Sunday | Ahad | Arabic |

The word for "day" is *hari*: Wiktionary analyses *tengah hari*, "noon", as *tengah* "middle" plus *hari* "day", and lists *Hari Satu*, literally "day one", as a synonym of Isnin. Wikipedia's article on the names of the days of the week explains two of the Arabic sources. Friday, *Jumaat*, comes from an Arabic word meaning "to gather", and Saturday, *Sabtu*, is from the word for the Sabbath. The same table sets the Malay names beside the Arabic ones they come from, such as *al-ithnayn* for Isnin and *al-khamīs* for Khamis. Wiktionary also records variants worth recognising. *Senin* is an alternative form of Isnin, *Kamis* of Khamis, and *Minggu* is given as a synonym of Ahad. Wikipedia's comparison of Indonesian and Standard Malay shows the split: Sunday is *Ahad* in Malaysian Malay and *Minggu* or *Ahad* in Indonesian. In Malay, **minggu** on its own means "week"; Wiktionary traces it to Portuguese *domingo*, "Sunday".

## Januari to Disember: the twelve months

Wiktionary's list of Malay month names:

| Month | Malay |
| --- | --- |
| January | Januari |
| February | Februari |
| March | Mac |
| April | April |
| May | Mei |
| June | Jun |
| July | Julai |
| August | Ogos |
| September | September |
| October | Oktober |
| November | November |
| December | Disember |

Wiktionary notes that *Januari* and *Ogos* are borrowed from English, and that Indonesian uses *Maret* and *Agustus* where Malay has *Mac* and *Ogos*. The general word **bulan** means both "moon" and "month": *Aku pergi ke sana dua kali untuk setiap bulan*, "I go there twice every month".

## Pukul, jam and asking the time

Wiktionary gives two words you need:

- **pukul**: as a verb "to hit, to strike", and as a noun "hour" in clock times: *pukul tiga*, "three o'clock".
- **jam**: "hour" as a period of sixty minutes, and "clock".

To ask the time, say *Pukul berapa?* or *Sekarang pukul berapa?*, literally "now, how many o'clock?". [Malay question words](/blog/malay-question-words/) covers *berapa* and the rest, and [Malay numbers](/blog/malay-numbers/) gives the numbers that go after *pukul*.

## Half and quarter: setengah and suku

**Setengah** means "a half", from *se-* plus *tengah*, "middle". **Suku** means "a quarter"; Wiktionary's example is *Sekarang satu suku*, "It's a quarter past one now". In Malaysian Malay the fraction comes **after** the hour: Wikipedia's comparison article gives *pukul tujuh setengah* as half past seven.

## A worked example: one phrase, two times

Take the same three words in two standards:

1. **Malaysian Malay**: *pukul tujuh setengah* = 7 + 0.5 = **7.30**, half past seven.
2. **Indonesian**: *pukul setengah tujuh* = half an hour before seven = 7 − 0.5 = **6.30**. Wikipedia explains that this follows Dutch *half zeven*.

The difference is exactly one hour, which is enough to miss a bus. Wikipedia adds a second difference: Malaysia uses the **12-hour clock** as standard, while Indonesia, Brunei and Singapore use the 24-hour clock as standard. [Malay vs Indonesian](/blog/malay-vs-indonesian-differences/) covers more of these splits.

## Pagi, tengah hari, petang and malam

With a 12-hour clock, the part of the day carries information. Wiktionary gives:

| Malay | English |
| --- | --- |
| pagi | morning |
| tengah hari | noon, midday |
| petang | afternoon, between noon and evening |
| malam | night |

These also make greetings: Wiktionary's example for *malam* is *Selamat malam*, "Good night". Wiktionary lists *sore* as a synonym of *petang*, and Wikipedia's comparison table gives *selamat* as the "good" of "good morning" in both standards.
