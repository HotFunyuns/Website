Almost every difference between an anime and the manga it came from traces back to a scheduling problem.

**Televised anime episodes are generally published at a higher rate than the manga chapters from which the anime draws its source material.** A weekly series consumes its source faster than the source can be produced. Everything downstream — filler arcs, pacing changes, anime-original endings, the divergence that sends people to forums to argue about which version is canon — is a consequence of a production running out of material.

That is not the only cause. But it is the one that explains the most, and it is rarely the one people reach for.

## The two media are not the same instrument

Before the adaptation problems, there are differences that exist regardless of scheduling, because the media can do different things.

**Manga controls pace loosely.** A page of panels sets an order and a rhythm, but the reader decides how long to look. A double-page spread is as long as you want it to be. A rapid exchange can be read in three seconds or savoured for thirty.

**Anime controls pace exactly.** Every beat has a duration chosen by the director. That is a gain — timing becomes a tool, and a held silence is a real effect — and a loss, because the reader's ability to linger is gone.

**Sound exists in only one of them.** Voice performance, music and effects are an entire expressive layer that manga has to achieve through lettering, panel composition and the reader's imagination. A line that is ambiguous on the page is disambiguated the moment an actor reads it, for better or worse.

**Motion costs money.** Animation is expensive per second, and limited animation techniques — reusing frames, holding on static shots, panning across a still image — are long-established production responses to that. A manga panel is drawn once and costs nothing to look at for a minute; the animated equivalent is a budget decision.

Those differences mean that even a scrupulously faithful adaptation is a different experience. Faithfulness to a script is achievable; faithfulness to an experience is not, because the instruments differ.

## Filler, properly defined

**Filler is content the anime producers generate themselves rather than adapting from the manga**, and its function is to stall for time while the mangaka and their team catch up.

It is worth being precise about the incentives, because "filler" is usually said as an insult and the underlying situation is a genuine constraint. A weekly broadcast slot has to be filled every week. The source produces perhaps one chapter a week, and an episode consumes more than one chapter. The arithmetic guarantees that a long-running adaptation will overtake its source, and when it does the production has three options:

1. **Stop and wait.** Expensive, and it risks losing the broadcast slot and the audience.
2. **Slow down.** Stretch the available material with longer reaction shots, extended flashbacks and recaps.
3. **Invent.** Write original episodes or arcs that do not advance the main plot.

Naruto and Bleach are the series most often named as featuring large amounts of filler, and both are long-running weekly adaptations of ongoing manga — which is exactly the configuration the arithmetic above predicts.

Option two deserves more attention than it gets, because it is less visible than a filler arc and changes the experience more. Pacing padding does not announce itself. An episode that adapts half a chapter is not obviously filler; it just feels slow, and viewers attribute the slowness to the story rather than to the schedule.

## Anime-original endings

The same problem at the end of a run produces the most consequential divergence.

If an adaptation reaches the end of the published source and the source is not finished, the production cannot animate chapters that do not exist. An **anime-original ending** is a conclusion written for the adaptation.

The result is two different endings to the same story, both of which people will describe as what happened. Which one is "real" is a question with no answer that everyone will accept: the manga ending is the author's, and the anime ending is the one a large share of the audience actually watched.

A related and less discussed case: an adaptation that ends **without** an ending, stopping at a plot-neutral point because the source is still running. That is the polite version of the same constraint, and it leaves the viewer with an incomplete story rather than a different one.

## Which version is a question about?

This is the practical consequence for anyone testing or being tested on anime knowledge, and it is where the two halves of this article meet.

For a series where anime and manga diverge, a factual question can have two correct answers. *How does it end?* Depends which one. *Does this character appear in this arc?* Depends which one. *What happens at this moment?* Depends which one, and possibly on whether the episode in question was filler.

Any quiz that asks such a question without naming the version has written an ambiguous question, and ambiguity in a quiz is not a difficulty setting. It is a defect — it punishes the player who knows more, because a player who knows both versions has to guess which one the writer had in mind. Our [guide to what makes an anime quiz worth playing](/blog/anime-trivia-quiz-guide/) works through the wider version of that principle.

## The sourcing rule in our question bank

[Anime Trivia & Word Games](/apps/anime-trivia-word-games/) draws on a question bank covering characters, villains, powers, organisations, story arcs, locations, lore and memorable scenes, across modes including multiple choice, timed rounds, survival, fill-in-the-blank, matching, ordering events and word puzzles.

Questions carry source metadata, and the rule when the two versions disagree is simple and was arrived at the hard way: **the manga is the default source, and any question whose answer depends on anime-original material is tagged separately and says so in the question text.**

The manga default is not an aesthetic judgement about which version is better. It is a stability argument. The manga is the source the adaptation derives from, it is the version least likely to be superseded, and where a series has multiple adaptations — a first run, a later remake that re-adapts the source more closely, a film continuity — the manga is the one thing they share. A question written against the manga stays correct across all of them.

The separate tag on anime-original material exists so that those questions can be included deliberately rather than by accident. They are legitimate questions — a filler arc that a large audience watched is a real part of the cultural object — but a player who has only read the manga cannot answer them, and they should not be scattered through a general round as though they were ordinary lore.

The failure this replaced was mundane and instructive. Questions written from memory by someone who had watched the adaptation, sitting next to questions written by someone who had read the source, with no field recording which was which. Both sets were correct in their own frame. Together they were a round where players lost points for knowing the wrong version — which is the opposite of what a quiz is for.

There is an unglamorous cost to the rule: it means some genuinely memorable anime moments cannot be used in general rounds. We think that is the right price. A question a well-informed player cannot answer confidently is a worse question than a slightly less exciting one they can.

## So which should you start with?

Whichever you meet first becomes your canonical version. That is worth knowing before you choose, because the effect is strong and largely irreversible.

**Read first** if you want the author's pacing, the author's ending, and a version that will not be overtaken.

**Watch first** if performance, music and motion are what you are there for, and accept that the manga will subsequently read as an alternative cut of something you already know.

Either way, expect divergence and stop treating it as a flaw. Two versions of a story made under different constraints by different people will differ. That is what adapting is.
