To someone new to cricket, a five-day match that ends with no winner can sound like a mistake. In Test cricket it is one of the results the Laws provide for. Winning requires two things at once: more runs and enough wickets. If time runs out before one side achieves both, nobody has won, and Law 16 of the Laws of Cricket calls it a **draw**.

## Two currencies: runs and time

In a limited-overs match, the innings ends when the overs run out, so one side's total is always compared with the other's. A Test has no overs limit per innings. Each side bats twice, and the match ends when the scheduled time is up, five days under the ICC's October 2026 Test playing conditions.

So the side batting last can avoid defeat without reaching its target, by simply not getting bowled out. Law 16 lists the results:

| Result | What has to happen |
| --- | --- |
| Win | A side's total exceeds the opponents' total from their completed innings |
| Tie | All innings are completed and the scores are equal |
| Draw | Neither a win nor a tie (nor a match conceded or awarded), for example because time runs out |

## Why ties are so rare

A tie needs the fourth innings to end, all out, with the scores exactly level. Wikipedia's article on tied Tests records only two: Brisbane in 1960 and Madras, now Chennai, in 1986.

## Declarations: buying time to win

Because time is limited, captains trade runs for time with a **declaration**: closing an innings before the side is all out. Law 15 allows the batting captain to declare at any time when the ball is dead, and to forfeit an innings before it starts, **except in the final innings of the match**, a restriction set out in Law 15 of the 2026 edition. Once announced, a declaration cannot be changed.

A declaration has to balance two risks: set too small a target and the opposition may chase it; leave too little time and they may survive for the draw. The [follow-on](/blog/follow-on-rule-cricket/) is the other tool for the same problem, making a weaker side bat again immediately.

## The last day and the last hour

The ICC's Test playing conditions fix how much cricket the last day contains. On days other than the last, at least 90 overs must be bowled. On the last day, at least 75 overs are due before the final hour, and then at least **15 overs in the last hour**. A side batting for a draw therefore knows roughly how many balls it must survive.

**A worked example.** At the start of the final day, the fielding side needs six more wickets. With at least 75 overs due before the last hour and at least 15 in it, the batters must survive about 90 overs, 540 legal balls, while losing no more than five wickets. If they are 220 runs behind the target, a draw becomes the realistic aim, and survival rather than scoring shapes their batting.

Rain changes the arithmetic. The playing conditions reduce the minimum overs when time is lost and allow some lost time to be made up, but on the last day no time is made up for interruptions that start after the last hour begins.

## How the pitch shapes the odds

Wikipedia's article on the pitch notes that, as a multi-day match goes on, the surface may crumble and favour spin bowling, which can make the last day harder to survive. It also records that the heavy roller was banned for the 2010 County Championship season because it was believed to create excessively flat pitches and contribute to drawn matches. [How cricket pitches affect play](/blog/how-cricket-pitches-affect-play/) explains why.

## What a draw is worth

In the ICC World Test Championship, the playing conditions effective from October 2026 award points per match:

| Result | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Win | 12 |
| Tie | 6 |
| Draw | 4 |
| Loss | 0 |

Teams are ranked by the percentage of available points they earn, after deductions for slow over rates, so a draw is worth a third of a win.
