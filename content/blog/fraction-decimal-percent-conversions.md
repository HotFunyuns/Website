A fraction, a decimal and a percent are three ways of writing the same number, and each conversion is one move. Fraction to decimal is a division. Decimal to percent is a shift of two places. Percent to fraction is the number over 100, reduced. The skill worth having is not the procedure but knowing which conversions to do by memory instead.

Three worked in a line: 3/8 divides to 0.375, which is 37.5%. Going the other way, 0.625 is 625/1000, which reduces by 125 to 5/8, and is 62.5%. And 45% is 45/100, which reduces by 5 to 9/20, and is 0.45.

Seventeen rows. Once they are recall rather than calculation, most conversion questions never reach the division stage at all, and the rest can be built from them.

## Converting without dividing

**Scale the denominator to 100.** This works whenever the denominator is a factor of 100. For 17/25, multiply top and bottom by 4 to get 68/100, so 68%. For 9/20, multiply by 5 to get 45/100, so 45%. Halves, quarters, fifths, tenths, twentieths, twenty-fifths and fiftieths all yield to this.

**Split into anchors when it will not scale.** Sixteenths do not divide 100, but they decompose. 5/16 is 1/4 plus 1/16. A quarter is 25%, and a sixteenth is half of an eighth, so half of 12.5%, which is 6.25%. Adding gives 31.25%, and dividing 5 by 16 confirms 0.3125.

**Halve a known anchor.** Anything whose denominator doubles a familiar one is one halving away. Twelfths come from sixths, sixteenths from eighths, fortieths from twentieths.

## Which fractions terminate, and why

This is the rule almost nobody is taught, and it settles in advance whether a division will ever end.

**A fraction in lowest terms terminates as a decimal exactly when its denominator's only prime factors are 2 and 5.** MathWorld states the condition in the same form: the numbers with finite decimal expansions are fractions whose denominator is a power of two times a power of five.

The reason is short. A terminating decimal is, by definition, a fraction over a power of ten. Every power of ten is a product of twos and fives and nothing else. So a fraction can be rewritten over a power of ten only when its denominator is built from twos and fives.

- **7/40 terminates**, because 40 is 8 × 5. It comes to 0.175.
- **7/12 does not**, because 12 carries a factor of 3. It comes to 0.58333…

**Reduce before applying the rule.** 6/15 appears to carry a 3, but it reduces to 2/5, which terminates at 0.4. The test applies to the fraction in lowest terms, which is one more reason the ordering method in [simplifying a fraction quickly](/blog/simplifying-fractions-quickly/) earns its place first.

### How long the repeat runs

When a fraction does repeat, the block length is not arbitrary. MathWorld ties it to how powers of ten behave modulo the denominator, so the length is a property of the denominator rather than of the numerator.

- 1/7 repeats in blocks of six: 0.142857…
- 1/11 repeats in blocks of two: 0.0909…
- 1/13 repeats in blocks of six: 0.076923…

A practical consequence is that every seventh has the same six digits in a rotated order, which is a pattern set out with the full cycle in [mental math tricks that actually work](/blog/mental-math-tricks-that-work/). The related point about deciding before you divide whether a decimal will ever terminate is covered from the division side in [how to divide in your head](/blog/mental-division-tricks/).

## Percent to fraction, and why you would bother

Because a division is often easier than a multiplication.

**37.5% of 240.** As a decimal it is 0.375 × 240, which is three separate multiplications and a carry. As a fraction it is 3/8 of 240: divide 240 by 8 to get 30, multiply by 3 to get **90**.

The techniques library in our app states the underlying principle bluntly — memorise the big anchors as fractions, because dividing beats multiplying. That is the entire argument for learning the table above in the fraction direction as well as the percent direction.

## Why fluency across all three notations matters

The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide on fractions instruction places the idea that fractions are numbers — points on a line, comparable with any other number — at the centre of its recommendations, alongside using number lines as a central representation. Conversion fluency is what makes that concrete: a number you can write three ways is a number you can place, compare and estimate with.

There is a documented failure mode on the other side. In a study of sixth and eighth graders published in *Developmental Psychology*, Siegler and Pyke found that around a third of children estimated fraction magnitudes from the numerator alone or the denominator alone, a pattern associated with weaker fraction arithmetic. Conversion is a useful antidote precisely because a decimal has no separate top and bottom to misread.
