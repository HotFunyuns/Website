**30-2-1 (18 KO)** reads: thirty wins, two losses, one draw, of which eighteen wins came inside the distance. That is the whole notation, and it takes ten seconds to learn.

What takes longer is understanding how little it tells you — and which of the numbers is doing real work.

## The three numbers, and the one in brackets

The record runs wins, then losses, then draws, always in that order. A fourth figure sometimes follows for no contests, or they are noted separately with the abbreviation NC.

The bracketed number is a convention rather than a rule, and it counts wins by knockout or technical knockout: victories that ended before the scheduled distance was completed. You will occasionally see it written as KO, sometimes as "KOs", and in some listings it is split to distinguish knockouts from technical knockouts.

Two habits make the notation more useful immediately:

- **Read the bracket as a ratio, not a total.** Eighteen stoppages from thirty wins describes a different fighter from eighteen from fifty-five. The ratio is a statement about *how* a fighter wins.
- **Read the draw column as a flag.** Draws are rare enough that one in a record usually points at a specific fight worth knowing about.

## What a no contest actually is

A no contest is a bout that ends for reasons outside the fighters' hands, with neither a winner nor a loser. It is not a draw. A draw is a result; a no contest is the absence of one.

The rules for when one is declared differ between countries and organisations. In the United States, a bout that ends before the completion of the fourth round due to an accidental headbutt is declared a no contest — before that rule was adopted, such stoppages were ruled technical draws. Other no contests arise from genuinely unusual circumstances: a power outage, or rain at an outdoor event.

There is a historical dimension worth knowing, because it explains why old records are littered with them. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, boxing was officially banned in many countries and in parts of the United States, and police would occasionally shut bouts down even though regional commissions sanctioned them. Referees of that era were also known to stop fights they felt were too slow through lack of aggression. With the sport now lawful almost everywhere, the number of fights called no contest has decreased dramatically.

## Reading the bracket properly

The stoppage figure is the most informative part of the notation and the most commonly misread, because people treat it as a power rating.

It is closer to a **style description**. A high proportion of stoppages tells you a fighter tends to end fights early; it does not tell you they would beat a fighter with a lower proportion. The two most common misreadings are worth separating:

**A high ratio can be an artefact of opposition.** Stopping opponents who were not durable is easier than stopping opponents who were, and the record cannot distinguish the two. A fighter developed carefully through a run of short-notice opponents will carry an impressive bracket that says more about matchmaking than about punching.

**A low ratio is not evidence of weak power.** Fighters who face better opposition get fewer stoppages, because better opposition is harder to stop. A fighter who moves up in class typically sees their stoppage ratio fall while getting better, not worse.

Where the figure genuinely earns its place is in **matchmaking**, which is where style information matters more than ranking information. A high-stoppage fighter against an opponent who has never been stopped is an interesting fight in a way that two fighters with similar records and similar ratios is not. That is the same reasoning a matchmaker applies when building a card, covered in [how fight cards are built](/blog/how-fight-cards-are-built/).

One practical note on the notation itself: some listings split knockouts from technical knockouts and some combine them, so a bracketed figure is not always comparable between sources. Check what the source is counting before comparing two fighters from two databases.

## Why records mislead, specifically

A record counts results. It does not count opposition, and that is the whole problem.

Consider two fighters, both 20-0.

The first has fought twenty opponents with losing records, at a comfortable weight, mostly at home, mostly early in their opponents' careers or late in them. The second has fought six ranked opponents, moved up a division once, and won three close decisions.

Identical records. Completely different careers. The record has no field for any of the information that separates them.

This is why an unbeaten record functions more as a marketing asset than as an evaluation. It is genuinely valuable — it creates leverage in negotiations and it sells tickets — but a fighter protecting a zero is under a commercial incentive that does not always point toward the hardest available fight. [How fight cards are built](/blog/how-fight-cards-are-built/) covers how that incentive shapes matchmaking.

The other thing records omit is *how* the losses happened. A fighter at 30-2 who lost two close decisions to champions is in a different position from one who was stopped twice, and the notation treats those identically.

## Records a drafted stable produces

The record notation matters in [40-0 MMA & Boxing Fight Draft](/apps/mma-boxing-fight-draft/) because the entire game is named after one: forty fights without a loss.

The app runs two separate simulators, and they produce different result vocabularies. The mixed martial arts simulator resolves bouts by knockout, technical knockout, submission or decision. The boxing simulator adds draws and judges' scorecards. Records accumulate from both, which means a stable's combined record is assembled from two different outcome sets — one that cannot produce a draw and one that can.

Here is the pattern that took us longest to understand, and it is the cleanest illustration of the point above.

**A drafted stable can post an excellent combined record while the matchmaking behind it was poor.**

The mechanism is simple arithmetic. If you draft ten fighters and repeatedly put your strongest ones into favourable matchups while your weaker signings fight rarely, the aggregate record looks outstanding. The stable is winning. It is also not being tested, and the moment a title path requires the weaker fighters to face ranked opposition, the record and the reality separate sharply.

That is not a bug we introduced. It is what happens whenever you aggregate results without weighting opposition — the same flaw the notation has in reality, reproduced faithfully. What we did in response was expose the component records alongside the combined one, so a stable's headline number can be checked against who actually did the winning.

The practical advice is the same advice that applies to reading any record: **look at the distribution, not the total.** A stable where one fighter is 12-0 and six others are 1-0 is a narrower achievement than a stable where eight fighters are 4-1.
