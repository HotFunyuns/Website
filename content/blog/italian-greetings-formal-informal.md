Italian greetings come down to two decisions: what time of day it is, and whether you are on *tu* or *Lei* terms with the person in front of you. Get those two right and the rest follows, because the verb you use next (*come stai?* or *come sta?*) depends on the same choice. This reference sets out the main greetings, the formal "you", the goodbyes, and a short sequence for a typical day.

## Ciao is for people you would call tu

*Ciao* is the greeting most visitors already know, and Wikipedia's article on it gives two cautions. First, it means both **hello and goodbye**. Second, in Italy it is **mainly used in informal contexts**: among family members, relatives and friends, "those one would address with the familiar *tu*" rather than the courtesy form *Lei*. In those circles, Wikipedia adds, *ciao* can be the norm even in the morning or evening, where *buongiorno* or *buonasera* would feel too formal.

The word has a surprising history. Wikipedia traces it to the Venetian phrase *s-ciào vostro*, "(I am) your slave", a courtesy formula along the lines of "at your service", which lost its servile meaning as it shortened. It spread through northern Italy in the late 19th and early 20th centuries and then across the country.

## Buongiorno, buonasera and buonanotte

For everyone you would not greet with *ciao*, use the time-of-day greetings.

| Greeting | Meaning | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| buongiorno | good morning, good day | morning and the very early afternoon |
| buon pomeriggio | good afternoon | afternoon |
| buonasera | good evening | later afternoon and evening |
| buonanotte | good night | at night |

Wiktionary lists *buongiorno* as "good day" and "good morning", and as "good afternoon" for the very early afternoon, with *buon pomeriggio* as a synonym there. It can also be written *buon giorno*. *Buondì* is a colloquial alternative.

The line between *buongiorno* and *buonasera* is the one that needs care. Wiktionary's usage note on *buonasera* says the division between *pomeriggio* (afternoon) and *sera* (evening) **varies regionally**: in much of Italy it falls around 5pm, but in the far south *sera* can also mean the afternoon, so *buonasera* may start earlier.

**Salve** looks like a handy all-purpose hello, and Wiktionary lists it as a synonym of *ciao*, *buongiorno* and *buonasera*, but it warns that it is not common as a greeting and is usually felt to be too stiff even on formal occasions.

## When to switch to Lei

Italian has two ways to say "you" to one person. Wikipedia's Italian grammar article explains that **tu** is informal and **Lei** is formal, a form used since the Renaissance, much as German uses *Sie* and French *vous*. Wikipedia's article on the T–V distinction gives the verbs: speaking to someone with *tu* is *dare del tu*; with *Lei*, *dare del Lei*.

The grammar is the tricky part. *Lei* is pronounced the same as *lei*, "she", and takes **third-person verbs**, so the formal "how are you?" is literally "how is she?":

- informal: *Ciao, Marco, come stai?*
- formal: *Buongiorno, signora, come sta?*

Wiktionary lists *come stai* as the informal question and *come sta* as its formal synonym. Wikipedia adds that *Lei* is grammatically feminine whoever it refers to, but adjectives agree with the real person: to a man you say *Lei è alto*, "you are tall". In some regions, such as Campania, *voi* is still used as the formal singular. For a group, Wikipedia notes formal *Loro* is very rarely used in modern Italian; plain *voi* serves instead.

## Introductions and goodbyes

- **Piacere**: Wiktionary lists it as an interjection meaning "pleased to meet you", as in *Piacere, Alberto*.
- **Arrivederci**: the general "goodbye, see you", literally "to seeing each other again".
- **Arrivederla**: the formal goodbye. Wiktionary says it is used instead of *arrivederci* in more formal contexts, for people of either sex who would normally be addressed as *Lei*.
- **Ci vediamo**: an informal "see you".
- **Ciao**: goodbye as well as hello, again only on *tu* terms.

## A day of greetings, worked through

Take a visitor who uses the 5pm guide from Wiktionary, remembering it is only approximate and regional.

1. **9:00, at a café, to the barista**: a stranger, so *Buongiorno*. Leaving: *Arrivederci*, or *Arrivederla* to be more formal.
2. **15:30, meeting a colleague's friend for the first time**: still before 17:00, so *Buon pomeriggio* (Wiktionary gives *buongiorno* for the afternoon only in the very early afternoon), then *Piacere* and *come sta?*, because you have not been invited to use *tu*.
3. **19:00, a shop**: 19:00 is two hours past the 17:00 line, so *Buonasera*.
4. **22:30, leaving a friend's dinner**: friends, so *Ciao, ci vediamo!*, or *Buonanotte*, "good night".

The arithmetic is trivial (19 − 17 = 2 hours past the switch), but the habit is not: check the clock and the relationship before you open your mouth.
