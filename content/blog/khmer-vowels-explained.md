Khmer vowels are where the script stops behaving like an alphabet. Most vowels are not letters at all but signs attached to a consonant, and most of those signs have two readings. Which reading you use depends on the consonant underneath, so learning a vowel means learning a pair of sounds. This guide sets out every dependent vowel sign with both values, then the diacritics that change them and the separate set of independent vowels. IPA values and UNGEGN romanisations follow Wikipedia's tables, which describe the Battambang-area pronunciation that Standard Khmer is based on.

## Dependent signs: written around, read after

Wikipedia calls the dependent vowels *srăk nĭssăy* or *srăk phsâm*, "connecting vowels", and stresses two points. They can only be written in combination with a consonant or cluster, and the vowel is always **pronounced after** the consonant, even when part of the sign sits above, below or to the left of it. A sign like េ is written before the consonant but read after it; ោ wraps around both sides.

If no vowel sign is written, the consonant is usually read with its own inherent vowel, *â* for the a-series and *ô* for the o-series, which [Khmer consonants and the two series](/blog/khmer-consonant-series/) explains letter by letter.

## Why one sign carries two sounds

Wikipedia's Khmer language article traces the double readings to history. In Old Khmer, vowels after voiced consonants became breathy and diphthongised, so \*kaa and \*gaa drifted apart. When consonant voicing was lost, the vowel kept the difference; later the breathiness disappeared too, leaving modern [kaː] and [kiə]. That is the origin of the a-series and o-series, and it is why the sign ា reads differently after ក and គ.

Four signs, ួ ឿ ៀ េ, have the same value in both series in this table, so they are the easiest to read. Wikipedia adds that pronunciations may differ in weak syllables and when a vowel is shortened by a diacritic, and that vowel quality varies widely in dialects such as Northern Khmer.

## A worked example: reading five syllables

1. **កា**: ក is a-series, so ា is [aː]: [kaː].
2. **គា**: គ is o-series, so the same sign gives [kiə] in the Khmer language article's transcription.
3. **លា** *léa* [liə]: ល is o-series, so ា takes its o-series reading, romanised *éa*.
4. **ត្រា** *tra* [traː]: ត is a stop and a-series, and stops outrank the sonorant រ in a cluster, so the vowel is a-series [aː].
5. **ធំ** *thum*: the circle above is the nĭkkôhĕt, and Wikipedia notes this word for "big" is pronounced [tʰom], or [tʰum] in some dialects.

Five syllables, one vowel sign appearing four times, and three different vowel sounds.

## Nĭkkôhĕt, reăhmŭkh and bânták

Three diacritics change the vowel itself, and Wikipedia treats some combinations as vowels in their own right:

- **nĭkkôhĕt** ំ nasalises the vowel with an added [m] and shortens long vowels: អំ is [ɑm] or [um], អុំ [om] or [um], អាំ [am] or [ŏəm].
- **reăhmŭkh** ះ, "shining face", adds a final [h]: អះ is [ah] or [ĕəh].
- **bânták** ់, a small stroke over the final consonant, shortens the vowel: inherent *â* becomes [ɑ], and ា in the a-series becomes short [a].

The fourth mark, **yŭkôlpĭntŭ** ៈ, a "pair of dots", shows a short vowel followed by a glottal stop.

## Independent vowels: the ones that stand alone

Khmer also has independent vowels, *sră pénh tuŏ*, "complete vowels", which are written without a consonant. Wikipedia explains that they represent combinations of a vowel with an initial glottal stop or a liquid, appear in a small number of words that are mostly of Indic origin, and are used with some inconsistency. A handful are frequent: ឥឡូវ *ĕlov* "now", ឪពុក *âupŭk* "father", ឬ *rœ* "or" and ឮ *lœ* "hear". The Khmer language article adds that written words never begin with a regular vowel sign, only with a consonant or an independent vowel.
