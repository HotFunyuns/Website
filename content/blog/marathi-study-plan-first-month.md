How long it takes to learn Marathi depends on what you mean by *learn*, so here is a specific answer to a specific version of the question. Thirty days of short daily practice will get you reading Devanagari slowly, counting to a hundred, greeting people correctly and making simple present-tense sentences. It will not get you conversation. Anyone telling you otherwise is describing what you will *recognise*, not what you will be able to *say*, and the gap between those two is where most language plans quietly fail.

That is the honest ceiling. Here is how to actually reach it.

## Split the session, not the week

The most common plan a beginner writes is: script on Mondays and Wednesdays, vocabulary on Tuesdays and Thursdays. It feels organised. It performs badly.

Distributed practice — spreading study across many short sessions rather than massing it into a few long ones — consistently produces better retention for the same total time. The finding is not controversial and it is not new. The practical reading of it is that *every* day should touch *both* tracks, because a day off from the script is a day of forgetting the script.

So: a few minutes of letters, a few minutes of words, every day. When the two are running together, the vocabulary gives your reading something worth decoding and the reading gives the vocabulary a shape to hang on.

## The four weeks

### Week one — sounds, ten vowels, first greetings

Vowels in both forms, independent letter and matra together. The first two or three consonant rows. नमस्कार, both gendered forms of "how are you", and your own name in the script.

By the end of the week you should be able to sound out short words, even if you do not know what they mean. That is the target: decoding before comprehension.

### Week two — the rest of the consonants, numbers to twenty

Finish the main consonant chart. Add the letters Marathi uses beyond the standard Hindi set — the [Marathi alphabet guide](/blog/marathi-alphabet-and-devanagari/) covers what those are and why they are worth a separate pass.

Start numbers, and stop at twenty. Marathi numbers are largely irregular and behave more like vocabulary than like arithmetic; pushing to a hundred this week will cost you the week.

Vocabulary: family terms, food, the three or four sentences you would use walking into a shop.

### Week three — the present tense and agreement

The आहे family first, because it makes complete sentences immediately. Then simple present-tense verbs and the gender agreement that comes with them.

This is the week the course gets harder, and it is worth knowing that in advance rather than interpreting it as a personal failure. Marathi has three grammatical genders and the verb agrees with one of them; there is no way to make that a small topic. [Marathi verbs for beginners](/blog/marathi-verb-basics/) works through the agreement patterns in minimal pairs.

Numbers: twenties and thirties.

### Week four — consolidation, not new ground

Do not add a new grammar topic in week four. Add situations.

Run the roleplays. Do the dictation exercises. Take the mistake-review material seriously — it is built from the questions you personally got wrong, and it is the highest-value ten minutes in the course.

Numbers: finish the decades, which are the load-bearing words, and fill in the gaps afterwards. [Marathi numbers 1 to 100](/blog/marathi-numbers-and-counting/) explains why that order is the right way round.

## Session length, and why ours is what it is

The units in [Learn Marathi Language Fast](/apps/learn-marathi/) are built to be finished in a few minutes. That is a cap, not an average, and it was a deliberate constraint rather than a stylistic preference.

The reason is the population. Marathi learners on a phone course are disproportionately heritage learners fitting study around a job and a family, people preparing for or already inside a move to Maharashtra, and Marathi speakers running the course in reverse to study English. What those groups have in common is that their study time is fragmented and unreliable. It arrives in the gaps.

A lesson that needs twenty-five uninterrupted minutes is not a longer lesson for those learners. It is a lesson that never gets started, because starting it means accepting you probably will not finish it, and an unfinished unit records no progress at all.

So units got shorter, and anything that would not fit got split. The clearest case was the grammar block described in [is Marathi hard to learn](/blog/is-marathi-hard-to-learn/), which became four short units after the single long one turned out to be where people stopped.

**What that cap implies for a thirty-day plan is worth being blunt about.** If units are short, a month contains a fixed and modest number of them. You cannot compress the course by taking bigger bites, because the bites do not come bigger. What you can do is take more of them in a day — and the returns on that are real but not linear, because the spacing you lose by stacking three units into one sitting is part of what made each unit work.

Two sessions a day, morning and evening, is the version of "going faster" that we would actually defend.

## Plan for the bad week

A month-long plan that assumes thirty good days has already failed, because you will not get thirty good days.

Decide now what the minimum viable session is — one unit, or five minutes of review, or the numbers drill and nothing else — and treat that as the thing you protect. Streaks are good at making you show up and bad at telling you whether you are learning anything, and they punish exactly the person who had a hard week. [Building a daily practice habit that survives a bad week](/blog/building-a-daily-practice-habit/) goes further into designing for the worst day rather than the best one.

## What you will and will not have on day thirty

**You will have:** slow but real reading of Devanagari, including the Marathi-specific letters; numbers to a hundred with some hesitation; greetings, introductions and polite forms; a few hundred words grouped by situation; simple present-tense sentences with correct agreement more often than not.

**You will not have:** the past tense in any secure form, conjunct fluency, listening comprehension at natural speed, or the ability to hold a conversation without the other person slowing down for you.

That last one is not a shortcoming of any particular course. It is what happens when a learner has been practising with material that waits for them.
