The land under a golf course shapes how it plays. Two of the best-known course types sit at opposite ends: the **links**, the coastal ground where golf began, and the **parkland** course, the inland, tree-lined layout that GOLF.com says PGA Tour viewers will have seen plenty of. This guide compares them using Wikipedia's articles on links and golf courses, GOLF.com's guide to course types and Golf Monthly's explanation of why links stay dry.

## Links and parkland at a glance

| | Links | Parkland |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where | On or near a coast, on sandy land | Inland, away from the sea |
| Ground | Firm, undulating, sandy, often among dunes | Softer, lusher grass |
| Trees | Few, if any | Usually plenty |
| Hazards | Small, deep pot bunkers, thick rough; barely any water | Dug bunkers, ponds, built-up rough |
| Typical shot | Low and running, landing short of the green | Flown to the target |
| Weather | Wind plays a big part; drains quickly | Clay or silt soils can waterlog |

## What makes a links

Wikipedia calls the links **the oldest style of golf course**, first developed in Scotland. The word comes through Scots from the Old English *hlinc*, "rising ground, ridge", and describes coastal sand dunes. Links land has dunes, an undulating surface and sandy soil that was useless for arable farming but readily supports browntop bent and red fescue grasses. Together, the soil and grasses produce the **firm turf** behind the links "running" game.

The landscape also explains the hazards. Wikipedia notes that links courses have barely any water hazards and few, if any, trees, partly because early courses were shaped by hand, so earth-moving and irrigation were kept to a minimum. What they have instead is uneven fairways, thick rough and small, deep **pot bunkers**. The category is narrower than the name suggests. GOLF.com notes that many courses call themselves links or links-style, but a true links must be on the coast with sandy soil underneath; true links are mostly found in Scotland, Ireland and England, among them the Old Course at St Andrews, Royal Troon and Lahinch.

## What makes a parkland course

GOLF.com describes parkland courses as built **inland, away from the ocean**, often with lots of trees and lush grass, and named for looking and feeling like a park. They are usually well manicured and full of **man-made features**: dug bunkers, ponds and built-up rough.

Because parkland courses are often built where conditions are not ideal for golf, GOLF.com explains, the grass and soil are harder and more expensive to maintain, and with less natural movement in the land the architect must do much more work to make the course interesting. It calls **Augusta National**, home of the Masters, arguably the most famous parkland course in the world.

## How the ground changes the shots

The two surfaces ask for different shots. On a links, Wikipedia explains, the hard ground lets balls run much further after landing, so players often aim to **land the ball short of the green** and let it roll on, rather than landing it on the green itself as they would on softer turf. Low, bouncing shots that skip onto greens work better than high shots with heavy backspin.

**A worked example.** A golfer has 140 yards to a pin at the front of the green. On a soft parkland green, the natural shot is a full, high iron that lands near the pin and stops. On a firm links green with the wind behind, the same shot could land by the pin and run through the back. The links answer is a lower shot that lands short of the green and rolls up onto it. The yardage is the same; the plan is different. [Golf course management basics](/blog/golf-course-management-basics/) covers this kind of planning.

## Wind, weather and the seasons

Wind is the other big difference. Wikipedia attributes it to the coastal location and the lack of trees, and notes that windy weather calls for low, accurate shots. The layout adds a twist: many traditional links play an "outward" nine in one direction along the coast and an "inward" nine back, so a helping wind on the way out becomes a headwind on the way home.

The soil decides what happens after rain. Golf Monthly explains that sandy links soil drains exceptionally well and dries out quickly even after a deluge, which is why links stay playable in winter. Parkland courses, it notes, tend to have **clay or silt soils** that drain less effectively and become waterlogged more easily. Coastal wind helps dry links surfaces too.

## Heathland: the course in between

Not every inland course is parkland. GOLF.com describes **heathland** courses, mostly in Britain, as inland courses on heath, with heather, gorse and coarse grasses, usually more open than parkland because their style is based on links. Their soil is sandy, and Golf Monthly notes that heathland courses, like links, tend to stay drier through the winter.

## Where the big championships are played

Course type also separates golf's majors. Wikipedia notes that **the Open Championship is always played on a links**, one of the main features that sets it apart from the three majors held in the United States. American majors have sometimes borrowed the style: the 2004 PGA Championship was played on a links-style course, Whistling Straits in Wisconsin, and the 2015 US Open at Chambers Bay, a links-style course in Washington.
