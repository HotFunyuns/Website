Ask yourself one question before you pick a Russian verb: **am I describing a process, or am I describing a completed whole?** Process takes the imperfective. Completed whole takes the perfective. That single question resolves the large majority of aspect choices, and it does so without reference to English tense — which is important, because English tense is the thing that will mislead you.

*Я читал книгу* — I was reading a book, I read at it, I spent time on it. *Я прочитал книгу* — I read the book, finished, done. Both are past tense. The difference is not when. It is how you are looking at it.

## Aspect is a second axis, not a second tense

Russian verbs carry tense and aspect independently, and most verbs exist as a **pair** rather than as a single item.

The pairing is formed in several ways:

- **By prefix**, the most common: *писать* / *написать* (write), *читать* / *прочитать* (read), *делать* / *сделать* (do).
- **By suffix**, going the other direction — a perfective made imperfective: *решить* / *решать* (decide), *дать* / *давать* (give).
- **By different roots entirely**: *говорить* / *сказать* (speak, say), *брать* / *взять* (take).

You learn the pair, not the verb. A dictionary entry that gives you only one half of a pair has given you half a word, and this is the single most useful change a learner can make to how they record vocabulary.

## Why the perfective has no present

This is the fact that makes the whole system click, and it is rarely stated plainly enough.

A perfective describes an action as a completed whole. An action that is happening right now is by definition not complete. So there is nothing for a perfective present tense to mean, and Russian does not have one.

What it has instead is a set of forms that look exactly like a present tense and function as a future:

| | Imperfective | Perfective |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Past | я чита́л — I was reading, I read (at it) | я прочита́л — I read it, finished |
| Present | я чита́ю — I am reading, I read | (does not exist) |
| Future | я бу́ду чита́ть — I will be reading | я прочита́ю — I will read it and finish |

The future row is where beginners are most often caught, because the two halves are built completely differently: the imperfective future needs the auxiliary *быть*, and the perfective future is just the conjugated perfective verb. If you find yourself trying to say *буду прочитать*, you have combined the two systems, and the sentence is not recoverable — pick one.

## Five situations that force the choice

Definitions do not help at conversational speed. Situations do.

**1. It happened repeatedly, or as a habit.** Imperfective. *Я часто писал ей* — I often wrote to her. The number of times is what makes it imperfective, not the tense.

**2. It happened once and produced a result you care about.** Perfective. *Я написал письмо* — I wrote the letter; it now exists.

**3. The action was interrupted, or was the background to something else.** Imperfective for the background, perfective for the interruption. *Я читал, когда он позвонил* — I was reading when he called.

**4. You are asking whether something happened at all.** Imperfective. *Ты читал эту книгу?* — have you (ever) read this book? You are asking about the general fact, not about the completion of a specific reading. Use the perfective here and you are asking about one particular occasion you both have in mind.

**5. You are telling someone not to do something.** Imperfective. *Не закрывай окно* — don't close the window. This one is nearly categorical and worth learning as a rule rather than as a judgement call.

A sixth case worth adding because it is the commonest error after tense-mapping: after the verbs *начать*, *продолжать* and *кончить* — to begin, to continue, to finish — the following infinitive is **imperfective**. *Он начал читать*, never *начал прочитать*. The starting verb already supplies the boundary; the second verb cannot supply another one.

## Why translating from English fails

English speakers reach for a mapping: past simple equals perfective, past continuous equals imperfective. It is a reasonable guess and it is wrong often enough to hurt.

*I read the book yesterday* can be either. If you mean you finished it, perfective. If you mean you spent the evening on it, imperfective. English does not force the distinction, so the English sentence does not contain the information you need.

Worse, the mapping fails hardest in exactly the cases beginners meet most: questions about experience (case 4 above), negative instructions (case 5), and anything with *often*, *usually* or *every day* in it. Those are not exotic sentences. They are the first fifty things a learner wants to say.

The durable fix is to stop translating and start asking the process-or-whole question directly of the situation. That is a different mental operation, and it is slower at first, and it is the only one that generalises. The same shift is what eventually makes case selection automatic — our [cases article](/blog/russian-cases-explained-for-beginners/) makes the argument that six cases are six jobs rather than six tables, and aspect rewards the same treatment.

## Where aspect and motion verbs collide

If you have already met verbs of motion, you have met aspect without being told.

Prefixed motion verbs lose the unidirectional and multidirectional contrast and become ordinary aspect pairs: *прийти* perfective against *приходить* imperfective. Everything in this article then applies to them unchanged. *Он пришёл* — he arrived, he is here. *Он приходил* — he came round, and by implication left again. *Он приходит каждый день* — repetition, so imperfective.

That interaction is set out in more detail in [Russian verbs of motion](/blog/russian-verbs-of-motion/), and it is a good stress test: if you can pick the right half of a prefixed motion pair, your aspect instinct is working.

## What we changed in our own course

[Regal - Learn Russian Language](/apps/learn-russian/) teaches aspect through **paired example sentences** rather than through a definition followed by exercises. A lesson item presents two sentences that differ only in aspect and asks what each one means, so the distinction arrives as a difference in meaning rather than as a grammatical label to be applied afterwards.

That is not how we built it the first time, and the reason for the change is worth stating.

The earlier format did what most materials do: give the rule, then test it. Learners could complete the exercises. They could state the rule accurately if asked. And then in a free-composition or roleplay item they would choose by translating the English tense anyway — because the rule had been stored as a piece of knowledge rather than as a question they knew to ask. Reciting "perfective means completed" is not the same skill as noticing, mid-sentence, that the thing you are about to describe is a process.

Pairs fixed that, because a pair does not let you skip the question. You cannot compare two sentences without deciding what makes them different.

The course's general approach — cases, gender, agreement and verb forms introduced in stages, with exercises that explain why an answer works rather than only marking it — matters more here than anywhere else, because aspect errors are usually understandable errors. Saved words and missed-question practice bring back the specific pairs you keep getting wrong, which is the right granularity: nobody is bad at aspect in general, but almost everyone has ten or twelve verbs they consistently misjudge.

The honest limit is the same as with any grammar point. A course can build the instinct to ask the question. Speed comes from use, and there is no app version of that.

## The procedure, compressed

1. **Process, repetition, habit, duration, background, or a question about whether it ever happened?** Imperfective.
2. **One event, viewed as complete, usually with a result?** Perfective.
3. **Telling someone not to do something?** Imperfective.
4. **After начать, продолжать, кончить?** Imperfective infinitive.
5. **Talking about the future?** Decide aspect first, then build the form — auxiliary plus infinitive for imperfective, conjugated verb alone for perfective.
