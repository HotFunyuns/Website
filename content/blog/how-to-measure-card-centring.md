Centring is the one condition factor you can measure at home with real precision, because it is arithmetic rather than judgement. You are comparing four borders against each other and turning them into two ratios per face. Everything else a grader looks at — surface, corners, edges — needs an eye and a light. Centring needs a straight-on photograph and a bit of division.

That makes it the obvious thing to check first when you are sorting a pile of duplicates, and the obvious thing to get wrong if you photograph the card at an angle.

## The arithmetic, once

Take the left border and the right border, measured in the same units. Add them. Express each as a percentage of that total.

Left 1.1 mm, right 0.9 mm → total 2.0 mm → **55/45**.

Do the same top to bottom. Then turn the card over and do both again, because the back is printed and cut in the same pass but does not shift identically.

That gives you four numbers. Convention writes the larger side first, so 55/45 and 60/40 describe how far the printing has drifted from centre, and 50/50 is perfect.

The units genuinely do not matter. Millimetres, sixteenths of an inch, pixels on a photograph — a ratio cancels the units out. This is why measuring from an image works at all, and why a high-resolution photograph is not required: what you need is a *straight* photograph, not a large one.

## Why both faces, and why people skip the back

Card stock is printed in sheets and cut, and the front and back artwork are registered to each other imperfectly. The practical result is that a card can be visibly fine face-up and visibly shifted face-down.

Graders look at both. Collectors, left to their own habits, look at the front, because the front is the side that gets displayed and the side that photographs well for a listing. If you are screening your own copies, doing the back is the step that most often changes the answer — two cards that looked identical on the front separate immediately once you turn them over.

## How much of the scale centring can move

Centring is not a pass/fail gate; it sits alongside surface, corners and edges, and it can quietly cap a card that is otherwise excellent.

CGC publishes its grading scale openly, and two points on it are worth knowing because they bracket the range. Its **Pristine 10** — reserved for cards that are flawless under ten-times magnification — is described as having **50/50 centring**. Much further down the same scale, a **Very Good+** card may have **90/10 centring** alongside four rounded corners.

PSA publishes its own grading standards separately, describing a scale that runs from Gem Mint 10 down to Poor 1.

The reason to read those pages rather than rely on hobby folklore is that the thresholds are specific, they differ between companies, and they are revised. A number you half-remember from a forum is not a standard. The published page is.

The honest framing for a home measurement is therefore narrow and still useful: **a ratio tells you where a card sits relative to your other copies, and whether it is obviously outside the range the published standards describe for the grades you care about.** It does not tell you what grade anything will receive.

## Photographing a card so the measurement means something

Four rules, in descending order of how much they matter.

**1. Shoot straight down.** This is 90 per cent of it. A camera held even slightly off-axis makes the near border look wider than the far one — parallax — and the resulting ratio is confidently wrong and perfectly repeatable, which is the worst combination. Phone flat, card flat, lens over the centre of the card.

**2. Flatten the card and the background.** A card resting on a slightly curved surface, or held in fingers, bends enough to distort the border on one side. A hard flat surface, card face-up, nothing under a corner.

**3. Diffuse the light.** Direct light produces glare on foil and gloss, and glare eats the border edge exactly where you need to see it. Indirect daylight or a lamp bounced off a wall beats an overhead spotlight or a flash every time.

**4. Fill the frame, but do not crop.** Get close enough that the card occupies most of the image and far enough that the whole card including all four borders is inside it. A cropped edge cannot be measured.

Notice that none of this is about equipment. A modern phone camera has far more resolution than the measurement needs. What no camera can supply is any assurance that you held it level — that part is on you.

## The conditions that break the measurement

Being specific about failure modes is more useful than another feature list, so here are the ones that actually cause trouble.

- **Angled photographs.** Covered above, and still the number one cause of a wrong ratio.
- **Heavy glare on foil.** A reverse-holo or full-art surface can throw enough light to wash out a border. Move the light, not the card.
- **Very dark borders on a dark background.** The edge has to be visible to be aligned against. A plain light surface under a black-bordered card solves this in one step.
- **Sleeved cards.** A sleeve edge reads as a border. Take it out, or accept that the numbers are describing the sleeve.
- **Cards with deliberate asymmetry in the design.** Some printings have artwork that is not symmetric within the border, which fools the eye but not the measurement — measure the border, not the picture.
