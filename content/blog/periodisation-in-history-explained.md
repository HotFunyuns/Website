Periodisation is the practice of cutting continuous time into named stretches so that change can be described. Nobody in 1350 knew they were living in the Middle Ages; the label was applied later, by people with a reason for applying it. Every period boundary you have ever been taught was drawn by someone, for a purpose, using one kind of evidence rather than another — which means periods are arguments you are allowed to interrogate, not facts about the past that were waiting to be found.

## What a period claims

A period makes a specific and testable claim: that the stretch of time inside it has more in common with itself than with the time on either side. That is why the boundaries move. When new evidence shows that an institution, a technology or a social arrangement ran straight through a supposed dividing line, the line has failed to do its job and someone will propose a better one.

The standard European sequence — antiquity, the Middle Ages, modernity — carries an evaluative charge in its own vocabulary. A "middle" age is defined by what it sits between, and the word was applied by scholars who considered the period a decline from one classical peak and a prelude to their own recovery. The category began life as a verdict.

## The best-known boundary, and a serious objection to it

Jacques Le Goff spent a career inside medieval Europe and ended it arguing that one of its boundaries should go. In *Must We Divide History into Periods?*, his last book, he points out that the notion of the Renaissance as a luminous era marking a decisive break with the past only became standard in the nineteenth century. Many of the innovations popularly credited to the Renaissance, he argues, have medieval roots, and many of the worst features of medieval society carried on through it. His alternative is a long Middle Ages running to the middle of the eighteenth century, punctuated by several renaissances rather than one.

The part of his argument that generalises is the closing move. Le Goff does not say periodisation should be abandoned; he says it is necessary, and that the continuities that matter only become visible over a long perspective, because genuine turning points — the shifts that really do end one period and begin another — are much rarer than the conventional scheme suggests. A period scheme with many boundaries is not more precise. It is more confident.

Because the existing survey of [the major eras of world history](/blog/major-eras-of-world-history/) sets out what the standard labels contain, this article deliberately does not restate them. The question here is who drew them.

## A boundary you can stand on

It is easier to see what a period boundary is made of at a single site. UNESCO's description of Çatalhöyük, on the southern Anatolian plateau, records eighteen levels of Neolithic occupation in the eastern mound between 7400 BC and 6200 BC, and describes the western mound as showing the Chalcolithic period from 6200 BC to 5200 BC. The property is valued, in the same description, as evidence of the transition from settled villages to urban agglomeration, maintained in the same location for over 2,000 years.

Read those two facts together. One continuously inhabited place is split by a named boundary at about 6200 BC. The boundary is real in the sense that something in the material record changes there. It is also a decision: it privileges a particular class of evidence, and it turns a continuity that the site itself demonstrates into two ages with different names. Anyone using those labels is using someone's judgement about which change mattered most.

## Institutions carry periodisations, and act on them

Period schemes are not only in textbooks. They are in the structure of research funding, museum galleries, university posts and library classifications, and once they are there they shape which questions get asked.

Dumbarton Oaks, for instance, describes its Byzantine Studies programme — running since 1940 — as supporting historical, philological, art historical, archaeological and theological research into the civilization of the Byzantine Empire from the fourth to the fifteenth century, together with its interactions with neighbouring cultures. That is a perfectly defensible working definition, and it is also a decision with consequences: it determines which centuries a fellowship can cover and where the interaction with neighbours begins to count as someone else's subject. The same is true of every gallery label that says "Medieval Europe" over a case.

The most deliberate modern example of an institution rebuilding a periodisation is UNESCO's *General History of Africa*. UNESCO records that the initiative was launched in 1964, as African nations gained independence, bringing together African and international experts to reframe the continent's history from an African perspective, and it now carries a programme for bringing that history into national education systems. Reframing on that scale is not decoration. Deciding that African history has its own shape — rather than appearing as an appendix inside periods designed for Europe — is a periodisation choice with a stated purpose.

## What a period label hides

Every period boundary suppresses something. Choosing political events as the hinge hides economic continuity. Choosing technology hides institutions. Choosing one region's dates hides everywhere else, which is the standard failure of global surveys built on European turning points.

Selection is unavoidable, and it is the same problem the Historical Thinking Project describes for historical significance: the past is everything that happened to anyone anywhere, there is far too much of it to keep, and so choices have to be made about what is worth remembering. Its own summary notes that significance depends on perspective and purpose, and that what historians count as significant has changed — poor, rural and female lives are now treated as historically significant where once they were not. Period schemes embed exactly those choices, usually invisibly.

## Periodisation in a timeline app, seen honestly

Any chronology tool has to commit to a period scheme in order to have filter buttons at all, and the commitment is visible in the interface. World History Timeline Sim is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and it uses eight eras whose year bands deliberately overlap: the classical band runs from 500 BCE to 500 CE, the late antique band from 300 to 700, the medieval band from 500 to 1500. A century can therefore appear under two labels.

That overlap is the honest option, and it is worth understanding rather than treating as a bug. It concedes that the transitions are gradual and regionally uneven, which is exactly what the scholarship says. What it cannot do is tell you which label is right for a given question, because that depends on the question. The app is a learning and exploration tool whose entries carry no citations and no external review, and its own disclaimer screen says it is not a substitute for textbooks, peer-reviewed scholarship or primary sources. For a worked case of how much a period boundary can be argued over, [the four explanations for why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/) shows the same century being made to carry a collapse, a transformation or nothing much at all.

## Using periods on purpose

The practical habit is small. When you meet a period label, ask three things: what evidence drew the boundary, what it was designed to explain, and whether it was built for the region you are applying it to. If it was built somewhere else for something else, say so and use it anyway if it helps — knowingly.
