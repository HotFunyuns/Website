Boxing allows one weapon — the gloved fist, landing above the belt — in three-minute rounds, up to twelve of them, with a count after every knockdown. MMA allows punches, kicks, knees, elbows, takedowns and submissions in five-minute rounds, one to five of them and never more than 25 minutes, in gloves of 4 to 8 ounces rather than boxing's 8 or 10, and with no count when someone goes down. Both use three judges and the 10-point must system, but they ask the judges to reward different things.

Each of those differences changes what a good fighter does, which is the more useful way to read the rules. The comparison below uses the Association of Boxing Commissions' unified rules for each sport (the MMA document in its current version, amended 5 August 2026, which reads the same as the 2025 text on every point compared here), Nevada's regulations where a commission sets the detail, and the WBC's rules where a sanctioning body does.

## The rules side by side

| | Boxing | MMA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Round length | 3 minutes | 5 minutes |
| Rest between rounds | 1 minute | 1 minute |
| Rounds per bout | Fixed in advance, up to 12; WBC men's world title fights are 12 | 1 to 5; Nevada caps non-title bouts at 3 and title bouts at 5 |
| Most fighting time in one bout | 36 minutes | 25 minutes |
| Gloves (Nevada) | 8 oz at 135 lb or less, 10 oz above (8 oz allowed up to 147 lb by agreement) | 4 to 8 oz |
| Legal weapons | Punches with the gloved fist | Strikes, takedowns and submissions, minus 26 listed fouls |
| Off-limits targets | Below the belt, back of the head and neck, deliberate kidney punches | Back of the head and spine, groin, directed throat strikes; kicks and knees to a grounded fighter's head |
| The clinch | Holding is a foul; the referee breaks it | A fighting position; the referee separates only when nobody is trying to finish |
| The canvas | A boxer touching it with anything but the feet is down; nobody fights there | The fight continues on the ground |
| After a knockdown | Mandatory eight count | No count |
| Endings | KO, TKO, decision, draw, disqualification, no decision | The same, plus submission, and a no contest instead of a no decision |
| Arena | Ring | Ring or fenced cage |

Most of the table follows from one design choice. Boxing removed the floor from the fight; MMA kept it. The rest of the rules exist to make each of those choices workable.

## Time: why 36 minutes and 25 minutes feel so different

The arithmetic is simple. Twelve three-minute rounds hold 36 minutes of boxing, and with eleven one-minute rests a fight that goes the distance lasts 47 minutes. Five five-minute rounds hold 25 minutes of MMA; with four rests, 29 minutes. A three-round MMA fight, the Nevada cap for non-title bouts, is 15 minutes of action.

Two consequences follow, and both are about how the clock is spent rather than how much of it there is.

**MMA rounds are long enough to change position several times.** Five minutes gives a wrestler time to take an opponent down, hold them there and work, and gives the opponent time to get back up. The ABC's rule on standing fighters up addresses this: the referee restarts the fight when neither fighter shows real effort to finish, and merely holding a dominant position does not count as effort.

**Boxing resets more often.** A boxer gets a rest every three minutes, and every round is scored separately, so a bad round costs at most a 10-9 unless there was a knockdown or overwhelming dominance. The round-by-round logic is laid out in [how boxing scoring works](/blog/boxing-scoring-explained/).

## Gloves: shield and weapon

Nevada sets boxing gloves at 8 ounces for boxers weighing in at 135 pounds or less and 10 ounces above that, with 8 ounces allowed up to 147 pounds if both boxers agree. The WBC's championship rules split the same way, by division: 8 ounces up to and including welterweight, 10 ounces from super welterweight upward. MMA gloves in Nevada weigh 4 to 8 ounces.

A heavy glove is a shield as well as a weapon, and much of boxing defence uses it: a high guard, punches caught on the gloves and forearms. A light MMA glove covers far less, and it has to leave the hand free to grab, because gripping is part of the sport. The MMA rules even distinguish the two: holding an opponent's glove is a foul, but gripping the hand inside it is allowed.

## The clinch and the floor

In boxing, holding is a foul. Nevada's list includes holding with one hand while hitting with the other and holding or deliberately maintaining a clinch, and the ABC's judge manual tells judges that holding or clinching to avoid contact is not good defence. A boxer who falls, slips or is wrestled to the floor is either down or getting up; there is no fighting there.

In MMA the clinch is where much of the fight happens: knees, short strikes, takedown attempts and fence work. On the ground, fighters may strike and attempt submissions, with limits: no kicks, knees or stomps to a grounded opponent's head, and no strikes to the back of the head or spine. Who controls where the fight happens is the subject of [striking versus grappling in MMA](/blog/striking-vs-grappling-in-mma/).

## Knockdowns: a count versus a continuation

A boxing knockdown triggers a procedure: neutral corner, a count, a mandatory eight even for a boxer who rises immediately, and no saving by the bell. Nevada's counting rules state that they do not apply to mixed martial arts.

That absence changes the incentives. A boxer who drops an opponent must step away and wait. An MMA fighter who drops an opponent can follow them to the canvas, and the referee's question becomes whether the downed fighter is still intelligently defending themselves. An MMA knockdown can therefore be followed at once by a finish on the ground, while a boxing knockdown always produces a pause.

## What the judges reward

Both sports score with three judges under the 10-point must system: ten points to the round's winner, nine or fewer to the loser. The criteria are where they part.

The ABC's boxing judge manual lists four factors: clean and effective punching, effective aggressiveness, ring generalship, and defence. Moving forward only counts if it produces results; a pressure fighter who keeps getting beaten to the punch is not being effectively aggressive.

The ABC's MMA rules are explicitly ranked. Judges assess effective striking and grappling first; effective aggressiveness and control of the fighting area come into play only if that first assessment is even. A 10-8 in MMA is defined by "damage, dominance, and duration", while in boxing a knockdown normally produces one. The MMA side is covered in depth in [how MMA judging works](/blog/how-mma-judging-criteria-work/).

## Fouls that exist in only one sport

Some fouls are shared: head butts, strikes to the back of the head, groin attacks, eye gouging or thumbing, biting, and hitting after the bell. Others only make sense in one rulebook.

**Boxing only** (Nevada's list): holding and hitting; pivot blows; deliberate kidney punches; crouching below the opponent's belt; hitting an opponent whose head is outside the ropes. Boxing also treats low blows differently: in Nevada a boxer cannot win a fight on the strength of a low-blow claim, and a boxer who will not continue because of one loses by technical knockout.

**MMA only** (ABC list): small-joint manipulation of fingers and toes; fish hooking; pile-driving an opponent onto the head or neck; kicks, knees and stomps to a grounded opponent; and grabbing the fence with fingers or toes.

Division names overlap too, but the numbers do not: boxing's lightweight limit is 135 pounds, while MMA's lightweight division runs from 145 to 155.
