Essere vs avere is the question that comes up as soon as you start the Italian past tense. The passato prossimo is built from an auxiliary plus a past participle, and the auxiliary is either *avere*, "to have", or *essere*, "to be". English uses "have" for everything ("I have eaten", "I have gone"), but Italian says *ho mangiato* and *sono andato*. This comparison covers which verbs take which auxiliary, what changes in the participle, and the cases where the rules blur.

## Avere: the default for most verbs

Wikipedia's article on Italian conjugation states the main rule plainly: **all transitive verbs and most intransitive verbs** form the present perfect with *avere* in the present tense plus the past participle.

| | avere + amato |
| --- | --- |
| io | ho amato |
| tu | hai amato |
| lui / lei / Lei | ha amato |
| noi | abbiamo amato |
| voi | avete amato |
| loro / Loro | hanno amato |

A transitive verb is one that can take a direct object: *ho visto il ragazzo*, "I saw the boy". Wikipedia's article on unaccusative verbs gives *Ha telefonato*, "he has phoned", as an intransitive example that also takes *avere*.

## Essere: motion, change, reflexives

The same article says a **small number of intransitive verbs** use *essere* instead: *essere* itself, and verbs indicating **motion**, such as *venire* "to come", *andare* "to go" and *arrivare* "to arrive". Wikipedia's Italian grammar article adds two more groups:

- **Reflexive verbs** always take *essere*: *mi sono lavato*, "I washed myself"; *ci siamo visti*, "we saw each other".
- **Unaccusative verbs**, which the article describes as typically non-agentive verbs of motion and change of state, such as *cadere* "to fall" and *morire* "to die". Things that happen to the subject rather than things the subject does.

The passive is formed with *essere* too: *Il cancello è stato appena aperto*, "the gate has just been opened". Note that *essere* takes itself as auxiliary, and its participle is *stato*, shared with *stare*.

Wikipedia's article on unaccusative verbs puts the split in a wider frame. In languages with two auxiliaries, such as Italian, French and German, **unaccusative verbs combine with "be" and unergative verbs with "have"**. Its paired Italian examples are *È arrivato*, literally "he is arrived", against *Ha telefonato*, "he has phoned".

## Agreement: the participle that changes

The choice of auxiliary is not only about the helper verb. It decides whether the participle changes.

**With essere, the participle agrees with the subject** in gender and number, like an adjective. Wikipedia's table for *arrivare*:

| | essere + arrivato |
| --- | --- |
| io | sono arrivato / arrivata |
| tu | sei arrivato / arrivata |
| lui / lei / Lei | è arrivato / arrivata |
| noi | siamo arrivati / arrivate |
| voi | siete arrivati / arrivate |
| loro / Loro | sono arrivati / arrivate |

The four endings follow nouns and adjectives: *-o* masculine singular, *-a* feminine singular, *-i* masculine plural, *-e* feminine plural. The Italian grammar article's example is *egli è partito; ella è partita*, "he left; she left".

**With avere, the participle normally stays in -o.** Wikipedia's conjugation article gives one exception: a third-person direct-object pronoun placed **before** the verb. Then the participle agrees with that object:

- *ho visto la ragazza*, "I saw the girl", but *l'ho vista*, "I saw her".
- *Hai mangiato la mela? Sì, l'ho mangiata.* "Have you eaten the apple? Yes, I've eaten it."

With a first- or second-person object pronoun, Wikipedia says the agreement is optional: *non mi ha chiamato* or *chiamata*.

## Choosing the auxiliary, worked through

Run five sentences through the rules above.

1. "Anna went to Rome." *Andare* is a verb of motion → *essere*; Anna is feminine singular → **Anna è andata a Roma**.
2. "Marco and Luca arrived." Motion → *essere*; masculine plural → **Marco e Luca sono arrivati**.
3. "We (two women) washed ourselves." Reflexive → *essere*; feminine plural → **ci siamo lavate**.
4. "I read the letter." *Leggere* is transitive → *avere*; the object follows the verb, so no agreement → **ho letto la lettera**.
5. "I read it (the letter)." *Avere*, but now the third-person pronoun *la* comes first → **l'ho letta**.

Of the five, three take *essere* and two take *avere*, but every participle that changed did so for one of two stated reasons: an *essere* subject, or a preceding third-person object pronoun. With four endings for each *essere* participle, *arrivato, arrivata, arrivati, arrivate*, there are 4 forms to choose from, and the subject always tells you which.

## Where the rule runs out

Wikipedia is candid that **a reliable rule cannot be given** for the perfect tenses of intransitive verbs. It offers a rule of thumb, citing the Accademia della Crusca: if a verb's participle can work as an adjective, it takes *essere*. Its next sentence, on reflexive and unaccusative verbs, carries a citation-needed tag, with a note that *andare* is a counterexample. Some verbs take either: *io ho vissuto* and *io sono vissuto* both mean "I have lived". The article on unaccusative verbs adds that a single verb may combine with either auxiliary depending on meaning, context or regional variation, and that synonymous verbs in different languages do not always choose the same one.

The practical approach that follows from this is to learn each common intransitive verb together with its auxiliary, as a pair.
