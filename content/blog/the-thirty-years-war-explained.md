The Thirty Years' War was fought primarily in Central Europe from 1618 to 1648, and Wikipedia ranks it among the most destructive conflicts in European history. It began as a revolt in Bohemia against a Habsburg king and ended as a struggle between France, Sweden, Spain and the Emperor, settled by the Peace of Westphalia. Its two most quoted features — that it was a war of religion, and the scale of its death toll — both need qualifying, so this guide treats them carefully.

## A settlement that could not hold

The war's roots lie in the Reformation's aftermath, covered in [the Protestant Reformation](/blog/the-protestant-reformation/). Wikipedia explains that the 1555 Peace of Augsburg tried to fix the boundaries between faiths in the Holy Roman Empire: under *cuius regio, eius religio*, each state was Lutheran or Catholic according to its ruler. It was undermined by the spread of Protestantism after 1555, especially Calvinism, which both Lutherans and Catholics viewed with hostility.

The Empire's structure made tensions hard to manage. Wikipedia counts 300 imperial estates represented in the Imperial Diet — from seven prince-electors down to prince-bishoprics and free cities — and a Diet that met infrequently and focused on discussion rather than legislation. Above them sat the Habsburgs, emperors since 1440, whose Spanish branch was simultaneously fighting the Dutch.

## Windows at Prague Castle, May 1618

Ferdinand, elected king of Bohemia in 1617, had eliminated Protestantism in Styria within 18 months of taking control there in 1595, which made Bohemian Protestants doubt his promise to respect their freedoms. In May 1618 Protestant nobles led by Count Thurn met Ferdinand's two Catholic representatives at Prague Castle and threw them, with their secretary, out of the windows. All three survived. Wikipedia's infobox gives the war's start as 23 May 1618, though a footnote records that some commentators date it to the War of the Jülich Succession in 1609.

Events then escalated:

- **August 1619.** The Bohemian Estates rescinded Ferdinand's election and offered the crown to the Protestant Frederick V of the Palatinate; on 28 August Ferdinand was elected Holy Roman Emperor anyway.
- **November 1620.** The Bohemian army was destroyed at White Mountain and Frederick fled.
- **1625–1629.** Christian IV of Denmark intervened, was defeated, and under the June 1629 Treaty of Lübeck abandoned support for the German Protestants; Albrecht von Wallenstein led the imperial advance.
- **1630–1632.** Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden landed in Pomerania in June 1630 with nearly 18,000 troops, won at Breitenfeld in September 1631, and was killed at Lützen in 1632.
- **1635.** The Peace of Prague largely ended the war as a German civil war — and France then entered openly against the Habsburgs.

## Religion started it; rivalry spread it

Wikipedia's summary is precise on this. Religion, combined with disagreements over the limits of imperial authority, "was thus an important factor in starting the war". However, "its scope and extent were largely the consequence of external drivers such as the French–Habsburg rivalry and the Dutch Revolt."

The best evidence is the alliance pattern. France — whose king, Louis XIII, Wikipedia calls strongly Catholic — financed Sweden through Cardinal Richelieu under the 1631 Treaty of Bärwalde, and Wikipedia notes that Lutheran Saxony backed the Catholic Ferdinand in 1620 in return for Lusatia. Wikipedia adds that after 1635 the war became one theatre of a wider struggle between France and Sweden on one side and Emperor Ferdinand III and Spain on the other.

## Counting the dead without exaggerating

Wikipedia gives estimates of 4.5 to 8 million deaths, mostly civilians and mostly after Sweden entered in 1630. It also warns against inflated numbers: claims of up to 12 million deaths from a population of 18 million "are no longer considered realistic", and nineteenth-century German nationalists often exaggerated the war's impact to illustrate the dangers of a divided Germany.

The causes of civilian death matter as much as the totals. Local returns cited by Wikipedia attribute 64% of civilian deaths to bubonic plague, 12% to starvation, 5% to dysentery, 4% to typhus and only 3% to military action.

Population change is also not the same as deaths. Take the town figures Wikipedia gives for 1620 to 1650:

| Town | 1620 | 1650 | Fall |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Augsburg | 48,000 | 21,000 | 56.3% |
| Munich | 22,000 | 17,000 | 22.7% |

Augsburg lost 27,000 people, but Wikipedia notes that many were not dead — they emigrated as trade declined. For the German lands as a whole, a fall from 18–20 million to 11–13 million is anywhere from 27.8% (18 to 13) to 45% (20 to 11), so the "roughly 40%" decline that Wikipedia says modern historians generally agree on sits inside a wide band. The same caution applies to modern wars, as [why the Second World War death toll is a range](/blog/second-world-war-death-toll-range/) explains. For soldiers the record is grim even at village level: of 230 men conscripted from the Swedish village of Bygdeå between 1621 and 1639, 215 — 93.5% — are recorded as dead or missing.

Wikipedia also places the war within what some historians call the "General Crisis" of the mid-seventeenth century, alongside upheaval from the British Isles to [Ming China](/blog/the-ming-dynasty-explained/).

## Reading the Peace of Westphalia in its own words

Talks began in 1642 in two Westphalian towns, Münster and Osnabrück, with 109 delegations attending at one time or another. Wikipedia describes three agreements: the Peace of Münster between Spain and the Dutch Republic, signed 30 January 1648, confirming Dutch independence; and the Empire's treaties with Sweden at Osnabrück and with France at Münster, which Ferdinand III signed on 24 October — after a Swedish force had seized Prague Castle and the Malá Strana district in July, though not the Old Town, and after a Spanish defeat at Lens in August.

The treaty text with France, published by Yale's Avalon Project, closes "at Munster in Westphalia, the 24th Day of October, 1648". Its first articles promise "a Christian and Universal Peace" and "a perpetual Oblivion, Amnesty, or Pardon of all that has been committed since the beginning of these Troubles". It is worth reading a few pages directly; [how to analyse a primary source](/blog/how-to-analyse-a-primary-source/) suggests what to look for.

Wikipedia lists the key terms as autonomy for the Empire's states, the supremacy of the Imperial Diet, 1624 as the "Normaljahr" fixing each state's dominant religion, recognition of Calvinism, and gains for Sweden (including five million thalers), France and Brandenburg-Prussia. Pope Innocent X denounced the terms. Wikipedia notes the claim that Westphalia founded the principle of non-interference known as "Westphalian sovereignty" has since been challenged — and that fighting did not stop at once: the last Swedish garrison left Germany in 1654, and France fought Spain until 1659.
