Every buy or sell starts with a choice: what kind of order to place. The two basic types make opposite promises. A **market order** promises that your trade will happen, but not at what price. A **limit order** promises a price, but not that the trade will happen. The SEC's investor education office describes both, and the differences explain many surprises on a first trading screen.

## The market order: execution first

A market order is an order to buy or sell at the best available price, right away. The SEC's bulletin *Understanding Order Types* says it generally executes immediately, but its price is not guaranteed. Investor.gov's glossary adds that unless you specify otherwise, a broker will enter your order as a market order.

Two cautions from the SEC are worth memorising:

1. **The last traded price is not necessarily your price.** A market order to buy generally executes at or near the current ask, and a market order to sell at or near the current bid, not at the "last" price shown in large type. The gap between bid and ask is the [bid–ask spread](/blog/bid-ask-spread-explained/).
2. **Large orders can fill in pieces.** The bulletin's example: a market order for 1,000 shares placed when the best offer is $3.00 might fill 500 shares at $3.00 and the other 500 at a higher price, because only so many shares were offered at $3.00.

In a fast-moving market, the price can change between pressing buy and the order reaching the market, as described in [how the stock market works](/blog/how-the-stock-market-works/).

## The limit order: price first

A limit order is an order to buy or sell at a specific price **or better**:

- A **buy limit** order can only execute at the limit price or lower.
- A **sell limit** order can only execute at the limit price or higher.

The SEC's example: an investor who wants to buy ABC stock for no more than $10 can submit a limit order at $10, and it will only execute if ABC trades at $10 or lower. The trade-off is stated just as plainly: a limit order is not guaranteed to execute. It can only be filled if the market price reaches the limit, and even then there may not be enough shares at that price to fill the whole order.

## The two side by side

| | Market order | Limit order |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Promise | Execution | Price no worse than the limit |
| Risk | Paying more, or receiving less, than expected | Not being filled at all, or only partly |
| Typical fill | At or near the ask (buying) or bid (selling) | At the limit or better, if the price is reached |
| Useful when | Getting in or out matters more than a few cents | The price matters more than the timing |
| Weak spot | Fast markets, wide spreads, thinly traded stocks | Prices that move away and never come back |

## A worked example

A stock shows a bid of $49.95 and an ask of $50.05, with a last trade at $50.00. You want 100 shares.

- **Market order to buy:** you would expect to pay about $50.05 a share, the ask, so roughly $5,005 before costs. If the ask moves to $50.20 before your order arrives, you pay that instead.
- **Buy limit at $50.00:** nothing happens unless someone is willing to sell at $50.00 or less. If the price rises to $51 and stays there, you never buy. If it dips to $49.90, you may buy at $49.90, better than your limit.

Neither order is "right". The first values getting the shares; the second values the price.

## Partial fills and how long an order lasts

Both order types can fill in parts. A partially filled limit order leaves the remainder open. How long it stays open depends on its **time-in-force** instruction. The SEC bulletin explains that unless you specify otherwise, orders are **day orders**, which generally cancel at the end of regular trading hours, while a **good-'til-cancelled** order lasts until it is filled or cancelled, subject to the broker's own time limits. [Time-in-force](/blog/time-in-force-order-types/) covers those instructions, including immediate-or-cancel and fill-or-kill.

## Stop orders build on these two

Stop orders combine a trigger with one of these two types. A stop order becomes a market order when its stop price is reached; a stop-limit order becomes a limit order. The same trade-off carries over: [stop orders versus stop-limit orders](/blog/stop-order-vs-stop-limit-order/) explains what happens to each when a price jumps.
