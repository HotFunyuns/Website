Thai has five tones and four tone marks. That mismatch is not an oversight in the writing system — it is the writing system, and once you understand why the arithmetic does not work, the whole thing becomes learnable rather than arbitrary.

The short version: a Thai tone mark is one input among four. The other three are the consonant you started with, the vowel you used, and how the syllable ends. Beginners who try to learn the four marks and stop are learning a quarter of a rule.

## The five tones

Standard Thai distinguishes **mid**, **low**, **falling**, **high** and **rising**. These are contrastive: change only the tone and you have a different word.

The cleanest demonstration in the language uses four words that every Thai romanisation writes identically as *mai*:

| Thai | Tone | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ไม่ | falling | not |
| ไม้ | high | wood |
| ใหม่ | low | new |
| ไหม | rising | silk; also a question particle |

Four different words. One romanisation. This is why a phrasebook cannot teach you Thai pronunciation, and it is why the script is worth the effort — the spelling of each of those four tells a reader exactly which tone it takes.

A second classic set, this time with a long vowel:

| Thai | Tone | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| เสือ | rising | tiger |
| เสื่อ | low | mat |
| เสื้อ | falling | shirt, clothing |

Same consonant, same vowel, three tones, three unrelated meanings.

## The four inputs

Here is the actual rule, in the order you apply it.

### 1. The class of the initial consonant

Every Thai consonant letter belongs to one of three **tone classes**: mid, high or low. The class is not something you can see from the shape of the letter; it is a property you learn along with the sound.

The inventory splits unevenly:

- **Mid class — 9 letters:** ก จ ฎ ฏ ด ต บ ป อ
- **High class — 11 letters:** ข ฃ ฉ ฐ ถ ผ ฝ ศ ษ ส ห
- **Low class — the remaining 24**

Nine plus eleven is twenty, and the consonant inventory is usually counted at 44, which leaves 24 in the low class. That is why "low class" is the default you can assume when you have not learned a letter's class yet — it is more than half the alphabet.

The mid and high sets are small enough to learn as lists, and doing that early is the highest-value memorisation in the whole script. Everything else is low.

This is also the answer to the question every beginner asks about Thai: why 44 consonant letters for far fewer distinct sounds? Because several letters share a pronunciation and differ only in class, and class changes the tone of the syllable. The apparent redundancy is where a large part of the tone information lives.

### 2. Whether the syllable is live or dead

Thai grammar calls these คำเป็น (live) and คำตาย (dead).

- A **live** syllable ends in a long vowel, or in a nasal or glide sound — the finals written with letters for *m*, *n*, *ng*, *w* and *y*.
- A **dead** syllable ends in a stop — the finals pronounced *p*, *t* or *k* — or in a short vowel with nothing after it.

The test is about how the syllable stops, not about which letters are visible, which is why it has to be learned alongside the vowel system rather than as a separate topic.

### 3. Vowel length

Long or short. This only becomes decisive in one branch of the rules, but it is a genuine distinction in Thai vowels generally, and mishearing it is a common source of beginner errors that have nothing to do with tone.

### 4. The tone mark, if there is one

There are four, and they are written above the initial consonant:

| Mark | Thai name |
| --- | --- |
| ก่ | ไม้เอก (mai ek) |
| ก้ | ไม้โท (mai tho) |
| ก๊ | ไม้ตรี (mai tri) |
| ก๋ | ไม้จัตวา (mai chattawa) |

The ก above is a placeholder consonant so the marks have something to sit on — they never appear alone.

Note the names. They are ordinal: first mark, second mark, third mark, fourth mark. They are not named after tones, because they do not correspond to tones.

## The rules, in two tables

Most Thai words carry no tone mark at all, so start with those.

### Unmarked syllables

| Initial class | Live syllable | Dead syllable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mid | mid tone | low tone |
| High | rising tone | low tone |
| Low | mid tone | short vowel: high tone · long vowel: falling tone |

That single table covers the majority of Thai words you will read. It is worth over-learning.

### Marked syllables

| Initial class | ไม้เอก | ไม้โท | ไม้ตรี | ไม้จัตวา |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mid | low | falling | high | rising |
| High | low | falling | — | — |
| Low | falling | high | — | — |

Look at the first column. **ไม้เอก produces a low tone after a mid or high consonant and a falling tone after a low one.** The same mark, two different tones, decided by a letter written before it. That is the whole reason four marks can serve five tones, and it is the fact that makes "just learn the tone marks" bad advice.

The dashes are also informative: mid-class initials are the only ones that take all four marks. ไม้ตรี and ไม้จัตวา are essentially restricted to mid-class syllables in native Thai vocabulary, which is another reason the nine mid-class letters repay early attention.

## The two workarounds the script built in

Once you see the tables, an obvious gap appears. The low class has 24 letters, and several of its sounds have no high-class counterpart at all — there is no high-class letter for *m*, *n*, *ng*, *l*, *w* or *y*. So how do you write those sounds with a rising tone, which only high-class initials produce unmarked?

Thai solves this with two silent leading consonants.

**Leading ห (ho nam).** Put ห in front of one of those sonorant letters and the pair behaves as a high-class initial. The ห is not pronounced. This is why หมา ("dog") has a rising tone while มา ("to come") has a mid tone — same consonant, same vowel, one silent letter, two words.

Put that beside ม้า ("horse"), which is the same syllable with ไม้โท on a low-class initial, and you get a genuinely useful trio:

| Thai | Tone | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| มา | mid | to come |
| หมา | rising | dog |
| ม้า | high | horse |

**Leading อ (o nam).** A rarer version using อ before ย, which in modern Thai affects a very small closed set of extremely common words: อย่า, อยู่, อย่าง and อยาก. All four take a low tone. Because the set is closed, most courses teach these as four vocabulary items rather than as a rule, which is the sensible approach.

One more structural note: in a genuine consonant cluster, the class of the **first** consonant is what drives the tone. So ปลา ("fish") takes its tone from ป, a mid-class letter, and comes out mid.

## Why this is better news than it sounds

Compare this with how tone works elsewhere. In Cantonese, tone is stored with each word individually and there is nothing in the writing that tells you what it is — which is why [Cantonese tones](/blog/learn-cantonese-beginners-guide/) have to be learned at first exposure, one word at a time, forever.

Thai gives you a machine instead. Once the rules are automatic, you can look at a Thai word you have never encountered and derive its tone from the spelling. That is an enormous long-term advantage, and it converts the script from an obstacle into a tool.

The trade is that the machine takes a few weeks to install. The rules involve holding four things in mind at once, and until they become automatic they feel like arithmetic in the middle of a conversation. This is the stage where learners are most tempted to fall back on romanisation, and it is exactly the wrong moment to do it — the payoff arrives just past the point where the work feels pointless.

## A sequence that works

**Learn each consonant with its class, from the first exposure.** Treat the class as part of the letter, the way you would learn a German noun with its gender. Adding class information to letters you already half-know is much slower than learning them together.

**Memorise the mid and high sets as lists.** Twenty letters. Everything else is low by elimination, and elimination is fast.

**Learn live versus dead alongside the vowels.** The distinction is about syllable endings, so it belongs with vowel and final study rather than in a separate "tones" chapter.

**Derive tones for words you already know by ear.** This is the step most courses skip. Take vocabulary you can already say correctly, work out the tone from the spelling, and check your derivation against what you know. You are verifying the machine on known inputs before trusting it on unknown ones.

**Then read continuous text out loud, badly.** Thai is written without spaces between words, so word boundaries are a separate reading skill, and tone derivation only becomes useful once you can find where a syllable starts.
