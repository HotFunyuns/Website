Research links slow breathing with higher heart rate variability and with people reporting more relaxation, and a 2023 meta-analysis found breathwork was associated with a small-to-medium reduction in self-reported stress; the same authors warned against letting hype run ahead of evidence. Breathwork has, in that meta-analysis's words, "recently received an unprecedented surge in public interest". This guide looks at what the research on slow breathing shows, how strong it is, and where its limits are.

## How slow "slow breathing" is in research

Wikipedia gives the typical resting respiratory rate for a healthy adult as **12 to 15 breaths a minute**, with the brain's respiratory centre setting a quiet rhythm of about two seconds in and three seconds out. Slow breathing in research means something much slower. A 2018 systematic review in *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* defined slow breathing techniques as **fewer than 10 breaths a minute**.

The exercises covered on this site all fall below that line. If one count is one second:

| Exercise | One breath takes | Breaths a minute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical resting breath (2 s in, 3 s out) | 5 seconds | 12 |
| NHS exercise for stress (count 5 in, 5 out) | 10 seconds | 6 |
| Box breathing (4 in, 4 hold, 4 out, 4 hold) | 16 seconds | 3.75 |
| 4-7-8 breathing (4 in, 7 hold, 8 out) | 19 seconds | About 3.2 |

Whatever their names, these exercises share a rhythm far slower than the typical resting rate.

## Findings of the 2018 review and the 2023 meta-analysis

The 2018 review searched two databases and screened 2,461 abstracts, but only **15 studies** met its criteria. Across them, slow breathing was associated with **increased heart rate variability** and respiratory sinus arrhythmia, changes in brain activity on EEG (more alpha and less theta power), and psychological effects that participants reported: more comfort, relaxation, pleasantness, vigour and alertness, and fewer symptoms of arousal, anxiety, depression, anger and confusion. The studies were in healthy people.

A 2023 meta-analysis in *Scientific Reports* went further by pooling randomised controlled trials of breathwork. For self-reported stress, it combined **12 trials with 785 adults** and found breathwork was associated with lower stress than control conditions, a **small-to-medium effect**. Results for anxiety (20 trials) and depressive symptoms (18 trials) were similar in size.

## Cyclic sighing, box breathing and meditation compared

The meta-analysis compared breathwork with non-breathwork controls, not one technique with another. One study that did compare techniques is a 2023 randomised trial in *Cell Reports Medicine*, which compared daily five-minute sessions of cyclic sighing, box breathing and cyclic hyperventilation with retention against mindfulness meditation for a month. Breathwork, **especially exhale-focused cyclic sighing**, improved mood and lowered breathing rate more than meditation did. It is a single, short trial, so it hints that long exhales matter rather than proving it.

## How much practice the studies used

In the studies cited here, the amount of practice was small:

- the 2023 *Cell Reports Medicine* trial used **five minutes a day for a month**;
- a 2017 trial of diaphragmatic breathing used **20 sessions over eight weeks**, at about four breaths a minute;
- a 2022 study of 4-7-8 breathing measured effects straight after **three sets of six cycles**.

The NHS, for its part, suggests at least five minutes of its basic exercise and doing it regularly, as part of a daily routine. These abstracts do not report testing whether more practice brings more benefit, so they do not establish an ideal amount.

## What the research does not support

The same meta-analysis that found benefits also judged most of its studies to be at **moderate risk of bias** and warned of "a miscalibration between hype and evidence". Taken together, the research has clear limits:

- **Short-term, mostly healthy samples.** The individual studies cited in these guides ran from a single session to eight weeks, and most recruited healthy adults.
- **Self-reported outcomes.** The meta-analysis's main outcome was self-reported stress, not a physical measurement.
- **No proof of treatment.** None of this shows that breathing exercises treat anxiety disorders, insomnia, high blood pressure or other conditions.
- **No best technique.** The evidence does not establish one pattern as best for everyone.

## Fitting a few minutes of slow breathing into a day

The NHS describes its breathing exercise as a calming technique that takes just a few minutes, can be done anywhere, and gives the most benefit when done regularly as part of a daily routine. Choose a pattern that feels comfortable, breathe without forcing it, and stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy.
