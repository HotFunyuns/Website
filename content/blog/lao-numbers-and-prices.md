Phrasebooks get travellers as far as the greeting and the order. They tend to stop just before the moment that actually matters, which is when someone says a price out loud and you have about a second to work out whether you understood it.

Numbers are different from phrases in a way that makes them harder, not easier. A phrase is a fixed thing you can memorise. A number is generative: the speaker can produce a value you have never specifically been taught, and you have to build the meaning from parts while they are still talking.

## Two numeral systems, both in daily use

Lao has its own digits. The Unicode standard encodes them consecutively inside the Lao block, running from LAO DIGIT ZERO to LAO DIGIT NINE, which is a reliable indication that they are a live part of the writing system rather than a historical curiosity.

| Value | Lao digit |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | ໐ |
| 1 | ໑ |
| 2 | ໒ |
| 3 | ໓ |
| 4 | ໔ |
| 5 | ໕ |
| 6 | ໖ |
| 7 | ໗ |
| 8 | ໘ |
| 9 | ໙ |

Western digits are also widely used, and you will meet both — sometimes within a few metres of each other. The practical implication is that reading Lao numerals is a recognition skill with a small, finite syllabus: ten shapes, learnable in an afternoon, and then never a problem again.

## One to ten

This part is mercifully regular. The romanisations below are a reading aid only, not a standard — more on why that matters in a moment.

| Value | Lao | Reading aid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | ໜຶ່ງ | nueng |
| 2 | ສອງ | song |
| 3 | ສາມ | sam |
| 4 | ສີ່ | si |
| 5 | ຫ້າ | ha |
| 6 | ຫົກ | hok |
| 7 | ເຈັດ | chet |
| 8 | ແປດ | paet |
| 9 | ເກົ້າ | kao |
| 10 | ສິບ | sip |

The word for one opens with a digraph consonant form that the script treats as a single character — the Unicode chart lists these under a dedicated digraphs heading, and the word for ten thousand further down uses the other one — which is one small reason that [working through the Lao alphabet properly before anything else](/blog/lao-alphabet-explained/) is time well spent. Tone is carried by the interaction of consonant class, tone mark and vowel length rather than by anything visible in a romanisation, so the Latin spellings above will not tell you how to say these correctly on their own.

## Eleven to ninety-nine, and the two things that break

The building rule is straightforward: for the tens, put the multiplier before ສິບ; for the units, put them after.

Then there are two exceptions, and between them they account for most beginner errors.

**Twenty has its own word.** It is ຊາວ (*sao*), not "two-ten". Thirty onwards returns to the regular pattern — ສາມສິບ (*sam sip*) for thirty, and so on up — so this is a single item to memorise rather than a second system.

**The digit one changes shape inside a compound.** Eleven is ສິບເອັດ (*sip et*), not "sip nueng", and twenty-one is ຊາວເອັດ (*sao et*). The form ເອັດ (*et*) appears wherever one is the final unit. If you have drilled ໜຶ່ງ as "the word for one" and nothing else, every number ending in one will briefly stop making sense.

## Above a hundred: the myriad problem

This is where a learner who was doing fine suddenly cannot follow a spoken price, and the cause is structural rather than a lapse in vocabulary.

| Value | Lao | Reading aid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | ຮ້ອຍ | hoi |
| 1,000 | ພັນ | phan |
| 10,000 | ໝື່ນ | muen |
| 100,000 | ແສນ | saen |
| 1,000,000 | ລ້ານ | lan |

English groups large numbers in thousands, so an English speaker hears "fifty thousand" as fifty of one unit. Lao has a single word for ten thousand and another for a hundred thousand, so the same value is assembled differently, and the speaker is not building it out of the parts you are listening for.

The consequence is specific. You can know every word in the table above and still fail to parse a spoken figure, because your ear is segmenting at the wrong places. That is a listening problem, not a vocabulary problem, and it needs listening practice to fix.

## Why prices are where this becomes urgent

Lao prices are quoted in kip, and everyday amounts are long. That single fact reorganises which vocabulary is actually load-bearing: the thousand-and-above words do most of the work at a stall, while one to ten mostly appears inside larger figures rather than on its own.

This is the inverse of how most courses teach numbers, which start small and run out of steam somewhere around a hundred. If you are preparing for a market, a tuk-tuk fare or a guesthouse, drill ພັນ, ໝື່ນ and ແສນ until they are instant, then worry about the fiddly middle.

A related point on comprehension: knowing a number well enough to say it is a much lower bar than recognising it at conversational speed. The two skills need separate practice, which is why [a traveller's phrase list is worth pairing with real listening work](/blog/lao-phrases-for-travellers/) rather than treated as a substitute for it.

## Numbers rarely travel alone

Lao, in common with its neighbours, uses classifiers: counted nouns generally take a classifier alongside the numeral, and the ordering conventions are not the ones English uses. This is not a numbers topic exactly, but it is why a number you have learned in isolation can still come out sounding wrong in a sentence. Treat the numeral as one component of a counting phrase rather than as a standalone word, and learn a handful of common classifiers alongside the numbers themselves.

## Romanisation is a scaffold, not a target

There is no single romanisation of Lao that everyone uses. The Library of Congress maintains an ALA-LC table for cataloguing, first issued earlier and revised in 2012, and it is a genuine standard — but it is a *library* standard, designed for consistent transliteration of text rather than for teaching pronunciation, and it does not match the informal spellings you will see on menus, signs and travel sites.

That divergence is precisely why the script is worth the effort. ໜຶ່ງ is ໜຶ່ງ regardless of who is romanising it. Lao is a Tai-Kadai language and the statutory national language of the Lao People's Democratic Republic, with related varieties spoken across the border region — which means you will encounter its numbers in more than one spelling convention even within the same trip.

## Building the recognition habit

[Learn Lao Language Fast & Easy](/apps/learn-lao/) — our own app, and free with ads and no in-app purchases — puts the script in the first lessons and groups vocabulary by situation rather than alphabetically, with numbers sitting in the same strand as shopping, transport and time. Its practice formats include listening, matching, sentence building and dictation, which is the right mix for this particular topic: numbers need to be heard under time pressure, not just read.

Whatever tool you use, three habits help more than extra vocabulary.

Practise numbers as *audio first*. Read them, then cover the text and listen. The failure mode with numbers is always comprehension speed.

Practise in the ranges you will actually meet. If your trip involves prices, spend your time between a thousand and a hundred thousand, not between one and twenty.

Practise partial numbers. Real listening is rarely clean; you will catch ໝື່ນ and miss what came before it. Getting comfortable asking for a repeat, or asking someone to write the figure down, is a legitimate skill rather than a failure.

The wider [language learning library](/blog/category/language-learning/) covers the listening and review techniques that make this stick. But the specific thing to take away here is that the numbers course you need for Laos is not the numbers course most books give you — it is the one that starts at a thousand.
