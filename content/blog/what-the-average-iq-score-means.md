The average IQ score is 100 because the people who built the scale decided it would be. It is a unit, in the same way that zero degrees Celsius is a unit, and it is re-imposed every time a test is renormed against a fresh sample. Most readers meet the figure as though it were a finding about humanity. It is not; it is the origin of a coordinate system.

Taking that seriously changes several things that otherwise look puzzling — including why a whole century of rising raw performance never moved the average by a single point.

## The mean is set, not found

Reported IQ scores use a standard-score metric with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15 — the convention the psychometric literature states outright, including a 2026 meta-analysis of Wechsler profiles that standardised its comparisons against a control with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15.

Getting there takes one conversion. A publisher administers the battery to a standardisation sample, records how that sample performed at each age, and builds tables that map raw performance onto the fixed metric. The centre of the sample lands on 100 because the table sends it there. So "the average IQ is 100" is not a claim that can be false. It is a description of the tables, and it would be equally true on a scale centred on 500 or on 0.

Anything genuinely informative about a score lives in the comparison the tables encode — who was in the sample, when, and how they performed. That machinery is set out in our explainer on [how an IQ score is calculated](/blog/how-iq-scores-are-calculated/).

## What actually moved during the twentieth century

Raw performance on these tasks rose, substantially, across generations. The best single summary is the 2014 meta-analysis by Trahan, Stuebing, Hiscock and Fletcher in *Psychological Bulletin*, which pooled 285 studies with 14,031 participants, all of them involving people who took two intelligence tests built on different normative bases.

Their headline figure was a mean of **2.31 standard score points per decade**, with a 95 per cent confidence interval of 1.99 to 2.64. For a subset of modern comparisons the estimate was **2.93 IQ points per decade** (95 per cent confidence interval 2.3 to 3.5), which matched the long-standing rule of thumb of roughly three points a decade. The authors reported the pattern as robust across age groups, measures, samples and performance levels.

This is the effect named after James Flynn, who documented it across many countries. The finding is about raw performance, and it is genuinely striking — but it is not a finding about the average score, because the average score cannot move.

## Renorming: where the gain goes

Here is the mechanism that connects the two facts, and it is the part most explanations skip.

When a publisher renorms, it recruits a new standardisation sample and rebuilds the tables so that the new sample's average lands on 100. If the new sample performs better in raw terms than the old one did, then the same raw performance now converts to a *lower* reported score than it used to. The rising tide is absorbed into a stricter conversion rather than a rising mean.

Pearson describes the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, Fifth Edition — published in 2024 and covering ages 16:0 to 90:11 — as carrying updated norms reflecting a contemporary population. That is renorming in its ordinary form, and it is why a score has a shelf life. A result from an earlier edition was a rank against a different group of people, and quoting it years later without saying which edition produced it leaves out the only thing that made it interpretable.

## The drift is not a constant

It is tempting to treat three points a decade as a fixed rate and adjust old scores by it. Recent evidence makes that harder.

Winter, Trudel and Kaufman, writing in the *Journal of Intelligence* in 2024, examined two counterbalanced validity studies reported in the WAIS-5 Technical Manual: 186 adolescents and adults aged 16 to 90 who took both the previous Wechsler adult battery and the WAIS-5, and 98 sixteen-year-olds who took both the children's battery and the WAIS-5. Both studies produced the same unexpected result — approximately **1.2 IQ points per decade**, far below the conventional three.

The authors set out why that matters beyond the psychometric literature. A three-points-per-decade adjustment has been applied in intellectual disability determinations and in capital cases, where a few points can decide eligibility. If the contemporary rate is closer to 1.2, the conventional correction may be too large. They raise the question rather than settling it, and the honest summary is that the size of the effect in current data is unresolved.

## Why "average for my age" is the wrong question

People frequently ask what the average IQ is for a particular age. The answer is 100, for every age, and the reason is structural: the conversion tables are built inside age bands.

A fifty-year-old is compared with fifty-year-olds and a twenty-year-old with twenty-year-olds. Whatever age-related differences exist in raw performance are absorbed by that banding before a score is reported. This is why a stable score across decades does not mean performance was stable — it means performance tracked the age group. Which abilities actually change with age, and how differently, is covered in [fluid and crystallised intelligence](/blog/fluid-and-crystallized-intelligence/).

## What a practice app can and cannot say about "average"

[Mental Math & Memory Games](/apps/mental-math-memory-games/) is our own app, and it does not tell you how you compare with an average — because it has nothing to compare you with.

Everything the app reports is about your own sessions: points scored, accuracy on the questions it generated, longest streak, response time, and personal bests per mode. There is no standardisation sample behind those numbers, no age banding, no conversion table and no population reference. Any app claiming to place you against "the average" without a documented norm sample is describing something it has not collected.

That is a constraint rather than a deficiency, and it keeps the categories where they belong. A drill in Mixed Operations or a set of number-memory rounds is **practice**; a normed battery is **standardised psychological assessment**. The useful comparison in the first case is with yourself last week, on the same mode at the same difficulty — the kind of self-referenced tracking discussed in [building a daily practice habit](/blog/building-a-daily-practice-habit/).

## The short version

The average is 100 by design. Raw performance rose for most of a century, and renorming converted that rise into tougher tables rather than a higher mean. The current rate of drift is contested. And an age-specific average does not exist, because age is already inside the score.
