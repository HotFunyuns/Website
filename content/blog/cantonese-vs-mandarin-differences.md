The question people usually ask is "which one should I learn". The question that determines whether you waste six months is "how much of one gets me the other", and the answer is: much less than the shared writing system makes it look.

Both are varieties within the Chinese language family. Neither descends from the other. In conversation they are not mutually intelligible — a monolingual speaker of one does not follow the other. Everything below is an attempt to say precisely where the difference sits, because "they're different languages" is true and not actionable.

## Sound: the part with no useful overlap

If you take away one thing, take this. The two languages do not share a syllable inventory, a tone system, or a set of possible word endings. Nothing about Mandarin pronunciation carries across.

### Different tone systems, not different tone counts

Mandarin is normally described as having four tones plus a neutral tone. Cantonese is normally described with six contrasting tones, which Jyutping numbers 1 to 6.

It is tempting to read that as "Cantonese has two more", as though the four Mandarin tones sit inside the Cantonese six. They do not. The pitch shapes are defined differently, the contrasts a learner has to hear are different, and there is no mapping from one to the other.

The two systems also behave differently in connected speech. Mandarin has a well-known rule where two consecutive third-tone syllables shift, so 你好 (*nǐ hǎo*) is not said with two identical falling-rising tones. Cantonese has its own tone-change behaviour, which works on different principles and has to be learned as part of the words it affects.

The Cantonese six are worth meeting properly rather than in passing; our [beginner's guide to Cantonese](/blog/learn-cantonese-beginners-guide/) sets them out alongside the "nine tones" figure you will also see quoted.

### Cantonese kept endings Mandarin lost

This is the difference that surprises people who assumed the two were close.

Cantonese syllables can end in **-p, -t, -k, -m, -n or -ng**. Mandarin syllables can end in **-n or -ng** and nothing else, aside from the *r* suffix common in northern speech.

That is not a small phonetic footnote. It means a large number of Cantonese syllables have no Mandarin equivalent at all, and it is why Cantonese poetry rhymes in places Mandarin does not. It also means the very common Cantonese word 唔 (*m4*, "not") is a syllable made entirely of a nasal consonant, with no vowel — a shape Mandarin does not use.

Running the other way, Mandarin has a set of retroflex initials — the sounds written **zh, ch, sh** and **r** in Pinyin — that Cantonese does not have. Each language has sounds the other simply lacks.

## Two romanisations, and you cannot swap them

**Pinyin** is a Mandarin romanisation. It was designed for Mandarin sounds and it encodes Mandarin tones with diacritics: *nǐ*, *hǎo*, *shì*. Applied to Cantonese, it is not inaccurate so much as inapplicable — there is no Pinyin spelling for a syllable ending in -p, and no Pinyin tone mark for Cantonese tone 5.

**Jyutping** is a Cantonese romanisation published by the Linguistic Society of Hong Kong. It writes tone as a digit after the syllable: *nei5*, *hou2*, *hai6*. That makes it typeable on any keyboard and unambiguous to read aloud.

The clearest way to see the gap is a word both languages write identically. 你好 is "hello" in both, with the same two characters:

| | Written | Romanised |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mandarin | 你好 | nǐ hǎo |
| Cantonese | 你好 | nei5 hou2 |

Same page, different language. This is exactly why a Cantonese course has to be built as a Cantonese course rather than adapted from Mandarin material, and it is the design premise behind [Learn Cantonese for Beginners](/apps/learn-cantonese/), which teaches traditional characters and Jyutping together from the first lesson.

One more romanisation note, because it causes real confusion in libraries and catalogues: the Library of Congress ALA-LC tables exist to transliterate Chinese consistently for cataloguing, not to be pronounced from. If you meet a romanised name that matches neither Pinyin nor Jyutping, that is often what you are looking at.

## The same characters, in two different situations

Here the languages genuinely do overlap — and the overlap is more complicated than "they share a script".

### Traditional and simplified are about place, not about language

Character standards follow jurisdictions rather than spoken varieties. Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan use **traditional** characters. Mainland China uses **simplified** characters, and that includes Guangdong province, where Cantonese is widely spoken.

So there is no such thing as "the Cantonese character set". A Cantonese speaker in Hong Kong reads traditional; a Cantonese speaker in Guangzhou reads simplified. The Unicode Standard encodes both, which is why your phone renders either without being told which language it is looking at.

For a learner the decision is practical: pick the set that matches where you intend to use the language. Learn Cantonese for Beginners teaches traditional characters, which matches Hong Kong and Macau usage.

### Written Cantonese is a third thing

Most formal writing in Hong Kong — newspapers, contracts, textbooks, government notices — is **Standard Written Chinese**, whose grammar and vocabulary are closer to Mandarin. A Cantonese speaker reads it aloud using Cantonese pronunciations, which means the sentence on the page is not the sentence they would have said.

**Written Cantonese** is the other register: spoken Cantonese committed to the page, used in messaging, social media, advertising, comics and fiction dialogue. It uses characters that do not appear in Standard Written Chinese at all — 係 (*hai6*), 唔 (*m4*), 佢 (*keoi5*), 嘅 (*ge3*).

A Mandarin reader can usually handle the first register and is stopped by the second. That asymmetry is the single best illustration of how the two languages relate: close in the formal written channel, genuinely separate in the spoken one.

## Everyday words that are simply different words

The character overlap hides this, so it is worth showing directly. These are not different pronunciations of one word. They are different words that happen to be written in the same script.

| English | Mandarin | Cantonese |
| --- | --- | --- |
| to be | 是 (shì) | 係 (hai6) |
| not | 不 (bù) | 唔 (m4) |
| he / she | 他 · 她 (tā) | 佢 (keoi5) |
| to eat | 吃 (chī) | 食 (sik6) |
| to drink | 喝 (hē) | 飲 (jam2) |
| to look at | 看 (kàn) | 睇 (tai2) |

Six rows, and between them they cover a large share of any ordinary sentence. A learner who assumes Mandarin vocabulary transfers will produce Cantonese that is comprehensible in the way that word-for-word translated English is comprehensible — decipherable, and clearly not the language.

Politeness diverges too, in a way English speakers find genuinely useful once it clicks. Mandarin uses 謝謝 (*xièxie*) broadly for "thank you". Cantonese splits the job: **唔該 (*m4 goi1*)** thanks someone for an action or a service and also does duty as "excuse me" and "please", while **多謝 (*do1 ze6*)** thanks someone for a gift or a compliment. Using *do1 ze6* for a refilled teacup is one of the most audible beginner errors there is.

Numbers, at least, behave. 一, 二, 三 are *jat1, ji6, saam1* in Cantonese and *yī, èr, sān* in Mandarin — same characters, same logic, different sounds, and both build larger numbers on the same regular pattern.

## Where each one is spoken

This tends to be the deciding factor, and it is worth stating without hype.

**Mandarin** is the standard spoken variety of the People's Republic of China and of Taiwan, and one of the official languages of Singapore. It is the language of national broadcasting and education across mainland China, and it has by far the larger volume of teaching material, dictionaries and classroom availability outside Asia.

**Cantonese** is the everyday spoken language of Hong Kong and Macau — where the official languages are Chinese and English — and of Guangzhou and much of the surrounding province, along with long-established communities overseas. It carries a century of film, television and popular music that exists in Cantonese and is not the same body of work in Mandarin.

Neither is "more useful" in the abstract. They are useful in different places, for different things.

## The difficulty question, answered carefully

The most defensible public benchmark is the US Foreign Service Institute's training categories, which group the languages FSI teaches by how long its own students typically need to reach an integrated score of 3 on the Interagency Language Roundtable scale. FSI places **both** Cantonese and Mandarin in **Category IV**, its hardest group, at **88 weeks of full-time instruction (2,200 class hours)**, alongside Japanese, Korean and Arabic.

Two things follow. First, the published institutional guidance does not rank one above the other, so anyone telling you Cantonese is "twice as hard" is not working from that source. Second, those figures describe intensive classroom training for diplomats on a schedule FSI puts at roughly 23 hours of class plus 17 hours of self-study a week. They are not a prediction about phone study and should never be read as one.

What FSI is genuinely good for is relative scale. Both languages sit at the demanding end, which is a reason to choose deliberately rather than a reason not to start.

## How to actually choose

Four questions, in the order that matters.

**Where will you use it?** This settles it more often than anything else. Hong Kong, Macau, Guangzhou or a Cantonese-speaking family points one way. Mainland China outside Guangdong, Taiwan or Singapore points the other.

**Whose voice do you want to understand?** If a specific person, a body of film, or a set of songs is the reason you are learning, learn the language they are actually in.

**How much material do you need around you?** Mandarin has more of everything: courses, tutors, dictionaries, graded readers. Cantonese material is thinner but sufficient, and thin is not the same as absent.

**Are you optimising for the least effort?** Then neither is the answer. Both sit in FSI's hardest category, and if breadth of conversation per hour invested is the goal, [Malay](/blog/learn-malay-beginners-guide/) sits two full categories lower and starts in the Latin alphabet.

The one strategy that reliably fails is starting both at once. The character sets overlap enough to feel efficient and the sound systems conflict enough to guarantee interference — you end up with a Cantonese accent full of Mandarin finals, or Mandarin tones bent toward Cantonese shapes. Finish a foundation in one before you open the other.

## If you have already learned one

The transfer is real and lopsided.

**Reading transfers well.** If you read traditional characters for Mandarin, a large part of Hong Kong signage, menus and formal writing is immediately available to you. You will still be stopped by written Cantonese, which is worth treating as a small separate vocabulary project rather than as a mystery.

**Speaking transfers badly.** You are learning a new tone system, new syllable endings, and a new romanisation. The most common failure among Mandarin speakers moving to Cantonese is assuming the tones will settle by themselves. They do not, because the habit already in place actively competes.

**Grammar mostly transfers, with edits.** Word order is broadly similar, classifiers exist in both, neither conjugates verbs for tense. But the specific grammar words differ, aspect marking differs, and Cantonese carries a large inventory of sentence-final particles that Mandarin does not match. Learn Cantonese for Beginners teaches that layer explicitly — word order, pronouns, classifiers, possession, negation, aspect markers, questions, comparisons, adjective placement and sentence-final particles — rather than assuming a Mandarin-shaped starting point.

## The short version

They share a script and a family. They do not share sounds, romanisation, everyday grammar words, or the ability to be understood by each other's speakers. Choose on geography and on whose voice you want to follow, pick one romanisation and one character set, and give it a year before you consider the second.
