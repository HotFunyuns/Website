To a beginner, a candlestick chart can look like a barcode. Each candle is actually a compact summary of one period's trading, and once you can read one, you can read them all. StockCharts' ChartSchool, a reference on charting, traces the method to Japanese rice traders and explains each part of the candle; this guide follows its definitions.

## The four prices in every candle

ChartSchool explains that a candlestick chart needs four values for each period: **open, high, low and close**.

- **The body** (or real body) is the thick part, spanning the open and the close.
- **The shadows** (also called wicks or tails) are the thin lines above and below. The top of the upper shadow marks the period's high; the bottom of the lower shadow marks the low.

A candle's period can be anything: a minute, an hour, a day, a week. A daily chart has one candle per trading day.

## Up candles and down candles

| Candle | Close vs open | Usual colour | How it is drawn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Up | Close above open | Hollow (white) | Body bottom = open, body top = close |
| Down | Close below open | Filled (black) | Body top = open, body bottom = close |

Some apps use green and red instead of hollow and filled, but the logic does not change. ChartSchool describes hollow candles, where the close is above the open, as indicating buying pressure, and filled candles as indicating selling pressure.

## Reading the shape

ChartSchool's guidelines for interpreting a single candle:

- **Long bodies** show strong buying or selling pressure: the price moved a long way from open to close.
- **Short bodies** show little movement, often consolidation.
- **Long shadows** show that the price travelled well beyond where it opened and closed during the period. A long upper shadow means it traded much higher before falling back; a long lower shadow means it traded much lower before recovering.
- **No shadows** (a Marubozu) means the period opened at one extreme and closed at the other.

**Example.** A daily candle with open $50, high $54, low $49.50 and close $50.20 has a small body near the bottom of a long upper shadow. Buyers pushed the price $4 above the open, but by the close it had given almost all of that back.

## A few named patterns

Chart readers give names to recurring shapes. A few that ChartSchool describes:

| Pattern | Shape | Usual reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Doji | Open and close (nearly) equal; tiny body | Indecision: neither side moved the price by the close |
| Hammer | Small body near the top, long lower shadow, after a decline | Sellers pushed prices down, buyers pushed them back |
| Shooting star | Small body near the bottom, long upper shadow, after a rise | Buyers pushed prices up, sellers pushed them back |
| Engulfing | A body that completely covers the previous candle's body | A shift in which side controlled the period |

Notice the "after a decline" and "after a rise" in the table. ChartSchool stresses repeatedly that a candle's meaning depends on its position within the broader picture: a long white candle after a long decline means something different from one after a long advance.

## What candlesticks cannot do

A candlestick is a record. It tells you exactly what happened in a period and nothing certain about what will happen next. Even ChartSchool says a doji on its own is not enough to mark a reversal and that hammers require further confirmation. The listing for our own simulator, [Learn to Trade Stocks Beginner](/apps/learn-to-trade-stocks/), says it directly: technical indicators summarise price or volume information but cannot confirm a future outcome or remove uncertainty. Chart analysis is one approach among several; [technical versus fundamental analysis](/blog/technical-vs-fundamental-analysis/) compares it with the study of a company's business.

## Practising chart reading safely

Three habits make chart practice more useful:

1. **Describe before you interpret.** Say what the candle shows (open, close, range) before deciding what it means.
2. **Look at context:** the trend before it, [trading volume](/blog/trading-volume-explained/) on the day, and nearby price levels; see [support and resistance](/blog/support-and-resistance-explained/).
3. **Record and check.** Write down what you expect and see how often you are right.
