An undefeated fighter has no losses on their professional record. A draw or a no contest does not break that zero, so "unbeaten" is a looser claim than "perfect". Among careers that are finished, Rocky Marciano, Joe Calzaghe, Laila Ali and Andre Ward retired from boxing without a defeat, and Khabib Nurmagomedov did the same in mixed martial arts. The list stays short for a reason that is mostly arithmetic: a career is a long chain of fights, and one bad night is enough to end the run.

Everything below concerns careers that are over. An active fighter's record is a running total that changes every few months, so those are left out on purpose.

## What breaks a zero, and what does not

Records are written as wins, losses and draws, with no contests usually shown separately — [how boxing records are written](/blog/how-boxing-records-are-written/) covers the notation itself. For the narrower question of whether a fighter is still unbeaten, results fall into four groups.

| Result | What it does to an unbeaten record |
| --- | --- |
| Win, by any method | Extends it |
| Draw (unanimous, majority, split or technical) | Keeps the zero, but the record stops being perfect |
| No contest, or a "no decision" | Not a result for either fighter, so the zero survives |
| Loss, by any method, disqualification included | Ends it |

Two rule details explain why the third row exists, and why a record is less fixed than it looks.

**Early accidental fouls usually produce no result at all.** In boxing, Nevada's regulations declare a bout a no decision when an accidental foul stops it during the first three rounds of a fight scheduled for six or fewer, or the first four rounds of a longer one, so under that rule a clash of heads in round two cannot cost anyone their zero. The current Unified Rules of Mixed Martial Arts — version 8.2026, published by the Association of Boxing Commissions and Combative Sports — define a no contest as an early stoppage for accidental injury before enough of the bout has been completed to go to the scorecards, although their foul procedure also lists disqualification as a possible outcome of such a stoppage.

**A result can change after the event.** Nevada's athletic commission will revisit a decision for collusion, for an error in compiling the judges' scorecards, or where the referee misapplied the rules. It can also turn a win or a draw into a no contest when the winner is found to have committed an anti-doping violation, and it notes the change on that fighter's record. In principle, that second power can hand a beaten opponent their zero back.

## Five careers that ended unbeaten

| Fighter | Sport | What the cited source records | Career ended |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rocky Marciano | Boxing | 49 fights without a defeat, 43 won by knockout | 1956 |
| Joe Calzaghe | Boxing | Undefeated in 46 bouts, 32 won by knockout | February 2009 |
| Laila Ali | Boxing | Undefeated in 24 bouts, boxing from 1999 to 2007 | 2007 |
| Andre Ward | Boxing | An unbeaten record of 32 victories | September 2017 |
| Khabib Nurmagomedov | MMA | 29-0-0 (W-L-D), status listed as retired | Last fought October 24, 2020 |

The sources are ESPN's SportsCentury profile (Marciano), Encyclopaedia Britannica (Calzaghe and Ali), Sky Sports' report of Ward's retirement, and Nurmagomedov's athlete page on UFC.com. Each record is given in its source's own terms, which is why the third column does not use one uniform notation. It is a set of examples, not a complete list.

One thing all five have in common is easy to miss: each run ended with a decision to stop rather than a defeat. When Ward announced his retirement, Sky Sports quoted him saying his body "can no longer put up with the rigors of the sport". Choosing when to leave is part of how a zero survives — and a fighter who announces a comeback reopens the record, which is why no such career appears in the table.

## The arithmetic behind a long zero

Suppose a fighter wins any single bout with the same probability, and that every bout is independent of the others. Neither is true of a real career — opponents differ, and somebody chooses them — but the simplification shows the shape of the problem.

| Chance of winning each bout | Still unbeaten after 10 | After 25 | After 50 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 90% | about 35% | about 7% | about 0.5% |
| 95% | about 60% | about 28% | about 8% |
| 98% | about 82% | about 60% | about 36% |

Turn the question around and it gets starker. Even odds of reaching 25-0 need a win chance of about 97.3% in every bout; even odds of 50-0 need about 98.6%, which is 0.5 raised to the power of 1/50. Being the better fighter nine nights in ten is nowhere near enough.

That is the strongest argument for reading a long unbeaten record partly as a matchmaking achievement. The most dependable way to hold a per-fight probability that high for that long is to avoid fights you might lose, and while a zero is commercially valuable, the [economics of a fight card](/blog/how-fight-cards-are-built/) reward exactly that caution. The same relationship between long runs and small edges is worked through for whole seasons in [understanding sports simulation probability](/blog/understanding-sports-sim-probability/).

## What zero losses shows, and what it does not

**It shows the fighter never lost under the rules that applied.** Across dozens of fights that is a real achievement, and nothing below takes it away.

**It does not show the fights were comfortable.** Marciano won the heavyweight title from Jersey Joe Walcott in 1952 while trailing: ESPN's account has Walcott ahead on all three officials' cards after 12 rounds, before Marciano knocked him out early in the 13th. An unbeaten record keeps the result and discards the margin.

**It does not show who the opposition was.** Two identical zeros can sit on very different careers, one built against contenders and one against opponents chosen to lose. The record has no column for that.

**It does not travel between sports.** A mixed martial arts bout can be lost to strikes, to a submission or on the cards, and Nevada's regulations call for MMA gloves of 4 to 8 ounces, against 8- or 10-ounce gloves in boxing depending on the boxer's weight. More routes to a sudden finish arguably make each MMA bout a riskier link in the chain, though no record table can measure that directly.

**It does not mean the fighter could not have lost.** It means they did not. A draw on the cards is the result that sits closest to the line, and [how boxing scoring works](/blog/boxing-scoring-explained/) shows how three judges' totals can produce one.

## Five questions to ask of any unbeaten record

1. **Who were the wins against, and when?** Opponents at the very start or the very end of their own careers flatter a record.
2. **How many fights were close?** Split and majority decisions say more about risk taken than a run of early stoppages against overmatched opponents.
3. **Is there a draw or a no contest in it?** Both leave the zero intact, so the headline can hide them.
4. **Has any result been changed since the event?** Commissions have the power to revisit results, and a changed result moves two records at once.
5. **Is the career finished?** A running zero is a provisional figure, not a final one.
