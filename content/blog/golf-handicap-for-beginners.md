The most common misunderstanding about a golf handicap is that it describes your average round. It does not. A handicap is a numerical measure of a golfer's **demonstrated ability, or potential ability** — closer to what you are capable of when things go reasonably well than to what you typically shoot. Lower is better, and it exists so that players of different standards can compete against one another meaningfully.

That distinction explains almost every confusing thing about the number, including why it moves so reluctantly upward.

## The calculation, in plain terms

The World Handicap System was devised by the sport's governing bodies working with existing handicapping authorities, and began rolling out globally in 2020, replacing a patchwork of incompatible national systems.

It works in three steps.

**1. Each round becomes a score differential.** The differential adjusts your score for the difficulty of the course you played it on, using the formula 113 divided by the slope rating, multiplied by your adjusted gross score minus the course rating (with a playing-conditions adjustment where one applies).

**2. Only your best rounds count.** An ongoing handicap is based on the average of the **best 8 score differentials from your most recent 20 rounds**.

**3. Adjustments protect the number from distortion.** There is a per-hole cap of net double bogey for handicapping purposes, so one or two disastrous holes cannot have a disproportionate effect. And there is an "anchor" that prevents rapid increases which would not reflect a player's true potential.

Getting started requires a sample. The recommendation is a minimum of 54 holes, in any combination of 9 and 18-hole rounds, though handicaps may be issued from fewer. Both competitive and recreational rounds may be submitted, with updates published daily.

## Why course rating and slope rating exist

These two numbers are what make a handicap portable, and they solve different problems.

**Course rating** describes how difficult a course is for a scratch golfer — effectively, what a very good player would be expected to shoot.

**Slope rating** describes the course's relative difficulty for a bogey golfer compared with a scratch golfer. The term was invented by the United States Golf Association to address a specific observation: on harder courses, higher-handicap players' scores rise more quickly than their handicaps alone would predict. Without slope, a handicap earned on a gentle course would be worth far too much on a severe one.

Together they mean your handicap travels. A 14 at your home course is a 14 anywhere, and the conversion into strokes for a particular set of tees happens at the course rather than being baked into the index.

## Why it goes down quickly and up slowly

This is the behaviour that irritates new golfers most, and it is entirely deliberate.

Because only the best 8 of your last 20 differentials count, a bad round is usually outside the counting set. It has no effect at all unless your recent form has been poor enough that a bad round is among your better ones — which is precisely the situation in which the handicap *should* move.

A good round, by contrast, enters the counting set immediately and displaces something worse.

The asymmetry follows directly from what the handicap is for. If the number described your average, it would be an accurate description of a golfer and a poor instrument for competition, because every player would win roughly as often by having one great day. By describing potential instead, it makes a great day the thing you have to produce to win, rather than the thing the system has already assumed.

## What it does and does not tell you

**It is a competition instrument.** Its job is to adjust results so that a 4 and a 22 can play a meaningful match. It does that by producing an allowance which is subtracted from a gross score to give a net score, as [golf scoring](/blog/golf-scoring-explained/) sets out.

**It is not a skill ranking with meaning outside competition.** A number derived from your best 8 of 20 is not a prediction of Saturday.

**It is not used in professional stroke play.** Tour events are played off scratch, which is why professional scores are quoted gross and why the cut in [how a golf cut works](/blog/how-a-golf-cut-works/) is an unadjusted line.

**It is not a substitute for knowing where your score goes.** For that you want the category thinking in [strokes gained](/blog/strokes-gained-for-club-golfers/), which answers a different question entirely.

## A career sim with no handicap in it

[Golf Career Simulator Pro Tour](/apps/golf-career-simulator/) has no handicap system, and the omission was a deliberate decision rather than a gap we never got to.

The reasoning is straightforward once stated. The game is a professional career: multi-round events with cuts, leaderboards, ties and playoffs, where finishes decide rankings, earnings and which events you can enter next season. Professional stroke play is played off scratch. Adding a handicap would have meant adding a mechanic the sport the game depicts does not use.

What replaces it is a **skill profile**: driving, accuracy, irons, short game, putting, composure and consistency, developed through training. Those seven numbers do the job a handicap does in a club context — they describe what your golfer is capable of — but they do it as components rather than as a single summary.

**The trade-off is real and it cuts both ways.**

What we gained: a developing player is legible. You can see that your putting has improved and your composure has not, and you can train accordingly. A handicap would have compressed all seven into one number that goes down, which is less informative and much less interesting to act on.

What we lost: there is no single portable figure that says how good your golfer currently is. Players ask for one regularly, usually so they can compare careers. We have resisted adding a composite rating because averaging the seven skills would produce a number that hides the thing that actually decides tournaments — that a golfer with excellent putting and poor composure is a different proposition from the reverse, even when the average matches.

Consistency, incidentally, is the skill that behaves most like the spirit of a handicap. It governs the gap between your good rounds and your bad ones, which is exactly the gap the best-8-of-20 rule is designed to look past.
