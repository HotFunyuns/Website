Nothing written by Cleopatra survives, and nothing written by anyone on her side survives either. Every narrative account of her life was produced by Romans, most of them well after her death and all of them after her defeat. That single fact should govern how you read anything about her — including this article, which can only tell you what the surviving evidence supports and where it runs out.

## Who is actually telling you this

The fullest ancient account is Plutarch's *Life of Antony*, written around 130 years after Cleopatra died. Plutarch is not a careless writer and he does not pretend to eyewitness authority; at one point he traces a story back through a named physician, Philotas of Amphissa, who told it to Plutarch's own grandfather. That kind of disclosure is exactly what you want from a source, and it also tells you how long the chain is.

Cassius Dio wrote his account of the same events in the third century CE, further away still, inside a Roman imperial historiography in which Octavian's victory was the founding act of the existing order.

The closest thing to contemporary comment is poetry. Horace's ode on Actium — *Nunc est bibendum*, "now is the time to drink" — was written within a few years of the event. It is worth looking at closely, because it is a masterclass in how a defeated enemy gets described. Cleopatra is never named in it. She is *regina*, the queen, and *fatale monstrum*, a fatal or doom-bringing monster, plotting ruin for the Capitol. And then, in the last stanza, the poem turns: she sought a nobler death, did not flinch like a woman, handled the serpents unafraid, and refused to be led in triumph — *non humilis mulier*, no humble woman.

That turn is the most useful thing in the poem. Even the propaganda could not sustain contempt to the end. When a hostile source concedes something against its own interest, the concession is worth more than the abuse.

## What the hostile tradition was for

Octavian's problem after Actium was that he had won a civil war against another Roman, and Romans had had enough of civil wars. Recasting the conflict as a foreign war against an Eastern queen who had bewitched a Roman general solved a political problem. It made the victory patriotic rather than factional.

Everything in the surviving literary tradition was written after that framing had won. Read this way, many of the familiar elements — the luxury, the seduction, the unmanning of Antony, the barge, the banquets — look less like reporting and more like the standard Roman vocabulary for what happens when a Roman goes East.

None of which proves the individual anecdotes false. It means each one needs to be weighed on its own, which is exactly the posture set out in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/), applied to a named person in [how we know what historical figures actually did](/blog/how-we-know-what-historical-figures-did/), and the same discipline that makes accounts of Roman decline so hard to adjudicate in [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/).

## The death, and an ancient writer's honesty

Everyone knows how Cleopatra died. Plutarch did not.

He records several versions: that the asp was brought in among figs; that a snake was kept in a water jar and provoked with a golden distaff until it struck her arm; that she carried poison in a hollow comb hidden in her hair. Then he writes that the truth of the matter no one knows. He adds three further complications: no spot or other sign of poison appeared on her body; the reptile was never seen inside the chamber; and some said two faint punctures were visible on her arm, which Octavian appears to have believed, since an image of Cleopatra with the asp was carried in his triumph.

Read that carefully and you find an ancient biographer doing something modern popular history rarely does: laying out incompatible accounts, refusing to choose, and noting that the winner had a reason to prefer one of them. The asp is in the tradition partly because it appeared in a Roman victory parade.

Plutarch is precise where he can be. He gives her age at death as forty years save one, and says she had shared power with Antony for more than fourteen years. Those are the kinds of details a source transmits without motive.

## What documents show that literature does not

The literary tradition is the loudest evidence, not the only evidence.

Coins struck in her reign carry portraits, and they are strikingly unlike the Roman image: a prominent, aquiline nose, a firm chin, a face presented for authority rather than charm. Whether they are a likeness is a separate question from what they were for, and what they were for was a claim to legitimate rule in a Hellenistic idiom.

Administrative material survives too. Ptolemaic Egypt generated an enormous volume of documents on papyrus and potsherd, and Greek papyri from Egypt are a major body of evidence for the period. One document in particular has drawn attention: a royal ordinance granting tax privileges, at the foot of which a single Greek word authorises the grant. Peter van Minnen argued in *Ancient Society* that this word may have been written by Cleopatra herself. The identification is debated. Its interest is not celebrity autograph-hunting; it is that an administrative grant to a Roman associate is a glimpse of what a Ptolemaic ruler actually spent the day doing.

## The state she was running

A biography that treats Cleopatra as a personality misses the structure she was operating inside, and the structure explains most of her choices.

The Ptolemies were a Macedonian Greek dynasty governing Egypt through a Greek-speaking administrative class, with an Egyptian population underneath it and a temple establishment that had to be kept aligned. Scholarship on Ptolemaic governance has examined how far that arrangement should be described in terms of class and colonial rule rather than simply as a monarchy. By the first century BCE the kingdom's independence was already contingent on Roman politics: Ptolemaic rulers borrowed from Roman financiers, depended on Roman military backing, and could be installed or removed by decisions taken in Rome.

Seen that way, her relationships with Julius Caesar and then with Mark Antony are not romantic episodes with political side-effects. They are the available strategy for a ruler whose kingdom's survival depended on which Roman faction won — a strategy that worked twice and failed the third time.

## Ancestry, and what the sources will not tell you

Her father was Ptolemy XII. Her mother is not securely identified in any surviving source; nor is her paternal grandmother. This matters because modern arguments about her ancestry are often conducted with a confidence the evidence does not license, in both directions. The dynasty's origin was Macedonian Greek; who else entered the family line in its last generations is simply unknown.

The one relevant thing Plutarch does record is that she learned the Egyptian language, which earlier rulers of her line had not troubled to do, and that she could converse in many languages without an interpreter. That is a statement about political practice, and a rather revealing one about her predecessors.

## Placing her in the century she lived in

Cleopatra is usually met in isolation, as a character. Set her in sequence and the shape of her reign changes: she is the last ruler of a Hellenistic kingdom that had been running for nearly three centuries, in a decade when every independent Mediterranean state was being absorbed by one expanding republic turning into an empire.

## The honest summary

We know roughly when she ruled, what she ruled, who she dealt with, how the war ended, and what she put on her coins. We know how Romans wanted her remembered, and we can see the seams in that account because their own best writer left them visible.

We do not know how she died, what she looked like, who her mother was, or what she thought she was doing. A confident answer to any of those is someone filling a gap. The gaps are part of the record, and saying so is not a failure of the article — it is the article.
