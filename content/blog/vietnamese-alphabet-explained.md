**Vietnamese is written in a Latin-based alphabet called chữ Quốc ngữ.** That fact makes the language look far more approachable than Thai or Khmer, and it is genuinely an advantage — but the letters do not mean what an English reader assumes, and the marks are not optional.

## What is and is not in the alphabet

Two surprises for an English speaker.

**Some familiar letters are absent.** F, J, W and Z are not part of the Vietnamese alphabet. They turn up in loanwords, brand names and abbreviations, but not in native words.

**Some unfamiliar letters are present, as letters.** These are not decorated versions of other letters:

| Letter | Relationship to the base letter |
| --- | --- |
| ă | Separate vowel, not a variant of a |
| â | Separate vowel, not a variant of a |
| ê | Separate vowel, not a variant of e |
| ô | Separate vowel, not a variant of o |
| ơ | Separate vowel, not a variant of o |
| ư | Separate vowel, not a variant of u |
| đ | Separate consonant, not a variant of d |

The **đ** is the one that catches people out most often. It is its own letter with its own sound, and writing d instead is a spelling error, not a typographical shortcut.

Standard listings give 29 letters, though counts vary with how the counter treats digraphs. A number quoted without its convention is not very informative.

## The two jobs diacritics do

This is the single most useful structural fact about written Vietnamese, and it clears up most of the confusion:

**Job one — vowel quality.** The circumflex (â, ê, ô), the breve (ă) and the horn (ơ, ư) mark *which vowel* is being written. These are part of the letter.

**Job two — tone.** The grave, hook above, tilde, acute and dot below mark *which tone* the syllable carries. These are applied on top.

A single vowel can carry both at once — a horn and an acute, for instance — which is why Vietnamese needs so many distinct precomposed characters. The [Latin Extended Additional Unicode block](https://www.unicode.org/charts/PDF/U1E00.pdf) exists largely to hold them.

**Practical rule:** if you remove a mark, ask which job it was doing. Removing a vowel mark changes the letter. Removing a tone mark changes the tone. Both change the word. [Vietnamese tones explained](/blog/vietnamese-tones-explained/) covers the second set in detail.

## Sorting and dictionaries

Because the marked vowels are separate letters, Vietnamese dictionary order is not English alphabetical order with the marks ignored. Words beginning with ă are grouped separately from words beginning with a.

This matters practically the first time you try to look something up and cannot find a word you are certain exists.

## Typing it

A standard keyboard has no keys for these characters, so Vietnamese input uses a conversion method. The two common ones:

- **Telex** — type a marker letter after the vowel (for example, typing a particular letter twice or following it with a designated key produces the marked form).
- **VNI** — type a number after the vowel to add the mark.

Phone keyboards handle this transparently once a Vietnamese layout is enabled. On a desktop it usually requires installing an input method.

The relevant point for a learner: **do not omit marks because they are inconvenient to type.** A note written without diacritics is a note you cannot read back reliably, and it trains the wrong habit.

## Why this system exists

Vietnamese was historically written with Chinese-derived characters. Chữ Quốc ngữ was developed from Latin script by European missionaries in the seventeenth century and became the standard system over the following centuries.

The design goal was phonemic representation — one sound, one spelling — which is why Vietnamese spelling is far more regular than English. Once you know the rules, you can pronounce an unfamiliar written word, which is not true of English at all.

## How this compares to the alternatives

If you have looked at other Southeast Asian languages, the contrast is stark:

- **[Lao](/blog/lao-alphabet-explained/)** uses its own abugida script with no spaces between words.
- **[Khmer](/blog/khmer-script-explained/)** uses a script where consonants carry an inherent series that determines vowel pronunciation.
- **Vietnamese** gives you letters you already recognise and asks you to learn what they mean.

Which is easier depends on the learner. What is certain is that Vietnamese moves the difficulty from decoding to precision.
