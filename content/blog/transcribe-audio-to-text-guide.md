A recording captures everything, but finding one detail in it means listening through again. A transcript lets you read, search, check a point and move on. That is the reasoning behind [Transcribe Audio to Text PRO](/apps/transcribe-audio-to-text/), which converts recorded speech into written text. This guide covers what it is for, how to get a transcript you can actually use, and what to check before you rely on it.

## Four everyday uses

The listing frames the app around four kinds of recording:

| Recording | What the transcript becomes |
| --- | --- |
| A lecture | Material to review alongside your notes, with unfamiliar terms to look up |
| A voice note | A written starting point for a journal entry, a plan or a first draft |
| A meeting | A reference while you write up notes and follow-ups |
| An interview | Text to work through before drafting an article or research |

Each has its own guide: [transcribing lectures](/blog/how-to-transcribe-lectures/), [transcribing a voice memo](/blog/how-to-transcribe-a-voice-memo/) and [transcribing an interview](/blog/how-to-transcribe-an-interview/).

## From recording to usable text in four steps

The app's own steps are simple:

1. **Start with recorded speech**: a lecture, meeting, interview or voice note.
2. **Transcribe it** into written text.
3. **Check the details**: compare names, numbers and quotations with the original audio.
4. **Use the text** for study notes, follow-ups, first drafts or research.

Step three is the one that catches the errors, so do not treat it as optional.

## How good is automatic transcription?

Automatic speech recognition is not perfect. Wikipedia notes that accuracy is usually measured by **word error rate** (WER), and that recognition is complicated by accent, pronunciation, volume and speed, by background noise and echoes, and by recording characteristics. It records that in 2017, Microsoft researchers reported reaching parity with professional human transcribers on the widely used Switchboard conversational speech benchmark. That is a benchmark result, not a promise about your own recordings.

The listing is candid in the same way: accuracy depends on **recording quality, background noise and how clearly people speak**.

## A worked example: what a low error rate means

Wikipedia explains that WER counts the substitutions, deletions and insertions needed to turn the transcript into the correct text, divided by the number of words actually spoken. Take a 30-minute lecture. At a comfortable speaking pace of about 150 words a minute, the pace Wikipedia notes audiobooks aim for, that is about **4,500 words**. A WER of 5% would still mean around **225 word errors**. Some may be harmless, a "the" for an "a", but any of them could be a name, a date or a number. That is why checking the details is part of the workflow, not an optional extra.

## Getting a better transcript

Several of the conditions Wikipedia lists can be improved before you transcribe:

- **Record close to the speaker**, so the voice stands out from room noise and echoes, two of the adverse conditions on Wikipedia's list.
- **Reduce background noise**: environmental noise is another.
- **One voice at a time**: people talking over each other are also harder to untangle when you check the transcript against the audio.
- **Note difficult names and terms** while recording, so you can correct them afterwards.

[Speech-to-text accuracy tips](/blog/speech-to-text-accuracy-tips/) goes into each of these.

## Reading and editing the transcript

Read the transcript with the audio to hand. Correct names and numbers first, then anything that changes meaning. Decide how polished it needs to be: a study transcript can keep its ums and false starts, while a quote for publication must be exactly right. [Verbatim versus clean verbatim transcription](/blog/verbatim-vs-clean-transcription/) explains the choice, and [adding timestamps](/blog/how-to-add-timestamps-to-a-transcript/) explains how to make long transcripts easy to navigate.

## Meetings: from transcript to follow-ups

Meetings are the one use without its own guide here, so a quick method. The listing describes using a meeting transcript to check what was said about a task, a proposal or a decision while you prepare notes and follow-ups. Search the transcript for words such as "agree", "decide", "by Friday" or people's names; copy each decision and action into a short list with an owner and a date; then confirm the list with the attendees rather than circulating the raw transcript.

## Keep the recording

The listing recommends keeping the original recording for **tone and context**. A transcript records words, not how they were said; sarcasm, hesitation and emphasis live in the audio. Keep both, and treat the recording as the source.

## What the app does not promise

Accuracy depends on recording quality, background noise and speech clarity, so check names and numbers against the audio, especially before quoting anyone.
