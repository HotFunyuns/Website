The method for analogy questions fits in one sentence: say the relationship between the first pair out loud as a complete sentence, then test each option by substituting it into that same sentence. Everything else in this article is elaboration on that, because the failures almost all come from skipping it — reading the options first, feeling a pull towards one, and reverse-engineering a relationship that justifies the pull.

An analogy in the classic form is written **A : B :: C : D**, read as "A is to B as C is to D". You are given three of the four terms and choose the fourth. What is being tested is whether you can identify a relation and carry it across to a new pair.

## Relations, not resemblances

The single most useful idea in this subject is that analogies run on shared **relations** rather than shared **attributes**. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on analogical reasoning sets this out carefully: an analogical argument maps from a source domain to a target domain, and what gets mapped is a system of relations, not a list of properties. Two domains are similar when they share many attributes; they are analogous when they share few attributes but many relations.

The review of relational reasoning by Lindsey Richland and Hongyang Zhao in *Behavioral Sciences* makes the same point from the education side: relational alignment, not surface similarity, is what an analogy trades on. Items that look identical can be structurally unrelated, and items that look nothing alike can share a deep relational structure.

For a solver, this converts directly into a rule. When you write your sentence, write it about a relation — "a thermometer is the instrument that measures temperature" — not about a resemblance. "A thermometer and temperature go together" is not a sentence you can test anything against.

## A taxonomy of relationship types

Most items draw on a short list. Recognising which family you are in narrows the search immediately.

| Relationship | Example | The sentence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Instrument and quantity | thermometer : temperature | X is the instrument that measures Y |
| Part and whole | petal : flower | X is a part of Y |
| Member and category | sparrow : bird | X is a kind of Y |
| Degree | warm : hot | X is a milder form of Y |
| Opposition | ascend : descend | X is the opposite of Y |
| Worker and tool | chef : knife | X characteristically works with Y |
| Creator and work | composer : symphony | X produces Y |
| Cause and effect | drought : famine | X can bring about Y |

The table is not exhaustive and is not meant to be memorised as a list. Its use is diagnostic: run down it, find the row whose sentence fits the first pair, and you have your test.

Worked through: **thermometer : temperature :: odometer : ?** The sentence is "X is the instrument that measures Y". An odometer measures distance travelled, so **distance** completes it. Compare **sparrow : bird :: trout : ?** — "X is a kind of Y" gives **fish**.

## Direction, and the distractor built to exploit it

Relations have a direction, and an analogy must preserve it. "Composer is to symphony" runs creator to work. If the answer options include a pair that runs work to creator, it is wrong no matter how natural the pairing feels. Making the sentence explicit protects against this automatically, because a reversed pair produces a false sentence.

The other standard distractor is the surface match. Take **hot : cold :: tall : ?** The relation is opposition, so the answer is **short**. An option like *height* is strongly associated with *tall* and will exert a pull, but it does not survive the sentence: "tall is the opposite of height" is not true.

This is not accidental. Figural analogies are generated by software in precisely this way. The IMak package, described by Diego Blum and Heinz Holling in *Frontiers in Psychology*, builds items in the A : B :: C : ? form by applying rules such as rotation, reflection, segment removal and dot movement, and constructs the options by combining correct and incorrect outcomes for each rule. The correct option gets every rule right; each distractor gets at least one wrong while getting the rest right. That design is why a wrong option can feel three-quarters correct — it is.

## Number analogies are underdetermined by one pair

Numerical analogies deserve separate treatment because they carry a problem that word analogies mostly avoid.

**3 : 12 :: 5 : ?**

Multiply by four gives 20. Add nine gives 14. Both rules fit the first pair exactly, and they disagree from the second pair onwards. Nothing in the item settles it, so any single answer is a guess dressed as reasoning.

Add a pair and the ambiguity evaporates. **3 : 12 :: 7 : 28 :: 5 : ?** — adding nine would give 16 for the second pair, not 28, so the rule is multiplication by four and the answer is **20**. This is the same underdetermination that afflicts short number sequences, treated at length in our guide to [number sequence puzzles](/blog/number-sequence-puzzles-explained/).

The other common numerical form is a straight ratio: **2 : 3 :: 8 : ?** Here the relation is proportional rather than a rule applied to one number, and the answer is **12**, because 8 is four times 2 and 12 is four times 3. Scaling both terms by the same factor is exactly the move that makes recipe adjustments and map scales tractable, and the arithmetic behind doing it quickly is covered in our [mental math training guide](/blog/mental-math-training-guide/).

## Building the habit

The sentence-first method is a habit rather than a piece of knowledge, and habits install through repetition with feedback. Two things help. First, write the sentence down at the start; the act of committing to specific wording is what prevents the vague "they go together" formulation. Second, treat naming the relation as an encoding task in its own right — putting a relationship into your own words is a form of elaboration, which our guide to [memory techniques that work](/blog/memory-techniques-that-work/) covers as a general principle.
