**Total sugars counts every sugar in a food. Added sugars counts only the ones put there.** The Nutrition Facts label separates them, and the reason is a judgement about diet rather than a fact about chemistry.

## What each line means

**Total Sugars** includes all sugars present, whatever their origin — the fructose in fruit, the lactose in milk, and any sugar added during manufacture.

**Includes X g Added Sugars** is a subset. The [FDA's page on added sugars](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/added-sugars-nutrition-facts-label) defines it as sugars added during processing or packaging, plus sugars from syrups and honey and from concentrated fruit or vegetable juices beyond what would be expected from the same amount of the whole food.

Some worked cases:

| Food | Total sugars | Added sugars |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A plain apple | Yes — the fruit's own | None |
| Plain milk | Yes — lactose | None |
| Fruit yoghurt with sugar added | Yes | Yes — the sugar added |
| A food sweetened with honey | Yes | Yes — the honey |
| Juice concentrate used as a sweetener | Yes | Yes |

The honey line is the one that surprises people. **The definition is about the role the sugar plays, not about whether it is refined.** Honey added to a product is a sweetener, so it is counted.

## Why the distinction exists

It comes from dietary guidance rather than from chemistry. The molecules are largely the same; what differs is what they arrive with.

Sugars naturally present in whole foods come packaged with fibre, water, vitamins and minerals. Added sugars arrive with, essentially, energy. The [Dietary Guidelines for Americans](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/) address limiting added sugars specifically, and the label was designed to make that possible to act on.

That is also why the added-sugars line carries a **Daily Value** and the total-sugars line does not. A Daily Value is a policy judgement about how much of something a diet should contain, and one exists for added sugars because guidance addresses them.

## Where the distinction stops being useful

Two situations where it does not tell you much.

**Low-carb tracking.** If you are counting carbohydrate, the relevant number is total carbohydrate, and all sugars — added or not — are already inside it. Whether a gram of sugar was added or naturally present does not change the carbohydrate arithmetic. [Net carbs explained](/blog/net-carbs-explained-keto-tracking/) covers what does matter there.

**Comparing whole foods.** Two fruits differ in total sugars and both have zero added sugars, so the added line does not distinguish them.

## Where it is genuinely useful

**Spotting sweetened versions of ordinary foods.** Plain yoghurt and fruit yoghurt, plain oats and flavoured oats, tomato sauces at different price points — the added-sugars line separates them immediately, and the ingredient list confirms it.

**Comparing products in the same category.** Two granolas or two cereals can differ substantially in added sugars while looking similar elsewhere.

**Following guidance that references added sugars.** If your target is expressed in terms of added sugars, this is the line that measures it.

## The relationship to other label lines

Sugars sit inside **Total Carbohydrate**, alongside dietary fibre and — where present — sugar alcohols. Those relationships matter more than the sugars line for anyone tracking carbohydrate:

- **Dietary fibre** is carbohydrate that is largely not digested in the small intestine.
- **Sugar alcohols** are carbohydrates used as sweeteners and appear inside total carbohydrate, sometimes with their own line. [Sugar alcohols explained](/blog/sugar-alcohols-explained/) covers them, including why "sugar free" does not mean carbohydrate free.
- **Total sugars** and **added sugars** are the two lines this article is about.

[How to read nutrition labels](/blog/reading-nutrition-labels/) covers the whole panel and how the lines relate.

## What the label does not tell you

It does not tell you how a food will affect your blood glucose. That is a separate question, and the usual proxies for it have their own substantial limitations — [glycemic index and glycemic load explained](/blog/glycemic-index-and-load/) covers why.

It also does not tell you which specific sugars are present. That is in the ingredient list.
