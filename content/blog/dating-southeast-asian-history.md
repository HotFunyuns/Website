Southeast Asian history is dated mainly from stone: Sanskrit and Old Khmer foundation inscriptions, Old Javanese and Old Burmese texts cut into temple walls, and the dated donation records that accompany them. Those inscriptions count years in the Saka era rather than the Common Era, so almost every date you read in English has already been converted. Around that epigraphic spine sit three other evidence types — court chronicles compiled centuries later, reports written by foreign visitors, and physical dating from excavation — and the disagreements between them explain most of the cases where two reliable books give two different years.

## The four kinds of evidence, and what each one can carry

Inscriptions are the strongest single source because they are contemporary with the event they record and usually carry a date on the face of the stone. The Corpus des inscriptions khmères, run by the École française d'Extrême-Orient with the École pratique des hautes études, describes the inscriptions of ancient Cambodia as the principal written source for reconstructing the country's history across the pre-Angkorian and Angkorian periods, roughly the fifth to the fourteenth centuries. The project, which began in earnest in 2004, continues an inventory model established by George Cœdès and now draws contributors from Cambodia, Thailand, Britain and Australia.

Chronicles are different. Burmese, Thai, Lao and Khmer chronicle traditions preserve dynastic sequences in far more narrative detail than any stele, but they were compiled well after the events and were written to establish legitimacy as much as to record. They are indispensable and they are interested parties.

Foreign records form a third layer: Chinese dynastic histories and envoy reports, and later Arabic, Portuguese and Dutch accounts. They often supply the only absolute date for a polity that left little epigraphy, and they are written by people describing a society they did not grow up in.

Physical dating is the fourth. Radiocarbon from construction fill, occupation deposits and organic inclusions dates the fabric of a site. So, indirectly, does airborne laser survey, which maps features under forest canopy and tells excavators where to dig. This layering is the same reasoning we describe in general terms in [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/); what follows is what changes when the evidence is Southeast Asian.

## Saka dates: the number on the stone is not a Gregorian year

The Saka era was carried into Southeast Asia along with Sanskrit learning and remained the standard reckoning for inscriptions across the Khmer, Cham, Javanese and Malay worlds. Its years run about seventy-eight behind the Common Era, and because the Saka year does not start on 1 January, one Saka year overlaps two CE years. That is why converted dates are so often written as a pair: 964 Saka is given as 1042–43 CE.

The consequences of getting a single character wrong are not academic. A short two-line Old Khmer inscription on a gilded bronze sword held in Boston, catalogued in the Cambodian inscription inventory as K. 1048, was long read as dated 964 Saka — 1042–43 CE, in the reign of Suryavarman I. A later publication read the same date as 862 Saka, which would place the object in the tenth century, and that reading was then cited as hard evidence for the use of mercury gilding by Khmer metalworkers a century earlier than anyone had shown. The 2010 re-edition by Gerdi Gerschheimer and Brice Vincent, working from better images, reads the date as 962 Saka and places the sword in 1040–41 CE. One digit had been carrying a claim about the history of technology.

This is the ordinary condition of the field rather than a scandal. Inscriptions are re-edited as photography, squeeze technique and comparative vocabulary improve, and a date published decades ago may be superseded. If you are checking a Southeast Asian date, it is worth asking how old the reading is.

## Chronicles and envoys: two different kinds of second-hand

Chronicle dates and inscription dates are not interchangeable. A chronicle may place a king's accession in a year that no surviving stele confirms, and it may compress or extend reigns to make a dynastic line work. Historians treat the chronicle sequence as a framework to be tested against epigraphy and archaeology, not as a ledger.

Foreign accounts carry a different bias. Chinese records name embassies and rulers with a precision local sources sometimes lack, because the imperial court kept dated registers of who arrived and when. They also render place and personal names through Chinese transcription, describe institutions through Chinese categories, and pay attention to what the court considered relevant. Used carefully they anchor chronologies; used alone they produce a Southeast Asia seen entirely from outside. The same caution applies to the European accounts that follow them, and it is the reason source criticism matters more here than raw source quantity — a theme we take further in [the guide to reading a world history timeline properly](/blog/world-history-timeline-guide/).

## What the physical record says, and where it contradicts the story

The monuments themselves supply dates that are stubbornly independent of the political narrative. UNESCO describes Angkor as an archaeological landscape of roughly 400 square kilometres containing the remains of successive capitals of the Khmer Empire from the ninth to the fifteenth century, with temples set among basins, dykes, reservoirs, canals and communication routes. Borobudur, in central Java, was built in the eighth and ninth centuries during the Syailendra dynasty and was abandoned as a Buddhist temple at some point between the tenth and fifteenth centuries — a range, not a date, because nothing records the moment. Bagan's monumental phase is placed in the eleventh to thirteenth centuries, when it was the capital of a regional empire.

Where physical evidence really bites is on famous turning points. Angkor is popularly said to have fallen in 1431. A 2019 study in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, using geoarchaeological data from the urban core, found a protracted decline in land-use intensity through the fourteenth century rather than an abrupt demographic collapse, and argued against explanations that make foreign intervention the decisive cause. The famous date still marks a military event; it no longer works as the date a city emptied. Long-distance commerce in the region is a related story, and the maritime and overland networks that connected it are covered in [the beginners' guide to the Silk Road](/blog/the-silk-road-for-beginners/).

## A practical checking routine

When you meet a Southeast Asian date you intend to rely on, four questions usually resolve it. What kind of evidence is behind it — inscription, chronicle, foreign report, or physical dating? If it is an inscription, which era is it counting in, and has the reading been revised? If it is a chronicle, what is the gap between the event and the compilation? And does the physical record agree, or is the date marking a political event rather than a material change?

That routine will not give you certainty on the pre-Angkorian centuries, because certainty is not available there. It will keep you from repeating a converted date as though it were carved that way.
