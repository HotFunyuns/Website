**Mathematics anxiety is the term researchers use for tension and apprehension that arise specifically around numbers and mathematical tasks.** It is measured with self-report questionnaires, it is treated as separate from mathematical ability, and the reason it matters is practical: it changes performance on tasks a person can otherwise do.

This article sets out what the research describes, not what to do about distress. That boundary is deliberate and it is stated again at the end.

## What the term covers, and what it does not

Math anxiety is a described pattern rather than a category anyone should be assigning to themselves from a web page. It shows up as apprehension before a task involving numbers, avoidance of situations that require them, and physical tension in the moment — and it is assessed separately from how well a person actually does the mathematics. That separation is the whole point: a person can be both anxious and competent, which is a combination that self-perception handles badly.

It is also not the same thing as being slow or out of practice. Those have their own causes, several of which are distinguishable from your own record, as [the five causes of slow mental arithmetic](/blog/why-am-i-slow-at-mental-math/) sets out. Anxiety sits alongside that diagnosis rather than inside it.

## The working-memory account

The most developed explanation of why anxiety lowers performance is competition for a limited resource. Working memory holds the intermediate values a calculation needs. Worrying thoughts occupy part of that same capacity, so there is less of it available for the arithmetic.

Finell and colleagues' [meta-analysis in Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8811497/) tested that pathway directly. Pooling eight studies with fifteen effect sizes and 1,824 participants, they reported a significant negative indirect effect of math anxiety on performance operating through working memory, with a mean indirect effect of −0.092. They are explicit that the small number of studies examining this specific mediation means the result should be read cautiously — which is a caveat worth carrying, because this finding is often reported elsewhere as though it were settled.

The direction of the relationship has also been examined over time. Pellizzoni and colleagues' [longitudinal study in the Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9304239/) found working memory acting as a mediator between anxiety and mathematics achievement rather than the reverse, and reported that math anxiety's role as a direct predictor emerged over time rather than being present from the start.

What none of this supports is the popular shorthand that anxiety "blocks" mathematical thinking outright. The mechanism described is narrower and more specific: capacity that would otherwise be spent on the problem is spent elsewhere. The general account of that capacity, and of what training does and does not do to it, is in [what working memory is and can training change it](/blog/working-memory-and-training/).

## The self-assessment problem

There is a second effect, less discussed and arguably more consequential for adults.

Pearl and colleagues studied 685 US adults completing fraction arithmetic problems with confidence judgements made before and after the task. Their [Frontiers in Psychology paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13190410/) reports that adults with higher math anxiety monitored their own performance significantly less accurately, and specifically that they were *more underconfident* — judging themselves as less capable than their actual performance demonstrated, even after accounting for prior fraction knowledge.

That is not a benign miscalibration. Underconfidence leads to avoiding tasks you could in fact handle, and avoidance removes the practice that would make them easier. A person who believes they cannot do percentages will not attempt percentages, and the belief becomes harder to test. If that describes you, a reasonable first step is picking a narrow, well-defined skill with a clear method — [percentages in your head](/blog/percentages-in-your-head/) is a good candidate, because the whole subject reduces to two anchors — and finding out empirically how it goes.

## What actually lowers the pressure of a practice session

Very little of this is about mathematics. The conditions that make a numbers task feel threatening are mostly conditions of the task, and they are adjustable.

**Remove the clock first.** A countdown adds a second thing to worry about, and the worry competes for the same capacity the sum needs. Timing can be reintroduced later, once accuracy is stable.

**Keep sessions short and frequent.** Five minutes several times a week is a lower commitment to face than a long session, and the retrieval practice benefits from being spread out anyway.

**Start below your level, deliberately.** Beginning where the work is easy establishes that the session is survivable. This is the opposite of the usual instinct to prove something.

**Separate practice from performance.** A drill with no consequences is not an exam, and treating it as one imports exactly the pressure you are trying to avoid.

**Expect the first minutes to feel worse than the rest.** Task initiation is generally the hard part, and a small fixed starting ritual is more reliable than waiting to feel ready.

## Where a practice app fits, stated honestly

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and it ships several features that reduce the pressure of a session. Accuracy Trainer has no timer, runs ten questions, offers a hint and gives feedback with an explanation after each one. Difficulty labels start low — single-digit addition, times tables from 1 to 9 — and the memory levels begin at a beginner setting with three or four items and a slow reveal. The adaptive setting adjusts to your performance but cannot drop below a level's floor, so a bad run does not send you backwards indefinitely. Replay is always free.

Those are comfort features, and that is the whole of the claim. The app does not diagnose, assess or treat anything, produces no test result, and makes no claim about intelligence or about anxiety. It records score, accuracy, streak, response time and personal bests on its own drills.
