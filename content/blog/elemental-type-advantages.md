If fire beats ice and water beats fire, a battle becomes less about raw strength than about the right match-up. The Pokémon series' type chart is the best-known example, and our own dragon battler leans on elemental strategy too. This guide explains the mathematics behind them and how to build a team that is hard to counter, using Wikipedia's articles on intransitive games and rock-paper-scissors.

## The cycle at the heart of every type chart

Type systems rest on an old idea. Wikipedia defines an **intransitive game** as one in which pairwise contests between strategies form a cycle: if A beats B, B beats C and C beats A, the relation "beats" is intransitive, because transitivity would require A to beat C. Its prototypical example is **rock-paper-scissors**.

The cycle is what keeps a game interesting. If one type beat all the others, everyone would pick it. In a cycle, the best choice always depends on what the other side has.

| Your choice | Beats | Loses to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rock | Scissors | Paper |
| Paper | Rock | Scissors |
| Scissors | Paper | Rock |

## From winning to damage multipliers

In the Pokémon series, type does not decide a battle outright; it changes damage. There, Wikipedia explains, types interact in rock-paper-scissors-style relationships: a creature takes **double damage** from attacking moves of types it is weak to and **half damage** from types it resists, and some types have special properties beyond the damage chart. A ×2 or ×0.5 multiplier makes type advantage a strong lean rather than a guarantee, leaving room for level, skills and tactics.

## What a balanced chart looks like

Wikipedia notes that rock-paper-scissors variants usually use an **odd number of moves**, in which **each move beats exactly half of the others and loses to the other half**. Versions with up to 101 moves have been published. The five-move variant rock-paper-scissors-lizard-Spock, invented by Sam Kass and Karen Bryla, gives each move two wins and two losses. More moves also make ties rarer: in an odd-numbered balanced game with *n* moves, the chance that two random picks tie is **1/*n***, so a third with three moves and a fifth with five.

Unbalanced charts behave differently. Wikipedia describes a French variant with a fourth move, the well, that beats both rock and scissors; rock has no advantage over the well, so the optimal strategy is to play paper, scissors and well a third of the time each, and never rock. A chart with a weak option effectively has fewer real choices.

## A worked example: covering a seven-element chart

Suppose, as an illustration, a game with **seven elements**, arranged in a balanced cycle in which each element beats the next three around the circle and loses to the three before it. Number the elements 0 to 6:

- Element 0 beats 1, 2 and 3.
- Element 4 beats 5, 6 and 0.
- Element 1 beats 2, 3 and 4.

Together, elements **0, 4 and 1** beat all seven: every possible opponent is beaten by at least one of them. Three is also the minimum, because each element beats only three others and two elements could beat at most six. A team of a single element, by contrast, beats only three of the seven: it loses to three and merely ties against its own element.

The same check works for any chart, however many types it has: list what each team member beats, and look for opposing types nobody covers.

## Building a balanced team

1. **Cover the cycle.** Make sure every opposing type is beaten by at least one team member.
2. **Avoid shared weaknesses.** Two members weak to the same type give opponents a double target.
3. **Keep one neutral option.** A strong all-rounder is useful against a team you cannot read.
4. **Check the actual chart.** Real games are not always perfectly balanced; some types have extra resistances or immunities, so test your team against the strongest opponents you face.
