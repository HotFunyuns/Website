Achaemenid Persia was governed through provincial officials — satraps — sitting above a central administration that ran on written, sealed and audited records. We know this in unusual detail because the empire's own paperwork survives: thousands of clay tablets from Persepolis recording who was fed, how much, on whose authority, and against what receipt.

That is a very different picture from the one most readers inherit, which comes from Greek writers describing wars Persia fought against Greeks.

## The source problem, first

Before any claim about Persian government, it is worth knowing where the evidence comes from, because the imbalance shapes everything.

Encyclopaedia Iranica's article on Darius I, by Alireza Shapur Shahbazi, sorts the primary material into four kinds. First, Darius's own record relief at Bisotun. Second, administrative and archival material from Persepolis, Susa, Babylon and Egypt — plus outliers such as a fragmentary Old Persian inscription from Gherla in Romania and a letter from Darius to Gadates preserved only in a Greek text of the Roman period. Third, Herodotus. Fourth, briefer notices by other classical authors and a few references in the Bible.

Two of those four categories are Persian. Two are Greek or later. And it is the Greek material that was copied, taught and transmitted continuously in Europe, while the Persian administrative record sat in the ground until modern excavation and then had to be deciphered before anyone could read a line of it.

None of that makes Herodotus useless. It does mean that anyone working on Persia has to keep straight which claims rest on a Persian document, which rest on an outsider's account of an adversary, and which rest on both — exactly the discipline set out in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/).

## The Persepolis tablets: an empire's expense claims

The single most useful body of evidence for how the empire actually worked is an administrative archive.

Richard Hallock transcribed, interpreted and edited over 2,100 Elamite texts from the Persepolis Fortification archive, published in 1969 as *Persepolis Fortification Tablets*, volume 92 of Oriental Institute Publications. The Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures, which holds them, describes the published corpus as already forming the largest coherent body of material on Persian administration available to us — and notes that a comparable but less legible body remains unpublished, along with a smaller group of Aramaic texts from the same archive.

The texts deal with the movement and expenditure of food commodities in the region of Persepolis across roughly fifteen years down to 493 BCE. Three things fall out of them.

**Everyone in the state sphere was on a scale.** The tablets make it clear that people working within the state economy received a fixed ration-scale — or, as the Institute's summary puts it, since some rations are on a scale impossible for an individual to consume, a fixed *salary* expressed in terms of commodities. That is a payroll, not charity.

**Travel was authorised in writing.** Travellers along the road carried sealed documents issued by the king or by officials of satrapal level, stating the scale on which they were entitled to be fed. You could not simply turn up at a way station and eat; you produced a document specifying your entitlement.

**The paperwork came back.** Tablets sealed by both supplier and recipient were returned to Persepolis, which means the centre was reconciling issue against receipt. Somebody was checking.

A state that pays salaries in kind, issues sealed travel warrants and files countersigned receipts is not the oriental despotism of the Greek literary tradition. It is a bureaucracy.

## Satraps and the shape of the provinces

The word "satrap" appears in these documents as an administrative level, not a literary flourish — officials "of satrapal level" are one of the categories authorised to issue travel entitlements.

This is worth stressing because the satrapy is usually described from the top down, as a map of provinces. The archive shows it from the bottom up, as a chain of authority that a road-station clerk could verify. A system is only as real as its lowest enforcement point, and here the enforcement point is a sealed tablet handed over in exchange for flour.

The documentary footprint also gives a sense of reach without anyone having to estimate square kilometres. Administrative material survives from Persepolis, Susa, Babylon and Egypt. That is four distinct scribal and legal traditions operating under one crown, in different languages, with the central archive kept in Elamite and a parallel Aramaic strand alongside it.

## Bisotun: a monument that is also an argument

About 60 metres up a limestone cliff in what is now Kermanshah province, on one of the main routes linking Persia with Mesopotamia, Darius I had a relief and inscription cut in 521 BCE. UNESCO calls it unique: the only known monumental text of the Achaemenids to document a specific historic event, namely the re-establishment of the empire by Darius.

The inscription is about 15 metres high and 25 metres wide, in three cuneiform languages — Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian — and it was the first cuneiform writing to be deciphered in the nineteenth century. UNESCO compares its value for Assyriology to that of the Rosetta Stone for Egyptology. Without Bisotun, a great deal of Mesopotamian history would still be unreadable.

Now read it as a document rather than a monument. Much of it celebrates Darius's victories over numerous pretenders to the throne. The bas-relief shows him holding a bow as a sign of sovereignty and treading on the chest of a prostrate figure. Encyclopaedia Iranica notes that Darius began what it calls his "autobiography" with a genealogy *purporting* to establish his right to the Achaemenid throne.

That single word — purporting — is the whole methodological point. Bisotun is, in UNESCO's assessment, the only known monumental Achaemenid text recording a specific historic event, and it is a legitimation document commissioned by a man whose accession was contested. Both things are true at once.

## Cyrus and the language of kingship

The empire's founder left his own claims, and they are similarly revealing. Encyclopaedia Iranica's article by Muhammad Dandamayev records that at Pasargadae Cyrus styled himself "I am Kūru the king, an Achaemenid", while an inscription from the Babylonian city of Ur opens "Kura, king of all the world, king of the land of Anshan". On the Cyrus cylinder from Babylon he called himself son of Cambyses, grandson of Cyrus, of a family that always exercised kingship.

Notice what changes with the audience. In Persia he is an Achaemenid. In Babylon he is king of all the world and heir to a dynasty of kings. Royal titles are addressed to somebody, and reading them as neutral descriptions of territory is a mistake.

This is also why claims that any ancient empire was "the largest of its age" should be handled carefully. Such figures are modern estimates built from mapped zones of control, and they depend on whether you count directly administered provinces, tribute-paying regions, or the lands a royal inscription simply claims. The tablets tell you where the salary system reached. The inscriptions tell you what the king wanted asserted. Those are not the same map.

## Persepolis as architecture and as statement

The terrace at Persepolis was begun around 518 BCE by Darius the Great, and successive kings built on it — Darius I (522–486 BCE), Xerxes I (486–465 BCE) and Artaxerxes I (465–424 BCE) raising the Apadana palace and the Throne Hall, also called the Hundred-Column Hall, across a 13-hectare half-natural, half-artificial platform.

UNESCO's description credits the Achaemenid architects with genuine engineering skill: by engineering lighter roofs and using wooden lintels they supported wide open spans on an astonishingly small number of slender columns, topped with the distinctive double-bull capitals. The design borrowed openly from Mesopotamian models, with Assyrianesque gateways and gigantic sculpted winged bulls.

Borrowing is not a weakness in this context. An empire assembled from Elamite, Babylonian, Median and other traditions built a ceremonial capital that looked like all of them at once, which is itself a statement about what the state claimed to be.
