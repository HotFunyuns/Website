In Cantonese you cannot say "two pig" or "this book" by putting the words side by side. A measure word, or classifier, has to sit between the number or demonstrative and the noun: 兩隻豬, "two pigs"; 呢本書, "this book". Cantonese then gives those same words jobs that Mandarin does not. This guide covers the common measure words with their Jyutping readings from Wiktionary, then the grammar that makes Cantonese classifiers distinctive. If the tone numbers are new, [Jyutping explained](/blog/jyutping-explained/) shows how to read them.

## What a measure word does

Wikipedia's article on Chinese classifiers explains that most nouns have one or more particular classifiers associated with them, often depending on the nature of what they denote: flat objects such as tables, paper and beds take 張, and long, thin objects take 條. It adds that everyday speech often calls all of these "measure words", 量詞, although they are not all the same kind of word. The same article stresses variability: across dialects and speakers, people often do not agree which classifier is best for a given noun.

## The core set with Jyutping

| Classifier | Jyutping | Used for, per Wiktionary | Cantonese example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 個 | go3 | people; things without a specific classifier; money | — |
| 隻 | zek3 | birds and certain animals; one of a pair; some utensils; body parts | 兩隻豬, two pigs |
| 條 | tiu4 | long, thin objects or animals; listed items; lives | 一條毛, a hair |
| 張 | zoeng1 | flat objects, sheets; in Cantonese also knives | 兩張紙, two sheets of paper |
| 本 | bun2 | books, periodicals, files | 呢本書, this book |
| 架 | gaa3 | planes, large vehicles; dialectally vehicles | 呢架車, this car |
| 對 | deoi3 | pairs | 兩對鞋, two pairs of shoes |
| 啲 | di1 | plurals and mass nouns ("some") | 呢啲車, these cars |

Note the contrast between 隻 and 對 for shoes: Wiktionary's entry for 隻 gives "a shoe" against "a pair of shoes" with a separate pair word, and its entry for 對 gives 兩對鞋, "two pairs of shoes".

## Where Cantonese differs: chairs, people and ages

A few uses are specifically Cantonese.

- **Chairs take 張.** Wikipedia notes that in Standard Chinese a bench can take 條, but that in Cantonese 條 cannot be used for 櫈, a stool or bench; 張 is used instead. Wiktionary's Cantonese example is 幾張凳, "several chairs".
- **People.** 個 go3 is the general classifier for people, but Wikipedia notes that some Yue dialects use 隻 for people. Wiktionary also lists a Cantonese use of 條 for people that is often derogatory, as in 嗰條女, "that girl", so it is one to recognise rather than use.
- **Ages and digits.** Wiktionary lists Cantonese 張 for ages in multiples of ten from thirty upwards, as in 三張嘢, "thirty-odd years old", and 條 for runs of the same digit: 三條九 is 999.

## A worked example: the same noun, four constructions

Take 書 *syu1*, "book", with its classifier 本 *bun2*, using examples from Wikipedia's Cantonese grammar article:

1. **This**: 呢本書 *ni1 bun2 syu1*, "this book". Wikipedia notes that 呢 is often said 依 *ji1* in fast speech.
2. **That**: 嗰本書 *go2 bun2 syu1*, "that book".
3. **Possession**: 佢本書 *keoi5 bun2 syu1*, "his book", where the classifier replaces the possessive 嘅 *ge3*. The Chinese classifier article gives the parallel 我本書 *ngo5 bun2 syu1*, "my book".
4. **Plural**: 呢啲書 follows the pattern of 呢啲車, "these cars", with 啲 *di1* taking the classifier's place.

In the first three, 本 stays put and only the word in front of it changes; in the plural, 啲 takes the place of 本.

## Bare classifiers: when the measure word means "the"

Wikipedia explains that in both Cantonese and Mandarin a classifier can individualise a noun, but that in Mandarin such a phrase is indefinite unless a demonstrative or "every" is present, and is heavily restricted in subject position. Cantonese has neither restriction. Its example is 架車撻唔著 *gaa3 ce1 taat3 m4 zoek6*, "the car won't start", which cannot be read as "the cars". The same article notes that classifiers cannot mark possession in Mandarin, which makes this one of the clearer grammar points in [Cantonese vs Mandarin](/blog/cantonese-vs-mandarin-differences/).

## Counting with numbers

With a number, the order is number, classifier, noun, and two becomes 兩 *loeng5*: Wiktionary's examples include 兩隻豬, 兩張紙 and 兩對鞋. Wiktionary also gives 聽講貓有九條命, "I've heard cats have nine lives", with 條 counting lives. For the numbers themselves, see [Cantonese numbers](/blog/cantonese-numbers/). Vietnamese uses a similar slot with its own set of words; compare [Vietnamese classifiers](/blog/vietnamese-classifiers/).
