When a car crashes or debris lands on the track, race control has to slow the field without stopping the race. Formula 1 has two tools for that: the **virtual safety car** (VSC) and the **safety car** (SC). This guide follows the FIA's 2026 Formula 1 sporting regulations, Section B, Articles B5.12 and B5.13. Other series write their own rules, so details differ outside F1.

## Which one, and when

| | Virtual safety car | Safety car |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Used when | Double waved yellows are needed and people may be in danger, but a safety car is not warranted | Competitors or officials are in immediate physical danger, but stopping the race is not necessary |
| Signals | "VSC DEPLOYED" message; FIA light panels show "VSC" | "SAFETY CAR DEPLOYED"; panels show "SC"; waved yellows and "SC" boards at every marshal post |
| Speed control | Each car must stay above a minimum time set by the FIA ECU in every marshalling sector | Cars queue behind a real car with orange lights |
| Gaps | No queue forms; each car slows where it is | Bunched to no more than 10 car lengths apart |

Wikipedia records that Formula 1 officially introduced the safety car in 1993, after trials in 1992, and the virtual safety car for the 2015 season; the VSC's first use was at the 2015 Monaco Grand Prix.

## Rules under the virtual safety car

Under a VSC, the regulations require that:

- no car is driven unnecessarily slowly, erratically or dangerously;
- every car stays above the minimum time at least once in each marshalling sector and at both safety car lines, or risks a 5-second, 10-second, drive-through or stop-and-go penalty;
- **no overtaking**, except when entering or leaving the pits at the safety car lines, within the pit lane, or past a car with an obvious problem;
- no car enters the pits during a race unless it is to change tyres.

When race control ends the VSC, drivers get a "VSC ENDING" message, and 10 to 15 seconds later the panels turn green and racing resumes immediately.

## Rules behind the safety car

The safety car joins the track with its orange lights on, wherever the leader is. Then:

1. **Queue up.** Cars reduce speed and form a queue no more than **10 car lengths** apart, or 20 in low visibility at the race director's discretion.
2. **No overtaking** until the control line after the safety car pits, except when signalled by its green light, at pit entry and exit, in the pit lane, or past a car with an obvious problem.
3. **Clear the field.** Cars between the safety car and the leader may be waved past with the green light. If the race director allows, the message "LAPPED CARS MAY NOW OVERTAKE" releases lapped cars to pass the leaders and rejoin the back of the queue; the safety car then normally comes in at the end of the following lap.
4. **Pit lane option.** The race director may route the whole field through the pit lane; a car that fails to follow that instruction gets a drive-through penalty.

## The restart

The message "SAFETY CAR IN THIS LAP" and the safety car's orange lights going out signal that it will pit at the end of the lap. From then, the **leader dictates the pace** and may drop back beyond 10 car lengths. Until the safety car has entered the pit entry road, no erratic acceleration or braking is allowed, and no car may be alongside the car ahead. Green flags and lights appear at the control line as the leader approaches, and overtaking resumes there.

Every lap under the SC or VSC counts as a race lap. If the safety car is still out at the start of the last lap, it comes in at the end of it and cars take the chequered flag without overtaking.

## Why safety cars decide races

Wikipedia sums up the effect: a safety car bunches the field together, erasing any lead the front-runner had built. It also creates an opportunity, because a pit stop made while everyone else laps slowly costs less time relative to rivals, and the car that pits rejoins the queue. That is why a safety car can reshape strategy, and why a well-timed one can turn [the undercut and overcut](/blog/undercut-vs-overcut/) on its head.
