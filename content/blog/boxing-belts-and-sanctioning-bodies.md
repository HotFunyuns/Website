Boxing has four major sanctioning bodies — the World Boxing Association (WBA), World Boxing Council (WBC), International Boxing Federation (IBF) and World Boxing Organization (WBO) — and each one recognises its own world champion in every weight class. That is why a single division can have four "world champions" at once. A boxer who holds two or more of those belts is a **unified** champion; one who holds all four at the same time is **undisputed**. A **lineal** champion is a different idea altogether: an unofficial title traced fight by fight through whoever beat the previous holder. An **interim** title is a stand-in, created when the full champion is unavailable.

The definitions below come from the organisations' own rulebooks as published on 2 October 2026, from the 2000 federal law that regulates them in the United States, and, for the two terms no organisation owns, from reference histories.

## Four organisations, one division

The system grew by splitting. The National Boxing Association, formed by American state bodies, became the WBA in 1962; the WBC was established in Mexico City in 1963. The IBF followed in 1983 and the WBO in 1988. The older bodies then came round to recognising the newest one's champions — the WBA by 2001, the WBC from 2004 and the IBF by early 2007 — which is what made a four-belt "undisputed" title possible as a goal.

Before those splits, the main American authorities usually recognised the same champion, so there was seldom a rival claim to settle.

## The title vocabulary, as the bodies define it

| Term | What it means | Where it is defined |
| --- | --- | --- |
| World champion | The champion one sanctioning body recognises in a division | Each body's rules |
| Unified | Also recognised as world champion by one or more other bodies in a comparable division | WBA rule C.16 |
| Undisputed | Holding the titles of all four major bodies at once | Reference usage; the IBF's rules also use it |
| Interim | A stand-in title while the champion is unavailable | WBC 3.22; IBF Rules 16–17; WBA C.21; WBO section 5 |
| Super champion | A special status for an exceptional champion, with flexible defence obligations | WBA C.17; WBO section 14 |
| Champion in recess / inactive champion | A champion temporarily unable to defend, who keeps a route back to the title | WBC 3.21; WBA C.21–C.22 |
| Franchise boxer | A WBC designation for an elite champion — explicitly "not a Championship" | WBC 3.26 |
| Diamond, Silver, Emeritus | Additional WBC titles and honours, each with its own rules | WBC 3.23–3.25 |
| Lineal | Unofficial: the line of boxers who beat the reigning holder | No body awards it |

Three of these deserve a closer look, because they are the source of most confusion.

### Interim titles differ by organisation

The WBC may sanction an interim championship when it declares a champion temporarily unavailable, and its rules call the interim title "a true WBC World title for all purposes" other than a bout against the actual champion. The IBF is stricter. Under its Rule 16 an interim title follows an illness or injury exception for the champion; if the champion cannot defend by the end of that exception, the interim champion becomes champion, and no interim reign may last more than twelve months. Its Rule 17 adds a separate case for an undisputed champion, or a unified champion with three or more belts, whose IBF mandatory is delayed by other obligations. The WBO may order an interim title when a mandatory defence has not happened in time.

### "Super" and "regular" are WBA vocabulary

The WBA's rules let its championships committee and president designate a super champion "in exceptional circumstances", and add that the vacant regular title "need not be filled" — which is how a division can carry both a super and a regular WBA champion. The WBO also designates super champions, weighing criteria such as at least ten successful title defences (or defences against highly recognised opponents) and the quality of the boxer's opposition; current champion status is not required.

### Lineal belongs to nobody

The lineal title is the one fans built for themselves: the champion is whoever beat the champion. There is no single accepted method for deciding where a lineage begins, a lineage can be broken by retirement or a move between divisions, and a sanctioning body stripping a boxer is generally disregarded when tracing it. That makes it a useful check on the alphabet titles and an unreliable source of agreement.

## How belts change hands outside the ring

Winning the fight is the obvious route. The rulebooks describe several others.

- **The scale.** Under the IBF's and WBA's rules, a champion who fails to make weight loses the title on the scale. The bout can still go ahead, but only a challenger who made weight can win the vacant title.
- **The calendar.** A champion who fails to defend against the mandatory challenger in time risks losing recognition. The WBO's regulations allow its committee to vacate a title if the champion does not defend within nine months.
- **The other belts.** A WBO champion may not hold WBO titles in two divisions; one who wins a second has ten days to choose, and the other is declared vacant.
- **Elevation.** An IBF interim champion becomes the full champion if the champion cannot defend by the end of an approved exception.

## Why undisputed is hard to keep

Each body runs its own mandatory-defence clock, and an undisputed champion is subject to all four.

| Body | Mandatory defence obligation (outside heavyweight) |
| --- | --- |
| WBC | At least one mandatory defence per year |
| WBA | Within nine months of winning the title for a former official contender, or 120 days otherwise |
| IBF | Within nine months of winning, then at intervals of no more than nine months |
| WBO | At intervals of no more than nine months |

Heavyweights generally get longer: twelve months for a first IBF defence, and twelve-month intervals at the WBO.

**Worked example.** Assume an undisputed champion outside heavyweight, and that each mandatory challenger must be fought separately. Three of the four bodies require a mandatory within about nine months or less, and the fourth within a year. That is four mandatory fights inside about a year — one every 91 days or so if they were spread evenly — before any voluntary defence or unification bout. The assumption is the catch: the bodies sometimes extend a deadline for a unification fight, and the IBF's rules acknowledge a rotation system among the four organisations, with interim titles filling the gap while a champion works through obligations in turn.

The arithmetic is one reason a four-belt reign can end without a defeat, through a vacated or stripped title, with no bad faith required from anyone.

## What US law asks of the sanctioning bodies

Congress addressed the bodies directly in the Muhammad Ali Boxing Reform Act of 2000. Its findings state that the sanctioning organisations "which have proliferated in the boxing industry have not established credible and objective criteria to rate professional boxers".

The act responded with disclosure rather than a single official ranking. A sanctioning organisation may not receive compensation in connection with a bout unless, by 31 January each year, it files with the Federal Trade Commission and the ABC a description of its ratings criteria, policies and general sanctioning fee schedule, its bylaws, its appeals procedure, and a list of the officials who vote on ratings. The act also directed the ABC to develop guidelines for objective and consistent ratings criteria.
