The French Revolution is not one event, and the single biggest improvement you can make to your understanding of it is to stop treating it as one. A fiscal crisis in 1787-89, a liberal constitutional experiment, a European war from 1792, the Terror of 1793-94, and the Thermidorian reaction that followed are five distinct things with different causes, different leading figures and different supporters. The Terror was not latent in the Declaration of the Rights of Man, and the Declaration was not an inevitable consequence of a bankrupt treasury. Establishing the sequence first is what makes the causal argument tractable.

## The crisis that forced the opening

The monarchy could not pay its debts and could not raise taxes without consent. That is the mechanical trigger, and it is why the Estates General — an assembly of the realm's three orders that had not met for generations — was convened at all.

The Château de Versailles dates the meeting to "4-5 May 1789" and the Tennis Court Oath, in which deputies bound themselves not to disperse until France had a constitution, to "20 June 1789". Within weeks the question had shifted from how to fund a deficit to who had the authority to answer that question.

Behind the immediate crisis sat slower pressures: a tax structure that exempted the wealthiest orders, the accumulated cost of foreign wars, poor harvests and high bread prices, and decades of political argument that had already made royal authority something to be justified rather than assumed. Keeping trigger and precondition apart is the whole discipline here. The deficit explains the timing. It does not explain why the response took the form of a constitution. The same separation is what keeps the argument over [what caused the Bronze Age collapse](/blog/what-caused-the-bronze-age-collapse/) from collapsing into a search for one villain, and it works identically in a period with far better documentation.

## 1789 as a documented year

The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen was approved by the National Assembly on 26 August 1789. The Avalon Project at Yale Law School holds the text, and its opening articles are worth reading rather than paraphrasing: "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Social distinctions may be founded only upon the general good", and "The aim of all political association is the preservation of the natural and imprescriptible rights of man. These rights are liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression."

In October the revolution physically relocated. The Château de Versailles records the king's departure from the palace on 6 October 1789, the moment the court ceased to be the centre of French political life. It is worth noticing what that institutional chronology foregrounds and what it leaves out: the storming of the Bastille on 14 July 1789, the event most readers name first, happened in Paris and does not appear in the palace's own list of key dates. Different institutions build different chronologies of the same year, which is exactly the phenomenon our guide to [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) describes.

## War, and what it did to domestic politics

From 1792 France was at war with a coalition of European powers, and this is the hinge that most short accounts skip.

War changes the meaning of every domestic dispute. Opposition becomes potential treason; shortage becomes suspected hoarding; a general's defeat becomes evidence of conspiracy. It also creates the administrative machinery — requisition, emergency committees, surveillance — that emergency government then uses. Any explanation of the Terror that does not have the war in it is incomplete.

That does not make the war a sufficient explanation, and here historians genuinely divide. One tradition reads the Terror as circumstantial: invasion, civil war in the Vendée, and real conspiracies produced real emergency measures. Another reads it as ideological: a politics founded on a single indivisible general will has no legitimate category for opposition, and the machinery follows from the idea. A third focuses on institutional competition between factions in which escalation was individually rational and collectively catastrophic. The evidence supports parts of all three, and the argument has run for two centuries without resolution — which is a feature of the subject, not a failure of it.

## Reading the revolution from its own documents

The most useful thing a general reader can do with this topic is spend an hour in the sources.

*Liberty, Equality, Fraternity: Exploring the French Revolution*, produced by the Roy Rosenzweig Center for History and New Media at George Mason University with the American Social History Project at the City University of New York, holds 250 images, 350 text documents, 13 songs and 13 maps alongside twelve topical essays. The Archives nationales, France's national archives at Pierrefitte-sur-Seine and Paris, make more than 10 million images available through their virtual reading room with some 31,000 finding aids.

Working from documents changes the questions you ask. A printed decree, a song, a caricature and a petition are different kinds of evidence with different reach, and knowing which is which is the substance of our guide to [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/).

The CHNM project also frames the period as 1787 to 1815 — beginning before the Estates General and ending with the restoration. That choice treats the Napoleonic years as part of the revolution rather than as what came after it, and it is a real interpretive claim rather than a filing convenience.

## The contradiction the revolution did not resolve

A declaration that men are born free and equal in rights was issued by a state whose colonial economy ran on slavery. The CHNM archive treats this directly, with essays on "the complicated relationship between revolutionary ideals and slavery that was practiced in the French colonies".

The consequences ran outward. The revolution in Saint-Domingue that began in 1791 and ended in Haitian independence was the largest and most successful revolt of enslaved people in modern history, and it is not a footnote to French events — it is a revolution with its own leadership, aims and chronology, and it deserves to be read as one rather than absorbed into a Paris-centred narrative. Any account of 1789 that mentions universal rights without mentioning who was excluded from them has edited the record.

## What to take away

**Five phases, not one event.** Fiscal crisis, constitutional experiment, war, Terror, reaction. Name which one you are talking about.

**The war belongs in the explanation of the Terror**, but it does not finish the explanation, and the competing accounts are worth knowing in outline.

**The end date is an argument.** 1794, 1799, 1804 and 1815 are all defensible, and choosing one commits you to a claim about what the revolution was.
