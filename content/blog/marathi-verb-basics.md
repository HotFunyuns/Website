तू कसा आहेस? and तू कशी आहेस? are both "how are you". The first is addressed to a man, the second to a woman, and the thing that moved is not a pronoun or a politeness marker — it is agreement, reaching into the shortest sentence in the language.

If you understand why that happens, Marathi verbs stop being a list of endings and become a single question you ask over and over: *what is this verb agreeing with?*

## The verb comes last

Marathi is subject–object–verb. मी पाणी पितो is, word for word, "I water drink".

Understanding this takes a second. Producing it takes months, and it is worth naming the reason: with the verb at the end, you cannot start a sentence until you know how it finishes. English lets you begin "I want..." and improvise the rest. Marathi makes you commit to the object first and land the verb afterwards.

The related habit is postpositions. Where English puts *in*, *to* and *from* before the noun, Marathi puts its equivalents after it, fused onto the word as case endings. घरात (*gharāta*) is "in the house"; घरी (*gharī*) is closer to "at home". The two carry different shades — one is location inside a well-defined space, the other a looser sense of place.

## Start with आहे, because it buys the most

The present tense of *to be* is the fastest route to complete sentences, and its forms are few:

- **मी ... आहे** — *mī ... āhe* — I am
- **तू ... आहेस** — *tu ... āhes* — you are (informal)
- **आपण / तुम्ही ... आहात** — *āpaṇ / tumhī ... āhāt* — you are (polite or plural)
- **आम्ही ... आहोत** — *āmhī ... āhot* — we are (exclusive)

मी ठीक आहे. माझं नाव ... आहे. तुम्ही कसे आहात. Three sentences, correct, useful on day one, and none of them requiring any other verb.

Notice आपण doing double duty. It is the polite "you" and it is also the *inclusive* we — "you and I" — as against आम्ही, "us but not you". Marathi keeps a distinction English and Hindi both lack, and the pronoun you pick changes the verb ending after it.

## Agreement: the real content of Marathi verbs

Marathi verbs inflect for tense — past, present, future — and agree for gender and number. Nouns carry one of three genders: masculine, feminine or neuter. Marathi preserves the neuter that most modern Indo-Aryan languages have lost.

So the verb ending is not a function of *who is speaking* in the way a European conjugation table is. It is a function of the gender and number of whatever the verb has been told to agree with.

In the simple present with an intransitive verb, that is the subject:

- तो जातो — *to jāto* — he goes
- ती जाते — *tī jāte* — she goes

Same verb, same tense, different ending, and the only thing that changed is the gender of the subject. This is also why the ordinary goodbye is येतो from a man and येते from a woman — a detail covered alongside the other everyday formulas in [Marathi phrases worth learning first](/blog/marathi-phrases-with-meaning/).

## Then the past tense moves the goalposts

Here is the part that surprises people who have quietly decided that "the verb agrees with the subject".

Marathi follows a split-ergative pattern. It is ergative in constructions with perfective transitive verbs and with obligative ones — the "should" and "have to" forms — and nominative everywhere else.

In an ergative construction, the subject takes a special marker and the verb agrees with the **object** instead. Change the object's gender and the verb ending changes, even though the person doing the action has not moved.

There is a third case as well, and it is the one with no English handhold at all: a construction where the verb agrees with neither the subject nor the object, producing a voice that English has no name for.

None of this needs to be produced in your first month. All of it will be heard in your first month, which is why it is better to know the shape of the problem early than to be ambushed by it later. A Hindi speaker gets a partial head start here, since Hindi also has perfective ergativity — but only partial, and [Marathi and Hindi compared](/blog/marathi-vs-hindi-differences/) sets out where the overlap ends.

## A minimum working sentence

1. **Pick the subject and know its gender.** If it is a person, natural sex usually decides it. If it is an object, you are relying on how the noun ends, and those are tendencies rather than rules.
2. **Put the object next**, with its postposition attached if it needs one.
3. **Choose the verb and ask what it is agreeing with.** Present tense, intransitive: the subject. Perfective past, transitive: the object.
4. **Attach the ending that matches** that element's gender and number.

Four steps, and step three is the whole game.

A table presents eight or twelve forms at once and asks the learner to infer which dimension each cell varies along, and the inference most beginners draw from a Marathi table is that the endings vary by person, because that is the pattern they have met in every other language they have studied.
