Mainland Southeast Asia is usually skipped between the fall of Angkor and the arrival of European empires, which leaves five modern countries with no pre-colonial history in most readers' minds. The region was in fact densely political: several substantial states, with different religious foundations, different writing systems and different relationships to China and India, competing and trading across the same river valleys for centuries.

This is a guide to who was where, kept deliberately at the pre-colonial and early-colonial end. The twentieth-century histories of Myanmar and Laos in particular run into live political conflict, and doing them justice needs a different article and a different standard of sourcing.

## Bagan and the Irrawaddy states

UNESCO describes Bagan as a sacred landscape on a bend of the Ayeyarwady River in central Myanmar, a serial property of seven components containing temples, stupas, monasteries, pilgrimage places, archaeological remains, frescoes and sculptures. It dates the peak of Bagan civilization to the eleventh to thirteenth centuries CE, when the site was the capital of a regional empire.

Bagan is a Theravada Buddhist state that built at extraordinary density, and the volume of construction is itself the evidence: sustained monumental patronage on that scale implies a functioning agricultural surplus and a system for converting it into merit-making. Later Burmese states — the Taungoo and then the Konbaung — continued from different centres.

## Sukhothai, Ayutthaya and the Siamese sequence

UNESCO records Sukhothai as the capital of the first Kingdom of Siam in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, and describes a civilization that absorbed numerous influences and ancient local traditions, assimilating them rapidly into what is known as the Sukhothai style.

Ayutthaya follows. UNESCO dates its founding to about 1350, calls it the second Siamese capital after Sukhothai, and records that it was destroyed by the Burmese in the eighteenth century, leaving remains characterised by *prang* — reliquary towers — and very large monasteries.

Two points are worth pulling out. The "first kingdom of Siam" framing is a national periodisation with its own history, and it compresses a more complicated picture of contemporaneous Tai polities into a single royal line. And the destruction of Ayutthaya is a reminder that the major violence in this region was overwhelmingly between its own states, not between the region and outsiders.

## Lan Xang

The Lao kingdom is the one most often missing entirely. UNESCO's inscription for the Town of Luang Prabang records that the town, known earlier as Muang Sua and then Xieng Thong, became the capital of the powerful kingdom of Lane Xang — the Kingdom of a Million Elephants — from the fourteenth to the sixteenth century, and that it was also the centre of Buddhism in the region.

Further south, UNESCO's Vat Phou inscription covers a planned landscape more than a thousand years old in Champasak, laid out along an axis from mountain top to river bank, with a development range from the fifth to the fifteenth centuries mainly associated with the Khmer empire. That is a useful corrective: the territory of modern Laos contains both Lao and Khmer imperial heritage, and the modern border is not a historical one.

## Dai Viet and Champa

Vietnam's sequence is the best documented on the mainland, because it was recorded in a Chinese-derived historiographical tradition.

Columbia University's Asia for Educators timeline gives the key sequence: Ngo Quyen defeating a Chinese force in 939 CE and ending Chinese political domination; the Ly dynasty from 1010 to 1225 establishing Dai Viet with its capital at Thang Long — "Emergent Dragon", today's Hanoi — and founding the Temple of Literature in 1070 while developing chu nom, a character set for writing Vietnamese; the Tran dynasty from 1225, repelling Mongol invasions in the 1280s; a Ming occupation from 1407 to 1427; and the Le dynasty from 1428, running a Confucian-style state with examinations.

Champa is the state that existed alongside it and then stopped existing. UNESCO describes a culture with spiritual origins in Indian Hinduism developing on the coast of contemporary Vietnam between the fourth and thirteenth centuries, with My Son serving as the religious and political capital of the Champa kingdom for most of its existence. Columbia's timeline records Le dynasty attacks on Champa and, in the fifteenth century, the destruction of the kingdom by Viet forces.

That should be stated plainly rather than folded into a narrative of national unification. A distinct polity with its own language, religion and dynastic tradition was conquered and dismantled, and its descendants remain a minority population in Vietnam today.

Later, UNESCO records Hué as established in 1802 as the capital of a unified Vietnam, and as the political, cultural and religious centre under the Nguyen dynasty until 1945.

## Why the dates wobble

Anyone comparing two books on this region will meet conflicting dates, and the reasons are technical rather than careless.

Research on dating Pagan's city wall, fortifications and palace site sets out two problems directly. First, construction in this region used wood from long-lived species such as teak, so a radiocarbon date can reflect when the tree was alive rather than when the timber was used — the "old wood" problem, made worse when buildings were repaired with reused material. Second, the shape of the radiocarbon calibration curve over the last thousand years makes precision in exactly this period difficult.

Chronicles add their own difficulties: they were compiled later, by courts with dynastic claims, and they often use regnal or era dating that must be converted. The method for handling all of this is the subject of [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/), and the short version is that a range honestly stated beats a single year invented for neatness.

Climate history has recently given the region a new dating tool. Tree-ring series beginning as early as 1030 CE have, for the first time, made it possible to reconstruct historical climate for much of mainland Southeast Asia, and the resulting work on cyclic drought and monsoon fluctuation has been used to re-examine the fortunes of several of these states.

The practical obstacle to learning this region is that no single chapter covers it; the states appear in five different national histories.
