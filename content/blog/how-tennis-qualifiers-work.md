Most places in a tennis main draw are handed out by ranking, in order, until the field is nearly full. Qualifying is how the last few places get filled by everyone else: a short knockout event played in the days before the tournament, in which only the players who win every round get in. Everything else — wildcards, lucky losers, protected rankings — is a mechanism for handling the cases that a simple ranked list does not cover.

## Who is already in before qualifying starts

Work backwards from the draw and the structure becomes obvious.

**Direct acceptances.** The bulk of the field. The tournament takes the entry list, sorts it by ranking, and accepts down the list until the reserved places are all that remain. If you are above the cut-off, you are in and you do not play qualifying.

**Wildcards.** A small number of discretionary invitations. A wildcard is exactly what the name suggests in other sports: a berth awarded outside the normal qualification path. In tennis it typically goes to a home player, a returning player whose ranking has lapsed through injury, or someone the event particularly wants in the field.

**Reserved qualifying places.** Whatever is left. These are the places the qualifying event is competing for.

That ordering is why the cut-off matters so much to a player's season. It is not a ranking of prestige; it is a line that decides whether your tournament starts on Monday with the field or on Friday with three extra matches to win first.

## What the qualifying event actually is

A qualifying draw is its own small knockout tournament, played immediately before the main event, usually over a handful of rounds. Win them all and you take one of the reserved places. Lose any of them and your week is over.

Three things about that are worth stating plainly, because they are what make qualifying a distinct sporting problem rather than just "extra matches":

1. **There is no reward for coming close.** A player who loses the final qualifying round has beaten the same opposition as one who wins it, up to the last match, and receives a fraction of the outcome.
2. **The physical cost is front-loaded.** A qualifier arrives in the main draw having already played several matches, potentially in the same heat, on the same surface, against opponents who very much wanted to beat them.
3. **The draw does not compensate for it.** Qualifier slots sit in the bracket wherever they sit. A player who has fought through three rounds can walk into a seed on Monday.

## Lucky losers: the second chance nobody plans for

A lucky loser is a competitor who loses in qualifying but then enters the main draw when another player withdraws through illness, injury or some other reason. The re-entry usually happens before all the main-draw competitors have started their first matches.

It is a genuinely strange position to be in. You have already lost, gone through the emotional process of losing, and possibly booked a flight. Then a withdrawal puts you back in the tournament with hours of notice.

The record shows how rarely it turns into anything: it is rare for a lucky loser to win a tour-level tournament, and the list of men who have managed it since 1978 runs to fewer than a dozen names. That rarity is the interesting part. Lucky losers are not a hidden advantage; they are a mechanism for keeping the draw full.

## Why we made qualifying playable

The straightforward way to build qualifying into a career game is to resolve it off-screen. You are ranked, the game tells you whether you are in the main draw, and the career continues.

We did not do that in [Tennis Career Simulator 2026](/apps/tennis-career-sim/). Qualifying is played. You enter the qualifying rounds and make the same decisions there that you make in a main draw.

The reason is that the early career is almost entirely qualifying, and a career game that skips its own first act has no first act. A junior coming out of the academy system is not choosing between tournaments — they are trying to get into one. Making that a thing you do rather than a thing you are told about is what gives the first two seasons any weight.

**It created a real pacing problem, and it is worth admitting.** Playable qualifying means the earliest part of the career is the part with the most matches per unit of progress, and the highest chance that a week ends with nothing. Early testers found the opening stretch grindy in exactly the way a real qualifying campaign is grindy, which is authentic and not necessarily fun.

What we changed in response was not the structure but the decision density: qualifying rounds surface fewer decision points than main-draw matches, so a qualifying week moves faster in real time even though it contains more tennis. The structure stayed. If your ranking is not good enough, you play your way in.

The wider consequence is that entry mechanics become part of scheduling. Choosing an event you can enter directly, versus a bigger event you would have to qualify for, is one of the recurring decisions of a season — and because points expire, it interacts with everything in [how tennis ranking points work](/blog/how-tennis-ranking-points-work/).

## The same problem in other sports

Tennis is not unusual in having a tier below the main tier trying to get in. Golf's equivalent runs annually rather than weekly and grants a whole season of access at once, which changes the psychology completely — [how golf Q-School works](/blog/how-golf-q-school-works/) sets out that structure and why a single week can define a year.

The tennis version is smaller, more frequent and more forgiving: fail this week and there is another qualifying draw next week. That difference is why tennis careers churn and golf careers pivot.
