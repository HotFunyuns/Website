Round every price **up**, and hold one running total rather than a list. Those two rules are the whole method, and the first one is the opposite of what estimation normally teaches. Neutral rounding minimises your error; biased rounding controls its direction, and under a spending limit the direction is the only thing that matters.

An estimate that comes in above the true total is a bound you can act on. An estimate that might be above or below is a guess, and a guess is exactly what you did not want when you started counting.

## Why neutral rounding fails a budget

Take a basket of seven items: 3.49, 1.99, 6.25, 2.10, 4.75, 0.89 and 12.40. The true total is **31.87**.

**Rounded to the nearest whole number**, those become 3, 2, 6, 2, 5, 1 and 12, which sum to **31**. That is 0.87 *below* the real figure. The estimate is accurate — off by under three percent — and it is also wrong in the one direction that can leave you short at the till.

**Rounded up to the next whole number**, they become 4, 2, 7, 3, 5, 1 and 13, which sum to **35**. That is 3.13 above, a cushion of just under ten percent. The estimate is less accurate and strictly more useful, because it cannot be an underestimate.

That trade is the entire argument. The neutral method wins on average error and loses on the property you actually need. [Estimation techniques that make mental math useful](/blog/estimation-techniques-explained/) develops the neutral discipline — round, then track which way you rounded — and this is the case where you decide the direction in advance and never vary it.

## Cutting the cushion without losing the guarantee

A cushion of nearly ten percent is more than most people want. Round up to the next **half** instead of the next whole and the guarantee survives while the cushion shrinks.

The same basket becomes 3.50, 2.00, 6.50, 2.50, 5.00, 1.00 and 12.50, which sum to **33.00** — still above 31.87, but by 1.13 rather than 3.13.

The pattern generalises. Rounding up to the next whole adds at most 1 per item, so a twenty-item basket can overshoot by twenty. Rounding up to the next half caps the overshoot at 0.50 per item. Choose the granularity by how many items you expect: coarse for a handful, fine for a full trolley.

## Hold one number, not a list

The arithmetic above is easy. Doing it while walking, reading labels and talking to someone is not, and the failure is almost never a calculation error — it is losing track.

The app's techniques library states the fix in one line: never hold a list in your head, hold ONE number, folding each new value into the total and moving on. This is why the running total beats the remembered list. A list grows without limit; a total is always a single number of about the same size, whether you are three items in or thirty.

Two practical additions. Say the total silently after each item, which gives the number a rehearsal it would otherwise not get. And if you do lose it, you have lost one figure rather than seven — check the trolley, re-add the last item or two, and carry on rather than starting again.

The adjacent skill of adding a long chain of figures without a running total, and the memory problem that creates, is a genuinely different task from the arithmetic one. This article is about the total; the chain is a separate discipline.

## Add tax and the cushion once, at the end

The mistake at this stage is compounding two upward roundings without noticing.

Take the rounded-up basket of **33.00**. Add a generous 10% for tax: 3.30, giving **36.30**.

The true figure, at an 8.25% rate on the real basket of 31.87, is 34.50. So the estimate is 1.80 high — the basket rounding contributed about 1.13 of that and the tax rounding the rest. Neither was wrong, but together they produce a cushion of over five percent of the true total, which is enough to talk you out of something you could afford.

Decide once. Either round the prices up and use the real tax rate, or use the true prices and round the rate up — not both, unless you specifically want the wide margin. The decomposition that makes an awkward rate easy to apply exactly is in [estimating sales tax from friendly blocks](/blog/estimating-sales-tax/).

## The change question is counting up, not subtraction

"Do I have enough, and what comes back?" is one calculation, and taking it away is the hard way to do it.

From a total of **34.50** against **50.00**: count up. Fifty cents takes you to 35, then 15 takes you to 50. The answer is **15.50**, reached with two additions and no borrowing at all.

This is the same move that makes three-digit subtraction tractable generally, and it is why the change question is usually easier than the total it depends on.

## Where the arithmetic actually sits in a budget

Mental arithmetic decides purchases. It does not build budgets, and it is worth being clear about the boundary.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's guidance describes four steps: record all of your income, track spending sorted into categories, map when bills fall due against when money arrives, and pull the lot together into a written worksheet. Its household-budget activity frames the exercise around distinguishing needs from wants so that a plan covers both obligations and savings. Every one of those steps wants paper or a spreadsheet, because they involve more numbers than anyone should hold.

Scale reinforces the point. The Bureau of Labor Statistics runs the Consumer Expenditure Surveys, which track household spending and feed the Consumer Price Index market basket; in its 2024 data, housing accounted for 33.4% of annual spending and transportation 17.0%. The largest lines in a household budget are fixed commitments decided months in advance. In-aisle arithmetic operates on what is left, which makes it a small lever — but it is the lever you are holding at the moment of the decision, and the only one available then.
