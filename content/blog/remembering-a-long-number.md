Holding a number in your head from the screen to the keypad is not the same problem as memorising it, and it does not want the same solution. A thirty-second hold is a rehearsal problem: the number has to stay alive in a loop that refreshes itself, and almost every failure comes from that loop being interrupted rather than from the number being too long. The fix is a grouping you can say in one breath, a steady rhythm, and thirty seconds of refusing to do anything else.

## The loop you are actually using

The classical account of verbal short-term memory describes a store that holds items in phonological form and loses them to decay after about two seconds, kept alive by an active articulatory control process that supports rehearsal. Robert Hughes, reviewing and then criticising that model in the *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*, summarises it in exactly those terms before arguing that a perceptual-motor account fits the data better and that no discrete store is needed.

The disagreement about architecture does not change the practical picture, which is what matters here: **a short verbal hold is maintained by something very like saying the material to yourself, and it lasts only as long as the saying continues.** That is why you catch yourself muttering a postcode, and why stopping the muttering ends the memory almost immediately.

It also explains a detail that sounds trivial and is not. The word-length effect — Hughes describes it as "the poorer recall of a list of long compared with short words" — means the time your items take to pronounce is part of the constraint. In English, *seven* is the only two-syllable digit name, and it really does cost more loop time than its neighbours. You are not holding abstract quantities. You are holding sounds.

## Why the chunk size is two or three

Nelson Cowan's review argues that the underlying store holds roughly **3 to 5 meaningful items in young adults**, and that the unit is the chunk rather than the digit. George Miller had made the operative point half a century earlier: "since the memory span is a fixed number of chunks, we can increase the number of bits of information that it contains simply by building larger and larger chunks."

The natural question is why we do not simply build enormous chunks and be done with it. The answer is the loop. A chunk only helps if it is a single unit *in rehearsal*, which in practice means something you can say as one word. "Eighty-three" is a word. "Five-nought-seven-three-nine" is a queue.

So take `5 0 7 3 9 1 6 2 8 4` and hold it as `507 · 391 · 6284` — three units instead of ten items, each of them sayable. Telephone formatting conventions around the world are, in effect, a crowdsourced experiment in this constraint. When you see a number formatted for you, accept the formatting; the work has already been done.

One warning: keep the grouping fixed. Starting in pairs and drifting into triples halfway through creates a boundary error that is very hard to detect from the inside, because the digits all feel present.

## Rhythm is a grouping cue

Say the three groups above with equal spacing and a pause between them, and the pauses do real work. Rhythm marks the boundaries, so a digit that drifts is more likely to drift *within* a group, where it is recoverable, than across one, where it is not.

This is also the reason a number read aloud to you at an unhelpful cadence is harder to hold than the same number read in groups: someone else's rhythm has overwritten the one you would have chosen. If a person reads you a long number in an undifferentiated stream, the highest-value thing you can do is repeat it back in your own grouping before you try to use it.

There is a second, subtler benefit. A rhythm is a structure you can check against. If the final group comes out three digits long when you set it up as four, you know something is missing without having to remember what.

## What actually knocks it out

The intuitive model is that numbers fade with time. The evidence points more strongly at competition.

Berman, Jonides and Lewis, in a study explicitly hunting for decay in verbal short-term memory, report that "both conditions with a filled interval led to worse memory performance, but the difficulty of the intervening task had no effect", and conclude that the mere passage of time does not cause forgetting in short-term memory, with interference doing the work instead. They also make the observation that most self-help advice misses: rehearsal "happens so habitually that it is often not noticed and is difficult to disengage."

Put those together and the practical rule falls out. It is not the thirty seconds that costs you the number. It is the question someone asks at second twelve, the notification you read, the price you glance at. Anything verbal competes; the difficulty of the interruption barely matters. Guarding a short hold means guarding the channel, not racing the clock. ## Practising the hold without pretending it is more

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and several of its memory modes drill exactly this: Number Recall and Sequence Memory show a string, take it away, and ask for it back. The seven difficulty levels change the real mechanics rather than just the label — Beginner presents three or four items with a slow reveal, and the higher levels shorten the reveal window and lengthen the sequence. A Custom Practice mode lets you set the content, the length and the reveal speed yourself, which is the setting to use if you want to isolate one variable.

Two honest limits. First, getting better at these drills means getting better at these drills; the evidence on span training does not support a claim that it enlarges memory in general, and we do not make one. Second, the app is practice and entertainment, not assessment: it records score, accuracy, streak, response time and personal bests, and contains no test of any kind.

Within those limits the drills are genuinely useful for one thing this article cares about — finding out, for you specifically, where the group size stops helping.
