**A goaltender's job is to make difficult saves unnecessary.** The spectacular ones make the highlights, but the position is won and lost on positioning — being in a place where an ordinary reaction is enough.

That framing is worth internalising before anything else, because it explains why goaltending is coached the way it is, why its statistics are so misleading, and why it plays so differently in a career simulation.

## The two controllable variables

Almost everything a goaltender does reduces to two decisions, taken continuously.

**Angle.** Being positioned on the line between the puck and the centre of the net. Get this right and both sides of the net are equally defended; get it wrong and one side is open regardless of how quickly you move.

**Depth.** How far out of the crease you are. Coming further out reduces the amount of net the shooter can see, because you occupy more of their visual field. The cost is that you have further to travel if the puck moves laterally.

That trade is the heart of the position:

| Depth | Reduces | Exposes you to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deep in the crease | Little of the shooter's view | Clean shots from distance |
| Moderate | A useful amount | Balanced |
| Aggressive challenge | Most of the visible net | Cross-ice passes and rebounds |

A goalie who is beaten by a shot from the point was probably too deep. A goalie beaten by a pass across the slot was probably too far out. Both are positioning errors, and both are more fixable than reaction speed.

## Why the statistics mislead

Hockey's standard goaltending measures are save percentage (saves divided by shots faced) and goals-against average (goals conceded per sixty minutes). Both fold the team's performance into a number attributed to one player.

The specific problem with save percentage is that **it treats every shot as equal.** A harmless shot from the boards and a point-blank chance after a defensive breakdown both count as one shot. A goalie playing behind a team that concedes many high-danger chances will post a worse number than an equally good goalie behind a team that concedes many harmless ones.

Goals-against average is worse still, because it does not even control for shot volume.

The practical consequence: **goaltending has the widest gap in hockey between how well someone played and what their numbers say.** Read the numbers alongside the situation. If your team is bleeding chances, your save percentage is not a report card on you.

The same distortion, in a different shape, affects skaters — [plus/minus and hockey statistics explained](/blog/plus-minus-and-hockey-stats/) covers why the most-quoted skater number has the same problem.

## The rules that shape the position

The goaltender is treated differently by the rulebook: there is a protected area around the net, restrictions on where and how the goalie may play the puck, and specific provisions about interference. Those rules are why a goaltender's decision to leave the crease is a genuine risk rather than a free option.

The specifics vary between competitions, which is why the freely readable [USA Hockey officiating rulebook](https://www.usahockeyrulebook.com/) is worth a look if you want the actual text rather than a summary. Different leagues apply different versions, so no single description covers all of hockey.

## What a goalie career actually plays like

In [Hockey Career Sim 2026](/apps/hockey-career-sim/), goaltender is one of four full position paths rather than a variant of a skater's career. Three things differ concretely:

**A different attribute tree.** Goaltending, positioning and awareness carry the position; skating and shooting are close to irrelevant. Development points spent as though you were a skater are wasted.

**A different decision rhythm.** A forward makes many decisions with small individual consequences. A goaltender makes fewer, each of which can decide a game. The pacing is closer to a closer's in baseball than to a winger's on the same ice.

**A different relationship with the team.** Your results depend more heavily on the quality of the defence in front of you than any skater's do on their linemates. A good season on a poor team reads worse than it was, and the game reflects that honestly rather than flattering you.

## Practical habits for playing the position well

1. **Decide depth by the threat, not by habit.** A shot from distance with no support wants you deep enough to react; a two-on-one wants you conservative enough to move.
2. **Treat rebounds as decisions, not accidents.** Where a save goes is partly controllable, and it is the difference between one save and three.
3. **Judge yourself over months.** Goaltending results are noisy over a handful of games. A ten-game stretch tells you almost nothing.
4. **Watch which chances you concede, not how many goals.** If the dangerous chances are declining, you are playing better, whatever the scoreboard says.
