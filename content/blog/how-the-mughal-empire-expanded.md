Mughal expansion was not a sequence of talented emperors making good decisions. It was a machine in which conquest produced revenue, revenue supported a service elite, and that elite's appetite for assignments made further conquest hard to stop. The Cambridge Economic History of India puts it bluntly, describing the empire as a conquest state created by, and largely existing for, acts of conquest, and identifying the ruling class's demand for material resources as the primary condition on which the empire established itself.

That framing matters because it changes the decline question. If growth was structural, decline is unlikely to have a single villain — and the most popular single-villain story, that one emperor's religious policy broke the empire, is exactly the one historians argue about most.

## The growth engine

Columbia University's Asia for Educators timeline dates the empire 1526–1858 and describes it as unifying north India and parts of the south, producing an amalgam of Persian and Indian culture in its courts and territories.

Underneath that summary, expansion worked through several instruments at once. Campaigns absorbed territory. Existing regional rulers were brought in as subordinate allies rather than simply removed, keeping local administration functioning while redirecting its surplus. Marriage alliances tied provincial dynasties to the imperial house. And revenue assessment converted agricultural production into the cash and assignments that paid for the next campaign.

The service elite was organised by rank. Officers held a numerical position that set their status, their pay and the size of the cavalry contingent they were required to maintain, and they were usually supported by assigned rights over revenue from particular lands rather than by a salary. The attraction of that system is obvious: it turned conquest directly into the currency that bought loyalty. Its vulnerability is equally obvious, because it required a steady supply of new assignments.

## Succession as a feature, not a bug

Mughal successions were fought, not inherited by rule, and the standard reading of that was long negative — intrigue and civil war as symptoms of a dysfunctional court.

Munis Faruqui's study of the Mughal princes challenges that directly. It argues that the court intrigues, rebellions and wars of succession that characterised Mughal political life, far from undermining the empire's foundations, helped spread, deepen and mobilise Mughal power through an empire-wide network of friends and allies. A prince building a faction was, in effect, recruiting the provinces into the imperial system.

Whether you accept that argument or not, it is a good example of a historiographical move worth recognising: taking a phenomenon everyone had filed under "weakness" and asking what work it was doing. The same move recurs in the argument over [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/), where the transformation school rejects the premise that the fall needs explaining at all.

## What the buildings record

Monuments are usually offered as evidence of Mughal glory. They are more interesting as evidence of Mughal priorities and Mughal mobility.

UNESCO records that Fatehpur Sikri, built in the second half of the sixteenth century by Akbar, served as the capital of the Mughal empire for only some ten years, and that its uniform complex of monuments includes one of the largest mosques in India. A purpose-built imperial city, abandoned within a decade, tells you something real about how portable Mughal government was.

The Red Fort complex, built as the palace fort of Shahjahanabad, the new capital of the fifth Mughal emperor, Shah Jahan, sits beside the older Salimgarh fort built by Islam Shah Suri in 1546. UNESCO describes its private apartments as pavilions linked by a continuous water channel known as the Nahr-i-Behisht, and notes that while the palace planning follows Islamic prototypes, the individual pavilions reflect a fusion of Persian, Timurid and Hindu traditions. UNESCO separately records the Taj Mahal as built at Agra between 1631 and 1648 by order of Shah Jahan in memory of his favourite wife.

Read as a set, these are not decorations on a political story. They are the political story: a court that moved, spent enormously on display, and absorbed the architectural vocabulary of the places it ruled.

## The decline, handled honestly

This is the part where popular accounts get least careful, so it is worth separating what is asserted from what is supported.

Several processes are documented: prolonged and expensive campaigning in the Deccan; growth in the number of rank-holders relative to the assignments available to support them; the emergence of powerful regional states; and, later, European commercial companies with their own armed forces. Columbia's timeline marks 1757, when victory at Plassey gave the East India Company control of Bengal, as the start of British territorial expansion.

What is contested is weighting, and one claim in particular. Aurangzeb's religious policies are the subject of an argument that is not only academic — it is politically live in India today, and positions on it map onto present-day disputes. The responsible approach is to say who holds which position and on what evidence, rather than to adjudicate in a neutral-sounding voice. Some historians treat his reversal of earlier accommodations as a serious cause of alienation among key constituencies; others argue the policy has been exaggerated, read selectively from hostile sources, or given causal weight that the fiscal and military evidence does not support.

The general lesson is the one in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/): court chronicles, European travellers' reports and later nationalist historiography each have interests, and a claim that survives only in one of them is a claim to handle carefully.
