The screen shows you a set of options for one position. There are no refreshes. Whatever you pick here is what you have, and you will not find out whether it was the right call for another dozen picks, by which point you cannot do anything about it.

This is the moment the football draft genre is built around, and it is a more interesting problem than it looks. You are not being asked which player is best. You are being asked to commit under uncertainty, with a partial view of a pool whose shape you can only guess at.

## Football rosters are a unit problem

Every drafting sport rewards balance. Football rewards it harder, and for a structural reason.

In a sport with a small lineup, a weak player is a weak player — costly, visible, and roughly proportional to their own contribution. Football groups its players into units that operate together, and a weak *group* does not fail proportionally. It fails as a group, and it takes the parts of the team that depend on it down with it. Excellence elsewhere does not compensate; it just gets stranded.

The game's own framing acknowledges this. Salary Cap Draft "rewards spending well and exposes the units you skimped on" — note that it is units, plural, that get exposed, not individual picks. That is the correct diagnosis of what goes wrong in football roster building.

The practical rule that falls out of it: **your team is capped by your worst unit, not averaged across all of them.** When you are deciding between upgrading something already good and repairing something bad, football pushes the answer toward repair more aggressively than the other draft sports do.

## Four rulesets, four different questions

[20-0 Football Draft & GM Mode](/apps/football-draft-gm-mode/) offers several draft rulesets, and they are not difficulty tiers stacked on one another. Each poses a distinct question.

| Ruleset | What it gives you | What it is actually testing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unlimited Draft | Refresh and compare options as long as you like | Whether you know what a good roster looks like when nothing stops you building one |
| Limited Draft | No refreshes at all | Whether you can commit on incomplete information |
| Salary Cap Draft | A budget as well as a board | Whether you can price value, not just rank it |
| Playoff Mode | The whole roster under pressure | Whether your build has a weak point that only shows up when it matters |

The reason to try all four is that they expose different failures. A drafter who is excellent in Unlimited and poor in Limited is not unlucky — they rely on search to substitute for judgement. A drafter who is fine in Limited and falls apart under a cap has never had to think about price. Those are two separate skills and the formats separate them cleanly.

## The cost of a refresh

Refreshing options feels free. It is not, and understanding why sharpens your drafting in both directions.

In an unlimited format, more searching genuinely does produce better picks — but with sharply diminishing returns, because you are sampling from the same pool and the ceiling is fixed. What extended searching mostly buys you is *confidence*, and confidence is not the same thing as roster quality. Plenty of long, satisfying Unlimited drafts produce rosters no better than a decisive one, with the time spent on validating a choice you had already made.

Remove the refresh and the calculation inverts. Now the value of a pick includes what it tells you about the rest of the draft. Every position you fill is a data point about the pool's quality level, and a good Limited drafter is updating a model of the board with every selection rather than evaluating each option in isolation.

Two habits follow from that:

- **Set a reference level early.** Your first few picks establish what "good" looks like in this run. Without one, you will either reject perfectly fine options waiting for something that is not coming, or accept a weak one because you have nothing to compare it against.
- **Take the scarce thing before the abundant thing.** If a position is generally deep, a late pick there costs you little. If it is thin, deferring it is how you end up with the hole that caps the roster.

## Spending well under a cap

A salary cap converts a ranking problem into an allocation problem, and that is a bigger change than it sounds.

Without a budget, the question is "who is the best player who helps me?" With one, it is "who is worth what they cost, given everything else I still have to buy?" Those questions have different answers often enough that cap drafting deserves its own approach.

The trap is the marquee signing. Concentrating budget into one exceptional player is emotionally satisfying and structurally dangerous, because in a sport built on units the money you did not spend shows up as an entire group that underperforms. Spending is not neutral — every unit you underfund is a fixed liability the rest of the team has to carry all season.

A defensible default is to buy competence everywhere first, then spend whatever is left on excellence. It rarely produces the most exciting roster on paper and it produces far fewer catastrophes.

## What the season simulator is doing

When you finalise a roster and the simulator returns a record, the record is a model's output. It reflects roster quality and how well the pieces fit together, evaluated by the game's own internal rules.

Three things are worth saying explicitly, because the genre attracts confusion on all three.

**It is not a prediction.** Nothing about the result describes, forecasts or relates to any real football game, team, player or season. The players in the pool are fictional and generated by the game.

**It is a score, and should be read like one.** Treat the record as feedback about your drafting, in the same way a puzzle game returns a score. The useful question is never "did I get lucky" but "which of my decisions is this record disagreeing with".

**The perfect record is meant to be hard.** A run of 20 games without a loss is the target the whole game is built around, not the expected outcome of a competent draft. If you finish short, that is the game working, and the gap between your record and perfection is precisely the information you drafted for.

None of this is betting guidance and none of it is a forecast. It is a scoring model for a roster puzzle, which is a more honest and more interesting thing to be.

## Past draft day

Franchise Mode and GM Mode extend control beyond the draft to the wider organisation, and they change what "a good roster" means.

A single draft is optimised for one season. A franchise is optimised for a sequence of them, and the two builds diverge quickly. The player who maximises this year's record and the player who leaves you flexible next year are frequently not the same person, and the discipline of franchise play is deciding which one you are building for *before* the board tempts you.

This is also where a draft-day mistake stops being fatal. Long play gives you the tools to convert a surplus at one position into coverage at another, which is the only real cure for an imbalanced draft. ## Gridiron IQ, and why a knowledge test belongs in a draft game

Sitting alongside the drafting are VS Computer, Battle, Tournament, a Daily Challenge, and Gridiron IQ, which tests how well you actually read a team sheet.

That last one is a better fit than it first appears. The core skill in this genre is not knowing football trivia — it is *reading a roster and seeing what it will do*. Being able to look at a filled-out team sheet and spot the group that will let it down is the same skill the draft board requires, isolated and tested directly. If your drafts keep coming back with records you did not expect, that is often where the gap is.

## A pre-draft checklist

1. **Decide the format's question before you start.** Unlimited asks what you know; Limited asks whether you can commit; Salary Cap asks whether you can price.
2. **Find your worst unit at every point in the draft.** That group, not your best one, is what the record will reflect.
3. **Set a reference level in the first few picks** so later options have something to be judged against.
4. **Buy competence before excellence** when a budget is in play.
5. **After the record comes back, name the one pick you would change.** Doing this after every run is the difference between playing a lot and getting better.
