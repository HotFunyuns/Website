Pick the method by how long the number has to survive. A verification code you will type in ninety seconds needs grouping and nothing else. An account number you want available for years needs an encoding system and a place to put it. Most advice about memorising numbers fails because it offers one technique for both jobs, so this article sets four methods beside each other and is explicit about what each one costs.

## Method one: group the digits

This is the default, and for short-lived numbers it is usually the whole answer. Break `8 3 0 5 1 7 4 2` into `83 05 17 42` and you have converted eight items into four.

The reason it works is the oldest result in this field. George Miller's 1956 paper observed that "the span of immediate memory seems to be almost independent of the number of bits per chunk", and drew the practical conclusion: "since the memory span is a fixed number of chunks, we can increase the number of bits of information that it contains simply by building larger and larger chunks." Nelson Cowan's later work puts the underlying store at roughly **3 to 5 meaningful items in young adults**, counted in chunks rather than digits, which is why the size of the chunk matters so much more than the number of digits.

Two practical points. Groups of two and three work better than groups of four or five for most people, because a two-digit group is a single spoken word. And keep the grouping consistent within one number — switching from pairs to triples mid-string creates its own error.

**Cost:** nothing. **Durability:** minutes at best. **Fails when:** you are interrupted, or the number is long enough that four chunks becomes eight.

## Method two: attach meaning

The second method is the cheapest large gain available, and it is badly underused because it feels like cheating.

An arbitrary string stops being arbitrary the moment part of it maps onto something you already know. Consider `1 4 9 1 6 2 5 3 6`. Nine digits, well past anyone's span. Read as `1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36`, it is the first six square numbers, and the whole thing collapses to a single rule you could reconstruct a year from now.

Real numbers rarely cooperate that neatly, but partial cooperation is common: a year you recognise, a repeated pair, a doubling, a familiar area code. You are not looking for elegance. You are looking for any structure that lets one unit stand in for several digits.

**Cost:** a few seconds of searching, and sometimes it finds nothing. **Durability:** high when it works. **Fails when:** the number is genuinely random, which is often.

## Method three: turn digits into sounds

The phonetic approach — commonly called the major system — assigns each digit one or more consonant sounds and treats vowels as free. In one widely used assignment, 3 is an *m*, 1 is a *t* or *d*, 4 is an *r* and 2 is an *n*. That makes `3142` into the consonant skeleton m-t-r-n, and inserting vowels gives you the word **matron**. If you recognise 3.142 as a rounded value of pi, you now have two independent hooks on the same four digits.

The appeal is obvious once you have seen it: words are vastly easier to hold and to recall in order than digit strings, and the mapping is reversible, so nothing is lost. Researchers have taken the system seriously enough to automate it. Fiorentini, Shao and Medero's work on generating mnemonic encodings of numbers built and compared encoding models, and evaluated them in a password memorability study, finding that combining part-of-speech sentence templates with a language model produced the most memorable representations.

The catch is the installation. Until the digit-to-sound mapping is automatic, converting a number takes longer than simply rehearsing it, so the system costs you more than it saves for weeks. [Our article on mnemonic systems built for digits](/blog/mnemonic-systems-for-numbers/) covers the major system and its rivals in detail, including what the set-up actually involves.

**Cost:** high up front, near zero afterwards. **Durability:** very high. **Fails when:** you have not finished learning it.

## Method four: put the number somewhere

The fourth method pairs an encoding with a location. You convert chunks into images, then place those images along a route you know well — rooms in your home, landmarks on a walk — and recall by walking the route again.

This is the technique associated with competitive memorisers, and the research describes it in those terms: a neuroimaging study of world-ranked memory athletes characterises users of the strategy as mentally navigating a familiar route and associating material with distinct landmarks along it. It is also trainable by ordinary people. Sandberg and colleagues tested a smartphone-delivered version in which participants "visualized 'things' and imagined placing them at specific places (loci) along familiar paths", with instruction videos using the body, a house and a car as memory palaces. Participants were asked to practise for three months, preferably daily — a detail worth holding on to, because it tells you the scale of the commitment.

**Cost:** highest of the four; you need a route *and* an encoding. **Durability:** highest. **Fails when:** you try to use it for a number you need in the next thirty seconds.

## Choosing between them

The table below matches the four methods to the job, so you can stop reaching for the wrong one.

| Method | Set-up cost | Best for | Typical failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grouping | None | Anything under a minute | Interruption; very long strings |
| Meaning | Seconds | Numbers with any internal structure | Genuinely random digits |
| Phonetic encoding | Weeks | Numbers you will reuse for years | Half-learned mapping slows you down |
| Spatial placement | Months | Long strings, ordered recall | Overkill for everyday numbers |

The honest summary is that most people need the first two and will never need the last two. Competitive memorisation is a sport, and treating it as general life advice is how people end up abandoning memory technique altogether.
