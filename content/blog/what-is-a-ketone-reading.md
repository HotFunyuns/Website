This article will not tell you what your ketone reading means. That is deliberate, and it is the most useful thing about it.

What it will do is explain what ketones are, what each type of test physically measures, and why the published sources that describe those tests are careful not to hand out interpretation. Once you understand why they decline, the number in front of you becomes easier to use correctly — as a record, not a verdict.

> This is general information, not medical advice, and it is not written or reviewed by a clinician. Nothing here interprets a reading, diagnoses a condition, or recommends a course of action. If you have symptoms of ketoacidosis, MedlinePlus advises seeking immediate medical help. If you are considering a low-carbohydrate diet, talk to a doctor or registered dietitian first — especially if you have diabetes or take prescribed medication.

## What ketones are

MedlinePlus puts it plainly: ketones are acids your body makes when it breaks down fat for energy.

Normally the cells in your body run on blood glucose, most of which comes from carbohydrate in food. When cells cannot get enough glucose, the body breaks down fat instead, and that process produces ketones. If fat is broken down too fast, ketones can build up in blood and urine, and high ketone levels make blood too acidic — a serious condition called **ketoacidosis**.

Two details in that description do real work later, so it is worth pausing on them.

**The body makes three types of ketone.** MedlinePlus lists beta-hydroxybutyric acid and acetoacetate among the other names for what these tests measure. Different test methods target different molecules, which is why the results are not interchangeable.

**Producing ketones and having ketoacidosis are different states.** Ketones appear whenever fat is being broken down for fuel. Ketoacidosis is what happens when they accumulate to the point of acidifying blood. Confusing the two in either direction is the origin of most bad information on this topic.

## Ketosis and ketoacidosis are not the same thing

This distinction matters enough that it deserves the source's own framing rather than a paraphrase.

MedlinePlus states that people who eat a low-carbohydrate ketogenic diet will have blood ketone levels that are higher but are not high enough to be ketoacidosis, and calls this condition ketosis. It explains that ketosis does not make blood too acidic because the body is able to use the ketones, so they do not build up.

And then it adds the sentence that most low-carb writing leaves out: **it is possible to develop ketoacidosis on a keto diet**, which is why it says to talk with your provider about whether such a diet is safe for you.

**Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)** is the most common form. MedlinePlus describes it as a medical emergency that often develops quickly and can lead to coma and be life-threatening. It affects people with diabetes, most commonly type 1, and is less common with type 2. Ketoacidosis can also arise without diabetes — MedlinePlus lists eating disorders and starvation, long-lasting vomiting or diarrhoea, and alcohol use disorder among the conditions that put a person at risk.

Symptoms of ketoacidosis listed by MedlinePlus include extreme hunger or thirst, urinating much more than usual, dehydration, headache, nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, trouble breathing, fatigue, dry skin and mouth, breath with a fruity smell, and muscle stiffness or aches. Untreated, symptoms can become severe quickly, and the guidance is unambiguous: seek immediate medical help.

This article does not, and cannot, help you tell which situation you are in. That is the entire reason the previous paragraph ends the way it does.

## The three ways people measure ketones at home

Each method measures something different, which is the technical reason readings disagree.

**Blood.** A finger-prick sample on a test strip read by a meter. MedlinePlus notes that at-home meters are available that check both glucose and ketones, and that a health care provider can advise which testing approach is right for a given person. Blood meters are regulated as in vitro diagnostic devices, and FDA maintains public material on blood glucose monitoring devices; the instructions that come with a device are part of how it is meant to be used, not an optional extra.

**Urine.** Dip strips, available without a prescription in most pharmacies. MedlinePlus lists the "nitroprusside test" among the other names associated with ketone testing, and reports that results may come back as a specific number or as a **small**, **moderate** or **large** amount. Urine reflects what has been excreted over a period rather than what is circulating right now, so hydration and timing both move the result.

**Breath.** Handheld devices used by some people who track low-carb eating. This is the method with the least public federal consumer guidance behind it, which is itself worth knowing when you compare a breath figure with a blood one.

Because these three approaches target different molecules through different chemistry, a disagreement between them is a normal property of the methods, not a malfunction in any of them.

## What the published sources deliberately will not tell you

Here is the thing you will notice if you go and read the primary sources for yourself: **they do not publish a target range for a person eating low-carb.**

MedlinePlus's material on urine ketone testing says normal results can vary depending on your diet, activity level and other factors, and that because high ketone levels can cause serious health problems, you should talk with your provider about what is normal for you and what your results mean.

That is not an oversight or vagueness. It reflects three things at once: the reading depends on method and timing, the meaningful interpretation depends on your health status and medication, and the same number can be unremarkable in one person and important in another.

You will find "optimal ketosis" ranges quoted confidently across the low-carb internet. Those figures do not come from a US federal health authority, and this article is not going to repeat them as though they did. If you want to know what a specific reading means for you, the person to ask is the one who can see the rest of your chart.

### A normal reading does not clear you

The most important single fact in the MedlinePlus material is easy to skim past. A normal or negative test result usually means your body is not making too many ketones — **but a normal test result does not always rule out DKA**, because the body makes three types of ketone, and in the early stages of DKA only one type may be high, and lab tests often do not measure that type.

That is the sentence that ends any argument about using a home ketone reading as a safety check. It cannot do that job, and the source says so.

## Why your numbers move around

If you do log readings, expect variability, and expect most of it to be uninteresting.

- **Method.** Blood, urine and breath measure different molecules. Comparing across methods is comparing different quantities.
- **Timing.** A morning reading and an evening reading describe different points in a day, and urine in particular lags.
- **Hydration.** Concentration affects a urine strip in ways that have nothing to do with metabolism.
- **Strips and storage.** Test strips have expiry dates and storage conditions, and the manufacturer's instructions exist for a reason.
- **What you did that day.** MedlinePlus explicitly notes that normal results vary with diet and activity.

The correct response to a surprising single reading is generally to note it and to look at the pattern, not to change your eating in response to one data point.

## So what is logging them actually for?

There is a genuine use, and it is narrower than the way ketone tracking is usually sold.

A dated series of readings, recorded consistently and alongside what you ate and when, is **a record you can hand to a clinician**. That is a far more useful thing to bring to an appointment than a recollection. It also lets you notice your own patterns without pretending the numbers grade you.

What it is not: a scoreboard, a measure of effort, evidence that a diet is "working", or a substitute for medical assessment. A higher number is not a better number. There is no achievement encoded in it.

## The short version

Ketones are acids produced when the body breaks down fat for energy, and there are three of them. Blood, urine and breath tests measure different ones, which is why they disagree.

Nutritional ketosis and ketoacidosis are different states, and diabetic ketoacidosis is a fast-moving medical emergency rather than a stronger version of a diet result. A normal home reading cannot rule it out, because in early DKA the raised ketone type is often the one not being measured.

No US federal health authority publishes a target ketone level for someone eating low-carb, and the reason is that the meaningful interpretation depends on the person rather than the number. Log the readings if they are useful to you. Take the interpretation to someone qualified to give it.
