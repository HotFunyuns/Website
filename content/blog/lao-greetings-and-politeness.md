ສະບາຍດີ — *sabaidee* — is hello, and it is the only greeting word you strictly need in Laos. It works at any hour, in any company, to anyone.

What changes with company is not the word. It is what you put on the end of the sentence and which pronoun you use to get there, and that is the part most phrasebooks skip.

## Sabaidee, and what it is made of

The two parts of *sabaidee* mean roughly *well* and *good*. It is closer to "well and good" than to "hi", which is why it does not feel casual and does not feel stiff — it is simply the thing people say.

Time-specific greetings are built by addition rather than substitution: *sabaidee* plus a word for the part of the day. That is a small structural point with a useful consequence. You are not memorising four unrelated greetings, you are memorising one greeting and three period words.

Departures are more varied than arrivals:

- **ລາກ່ອນ** (*la kon*) — goodbye, with the sense of "stay well", said by the person leaving.
- **ໄປດີ** (*pai di*) — "go well", said to the person leaving.
- **ໂຊກດີ** (*sok dee*) — good luck, and a common informal parting.
- **ຝັນດີ** (*fun dee*) — good night, literally "dream well".

## Politeness is a particle, not a tone of voice

Lao culture is stratified by age, occupation, wealth and standing, and the language reflects it directly. The amount of respect you encode is a judgement about the specific person in front of you, and it is expressed in two places.

**Pronouns.** Lao drops pronouns freely in casual speech. Putting them back in — and using the more formal forms — is itself a politeness move. Notably, Lao uses pronouns more frequently than Thai does.

**Sentence-final particles.** This is the mechanism to learn first:

- **ແດ່** (*dae*) ends most general statements and softens imperatives and requests. It is the workhorse. It can also intensify an adjective or adverb more politely, or make a demonstrative less blunt.
- **ເດີ / ເດີ້ / ເດ** (*deu / deu / de*) look like variants of the same thing and are not equivalent in effect. They are more intensive, giving requests and demands more urgency, and they are therefore **less** polite than ແດ່, not more.
- **ດອກ** (*dok*) is the one for negatives: a negative statement is made more polite by ending with it.

That ແດ່/ເດີ contrast is the trap. Two particles that look like near-neighbours on a chart sit on opposite sides of the politeness line, and a learner who treats them as interchangeable will be emphatic exactly when they meant to be deferential.

## Saying yes, and showing you are listening

Lao does not have a single word for *yes*. To agree, or simply to signal that you are still listening, speakers use **ໂດຍ** (*doi*), especially in formal situations, or **ເຈົ້າ** (*chao*). Another common strategy is to answer a question by repeating its verb.

Saying no is much simpler: **ບໍ່** (*baw*) placed in front of the verb, adjective, adverb or noun being negated. The same word does double duty as the yes-no question marker at the end of a sentence, which is why ສະບາຍດີບໍ່ is "are you well?"

## Not Thai: the difference that matters here

Thai attaches the speaker's gender to its polite particles — one form used by men, another by women — and every Thai course has to drill it early because getting it wrong is immediately audible.

**Lao does not do this.** Politeness runs through ແດ່, ດອກ and pronoun choice, and none of those is selected by who is speaking.

This matters because Lao and Thai are closely related, mutually intelligible to a large degree, and constantly treated as versions of one another. A learner who imports the Thai politeness system will look for a gendered particle, fail to find one, and either invent a rule or conclude that Lao is less polite. Neither is true. The system is real and it is built differently. [Lao and Thai compared](/blog/lao-vs-thai-differences/) covers where else the two part company.

## The nop

The gesture is palms pressed together, a slight bow. In Laos it is usually called the *nop*, and it belongs to the family that includes the Thai *wai* and the Cambodian *sampeah*, all descended from the Indian *Añjali Mudrā* and carried into Southeast Asia with Hinduism and Buddhism.

In the Thai version of the gesture, the higher the hands are held relative to the face and the deeper the bow, the more respect is being shown; Lao usage works on the same principle. The practical guidance for a visitor is the same as it is in Thailand: return a nop that is offered to you, and do not initiate one to someone much younger.

## Why the course teaches the particle before the phrase

The first unit of [Learn Lao Language Fast & Easy](/apps/learn-lao/) introduces the politeness particle *before* the full greeting exchange, which is the opposite of the usual order. Most courses give you "hello", "how are you" and "I'm fine, thanks" and then, several units later, mention that there are particles you can add.

We flipped it, and the reason came out of how the material was being used.

A traveller working through a Lao course is generally doing so shortly before or during a trip, and they are not going to reach unit nine. Whatever is in the first unit is, for a meaningful share of learners, the entire course. Putting the politeness mechanism in unit nine means shipping a large number of people to Laos with fluent, warm, slightly blunt Lao.

There is a second reason, which is about what a phrase teaches. A greeting learned as a fixed block is a block: you can say it and you cannot modify it. A greeting learned as *a sentence plus a particle* is a pattern, and the particle generalises to every sentence you learn afterwards. Front-loading it means the fifteenth phrase costs less than it otherwise would.

The cost is that the first lesson is denser than a first lesson ought to be. A learner who wants "how do I say hello" and nothing else gets a short grammatical concept attached to it, and some people will skip the explanation and keep the phrase. That is a fair outcome — they still leave with *sabaidee*.

On the audio: greeting lines are recorded by both a male and a female speaker. This is not because the words differ by gender, because in Lao they do not. It is because a learner needs to recognise the phrase in more than one voice, and a course recorded entirely by one speaker teaches recognition of that speaker as much as of the language. We would have wanted two voices regardless of what the grammar did.
