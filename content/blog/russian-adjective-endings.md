Russian adjective endings look like a wall of forms, but they come from one table with a few predictable spelling adjustments. Wikipedia's article on Russian declension says a Russian adjective is usually placed before its noun and **agrees with it in case, gender and number**, and that most adjectives follow one of a small number of regular patterns. Learn the main pattern with *но́вый*, "new", then the adjustments for soft stems and for certain consonants, and you can decline almost any adjective you meet.

## The hard-stem table, with новый

This is the pattern Wikipedia gives as the base for most adjectives, here with *новый*.

| Case | Masculine | Neuter | Feminine | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | но́вый | но́вое | но́вая | но́вые |
| Accusative | = N or G | но́вое | но́вую | = N or G |
| Genitive | но́вого | но́вого | но́вой | но́вых |
| Dative | но́вому | но́вому | но́вой | но́вым |
| Instrumental | но́вым | но́вым | но́вой | но́выми |
| Prepositional | но́вом | но́вом | но́вой | но́вых |

Three features make the table smaller than it looks.

- **Masculine and neuter share everything except the nominative and accusative.** From the genitive down they are identical.
- **The feminine has *-ой* in four cases**: genitive, dative, instrumental and prepositional. Wikipedia adds that the instrumental also has a stylistic variant, *-ою*.
- **The accusative borrows.** For masculine nouns and all plurals, the accusative copies the nominative for inanimate nouns and the genitive for animate ones, as nouns do. "I see a new house" uses *но́вый*; "I see a new friend" uses *но́вого*.

The animacy rule comes from the noun system, covered in [Russian cases explained](/blog/russian-cases-explained-for-beginners/).

## Counting the endings

How much is there to learn? Take the 24 cells of the table and list the written endings without repeats: *-ый, -ое, -ая, -ые, -ую, -ого, -ой, -ых, -ом, -ому, -ым, -ыми*. That gives **12 distinct endings**, half the number of cells, because the accusative borrows and so many cells repeat. A quick count confirms it: 24 cells, 12 unique strings.

Two of those endings sound different from how they look. Wikipedia notes that **-ого and -его are read as -ово and -ево**, so *нового* is pronounced with a v sound, though it is always written with г.

## Soft stems: синий

Some adjectives have a soft stem, and the ending vowels shift to their soft partners. Wikipedia's example is *си́ний*, "blue".

| Case | Masculine | Neuter | Feminine | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | си́ний | си́нее | си́няя | си́ние |
| Accusative | = N or G | си́нее | си́нюю | = N or G |
| Genitive | си́него | си́него | си́ней | си́них |
| Dative | си́нему | си́нему | си́ней | си́ним |
| Instrumental | си́ним | си́ним | си́ней | си́ними |
| Prepositional | си́нем | си́нем | си́ней | си́них |

The pattern is the same; only the vowels change. Where *новый* has ы, *синий* has и; *о* becomes *е*; *а* becomes *я*; *у* becomes *ю*.

## The spelling rules: высокий and хороший

Two rules adjust the hard pattern after particular consonants.

**After a velar or sibilant, write и instead of ы.** Wikipedia gives this rule for adjectives with stems ending in letters such as к, г, х, ж, ш. So *высо́кий*, "tall", has *высо́кий, высо́кие, высо́ким* where *новый* has ы, but it keeps the hard *-ое, -ая, -ого, -ому*.

**After a sibilant, neuter adjectives end in -ее.** Wikipedia calls this the *хоро́шее* rule, after *хоро́ший*, "good". Its table shows *хорошее, хорошего, хорошему, хорошем* and feminine *хорошей* in the oblique cases.

**Stressed -ой.** Some masculine adjectives end in *-ой* instead of *-ый*, and Wikipedia notes that this *-ой* is always stressed: *большо́й*, "big". The rest of *большой* follows the hard pattern with и after ш: *больша́я, большо́е, больши́е*.

## Working through four phrases

Take "new" with four nouns, applying the table.

1. *дом* (house, masculine, inanimate), nominative → **но́вый дом**.
2. *окно́* (window, neuter), genitive, "of the new window" → **но́вого окна́**.
3. *кни́га* (book, feminine), accusative, "I'm reading a new book" → **но́вую кни́гу**.
4. *дома́* (houses, plural), prepositional, "in the new houses" → **в но́вых дома́х**.

Now swap in "good": the same four become *хоро́ший дом*, *хоро́шего окна́*, *хоро́шую кни́гу* and *в хоро́ших дома́х*. Only the cells covered by the spelling rules changed.

## Short forms

Adjectives can also have a **short form**. Wikipedia's Russian grammar article says that in modern Russian the short form appears **only in the nominative** and is used when the adjective is **predicative**, that is, after the noun as its description: "the house is new". For *новый* the short forms are *нов, нова́, но́во, новы́*. Wikipedia adds that relational adjectives, which describe a relationship rather than a quality, are unlikely to have a short form.

Where the adjective sits in the sentence is a separate question; see [Russian word order and emphasis](/blog/russian-word-order-and-emphasis/).
