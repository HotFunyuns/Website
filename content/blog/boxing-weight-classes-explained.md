Professional boxing has 17 weight classes that all four major sanctioning bodies — the WBA, WBC, IBF and WBO — recognise, starting at 105 pounds (47.627 kg) and ending with a heavyweight class that has no upper limit. The WBC adds an eighteenth, bridgerweight, for boxers above 200 and up to 224 pounds. Below 200 pounds the bodies agree on every limit; what they disagree on is the names, and above 200 pounds, whether bridgerweight exists.

That is the short answer, and it is accurate as of each body's current published documents on 2 October 2026. The longer answer is that "the" limit depends on who you ask. Sanctioning bodies set divisions for their titles, state commissions set them in regulation, and at least one regulator prints different numbers.

## Every division, lightest to heaviest

Limits are upper limits: a boxer must weigh no more than the figure to compete in that division. Kilograms are converted at the exact factor and rounded to three decimal places; they match the WBC's published figures to within 0.001 kg.

## Where the limits stop agreeing

Up to 200 pounds the four bodies are interchangeable on numbers, so a "junior welterweight" and a "super lightweight" are the same 140-pound division under different labels. The differences cluster at the top.

**The WBC's bridgerweight.** The WBC's weight scale includes a division up to 224 pounds between cruiserweight and heavyweight, and its heavyweight class begins above 224. The WBA's current ratings, the IBF's rules and the WBO's regulations list no such class and put heavyweight above 200 pounds.

**Nevada's schedule.** Nevada's administrative code (NAC 467.476, last amended in 2007) lists seventeen classes from strawweight, up to 105 pounds, to heavyweight — but its cruiserweight runs from over 175 to 195 pounds, and its heavyweight starts above 195. That is not the 200-pound cruiserweight limit any of the four sanctioning bodies uses today.

**Worked example.** Take a boxer who weighs in at 210 pounds. To the WBA, IBF and WBO that boxer is a heavyweight. To the WBC, a bridgerweight. Under Nevada's printed schedule, a heavyweight again — but so would a boxer of 196 pounds be, who is a cruiserweight to every sanctioning body. The scale reading is the same; the classification depends on whose table is in force.

## Why boxing has so many divisions

Weight classes exist because mass is an advantage skill does not reliably cancel; the reasoning is set out in [our MMA weight classes guide](/blog/mma-weight-classes-explained/), and it applies equally to boxing. Boxing's version is unusually fine-grained, and history explains much of that.

In the early nineteenth century there were no standard classes at all. Recognised sets came later — notably those specified in 1909 by the National Sporting Club of London and in New York's 1920 Walker Law. The narrower modern bands followed the WBA–WBC split of the 1960s: more divisions meant more champions at once, and smaller steps made it easier for a boxer to move up or down.

The arithmetic of the table shows how narrow they are. From 105 to 175 pounds, each band is between 3 and 8 pounds wide — under 5% of the division's limit in every case, from 2.5% at bantamweight (115 to 118) to 4.8% at welterweight and super middleweight. Then the pattern breaks: cruiserweight spans 25 pounds (175 to 200), or 12.5% of its limit, and bridgerweight spans 24 pounds. In proportional terms, a cruiserweight band is two and a half to five times wider than the bands below it.

Nevada's code shows the same logic from a different angle. It sets the largest weight difference a commission will allow between opponents without approval: 3 pounds up to 118, rising through 5, 7, 9, 11 and 12 pounds, to 20 pounds between 175 and 195, and no limit at all for heavyweights.

## Making weight: when the scale is read

A limit means little without a schedule, and the sanctioning bodies' schedules differ.

- **The WBA** holds the official weigh-in between 16 and 36 hours before the bout and gives a boxer who misses two more hours to make weight.
- **The WBC** holds its pre-bout weigh-in 24 to 30 hours before the event, plus a day-of-bout weigh-in under its weight-management programme. Its safety scale also sets check weights in the weeks before a title fight: no more than 10% over the limit 30 days out, 5% at 14 days and 3% at 7 days. For a 147-pound welterweight bout, that means 162, 154 and 151 pounds.
- **The IBF** holds the initial weigh-in 20 to 30 hours before the first bout, then a second weigh-in between 8 and 10 a.m. on fight day. That second limit is roughly 10 to 14 pounds above the division limit — 158 for welterweight, 214 for cruiserweight — and the rule is waived for heavyweight championships and unification bouts.

Missing weight has title consequences that the rulebooks spell out. Under the IBF's and WBA's rules, a champion who misses after the extra time loses the title on the scale; the fight can still go ahead, and the title passes to the challenger only if the challenger made weight and wins. A challenger who misses cannot win the title, and the champion keeps it even in defeat.

## Same name, different weight: boxing versus MMA

The ABC's Unified Rules of MMA reuse many boxing division names with different numbers. Reading one sport's name with the other's limits is the most common weight-class mistake.

| Division name | Boxing upper limit | MMA range (ABC) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flyweight | 112 lb | over 115 to 125 lb |
| Bantamweight | 118 lb | over 125 to 135 lb |
| Featherweight | 126 lb | over 135 to 145 lb |
| Lightweight | 135 lb | over 145 to 155 lb |
| Welterweight | 147 lb | over 165 to 170 lb |
| Middleweight | 160 lb | over 175 to 185 lb |
| Light heavyweight | 175 lb | over 195 to 205 lb |
| Heavyweight | no limit (over 200, or over 224 for the WBC) | over 225 to 265 lb, with super heavyweight above |

An MMA lightweight can weigh 155 pounds; a boxing lightweight cannot exceed 135.

## Converting pounds to kilograms

One pound is defined as exactly 0.45359237 kilograms. Multiply the pound limit by that factor: 147 × 0.45359237 = 66.678 kg, matching the WBC's welterweight figure. Published kilogram figures can differ in the last decimal place because of rounding — 200 pounds is 90.718474 kg, which the WBC and IBF print as 90.719 and the WBO as 90.72.
