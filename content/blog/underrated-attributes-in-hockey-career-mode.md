Most people spend their first fifty training points on shooting, notice their player is still not dominating, and spend the next fifty on shooting. Then they fade out of third periods and conclude the simulator is unfair.

[Hockey Career Sim 2026](/apps/hockey-career-sim/) — our own app, built by Reign Creative — lists nine attributes that develop through focused training: skating, shooting, passing, puck control, checking, positioning, stamina, awareness and goaltending. Its own guidance says the simulator weights what you attempt against what you have built. That sentence is the whole argument for spending carefully, because it means an unspent attribute is not neutral. It is a ceiling.

## The nine attributes, and the two that absorb everything

Shooting and skating are the attributes with the most obvious story. You can picture what a higher number does. That legibility is precisely why they get over-funded: the payoff is easy to imagine, so it feels like the safe purchase.

The other seven do less imaginable things. Positioning decides whether you are somewhere useful when the puck arrives. Awareness decides whether you notice the option that was available. Puck control decides whether you still have the puck when you get to the place you were skating towards. Checking decides whether a defensive challenge works. Stamina decides how much of the third period you are still genuinely in. Passing decides whether your best idea survives contact with a teammate. Goaltending decides everything, but only if you are a goaltender.

None of those produce a highlight on their own. All of them decide whether the highlight attributes ever get a chance to fire.

## Stamina is a third-period attribute, and the third period is real

The strongest external evidence for taking stamina seriously comes from tracking what actually happens to hockey players as a game goes on. A 2018 analysis in *Biology of Sport* compared locomotor activity across the three periods of an international game and found the decline steep rather than gentle: sprint counts in period three were down by roughly 47 per cent against period one, effective time in high-intensity activity down by roughly 55 per cent, and total distance covered down by about 13 per cent across the game. Bench resting time went up. The authors concluded that the capacity to perform intense actions is impeded towards the end of the match.

That is the physiological reality a career sim's energy model is abstracting. You do not need to know the exact curve the game uses to know what an under-trained stamina attribute will feel like: a first period where your choices work, a second where they mostly work, and a third where the aggressive option keeps failing and you cannot tell why.

The useful reframing is that stamina is not a performance attribute. It is an *availability* attribute. It decides how many minutes of the game your other eight numbers are operating at full value.

## Positioning and awareness create what shooting converts

A shot attempt is the last event in a chain. Before it there is a read, a route, and an arrival. Positioning and awareness are the attributes that own the first three-quarters of that chain, and they are the two most commonly starved.

The symptom of a low-positioning build is distinctive and easy to misdiagnose. Your chances are not bad; they are late. You are consistently a beat behind the play, taking the shot that was good two seconds ago. Because the shot itself resolves badly, it looks like a shooting problem, and the natural response is to buy more shooting — which buys you a better version of a shot you should not have been taking.

Awareness has a related signature: you keep choosing the second-best option because the best one was not visible to you. In a game where you are handed a decision at the moment it matters, a low-awareness build is effectively being shown a shorter menu.

## Checking, skating and passing are position-weighted, not universal

Not every attribute repays every build equally, and the evidence that positions face genuinely different demands is not just intuition. A 2022 study in the *International Journal of Exercise Science* measured blood lactate through a game and found forwards markedly higher than defencemen in all three periods — means of 7.4, 7.1 and 6.4 mmol/L against 4.3, 4.3 and 3.1 mmol/L. The authors read this as forwards doing more high-intensity skating. Forwards and defencemen are not doing the same job at different intensities; they are doing different jobs.

That translates cleanly into spending. Checking earns its keep on a defensive build and is close to dead weight on a playmaker. Skating matters everywhere but matters most where repeated bursts are the job. Passing is the shared attribute — it is the only one that makes every other player on your line better — which is why it is the least regrettable purchase for a forward who is unsure.

If you want to understand why none of this shows up neatly in the box score afterwards, the [statistical vocabulary hockey uses to measure two-way play](/blog/plus-minus-and-hockey-stats/) is worth a detour. The short version is that prevention is measured badly, so a well-built defensive player often looks ordinary in print.

## A spend order that survives a season

This is a priority sequence rather than a formula, and it assumes you have already made the [archetype commitment that the rest of the career follows from](/blog/hockey-career-archetypes-explained/).

Start by funding the one or two attributes your archetype genuinely cannot operate without, and fund them until they are competitive rather than merely present. A partial specialist gets picked. A uniform generalist does not.

Next, buy stamina to the point where the third period stops feeling like a different game. This is the purchase people defer indefinitely, and it is the one that quietly changes the most minutes.

Third, buy positioning — or, for a defensive build, whichever of positioning and checking is lagging. This is the attribute that raises the quality of the chances your headline attribute is asked to convert.

Only then broaden. Awareness, puck control and passing are excellent late purchases precisely because their value scales with how good the rest of your build already is.

Goaltending sits outside the sequence entirely. If you are a netminder it is the sequence; if you are not, it is points on fire.

## When you have already spent badly

Everyone does this once. The recovery is not a rebalance.

Keep the over-funded attribute. It is your identity on a roster, and stripping value out of it is not possible anyway. Instead, pick the single attribute that is costing you the most right now — in most over-shooting builds it is positioning, in most veteran builds it is stamina — and put every development cycle into it until the symptom goes away. One repaired weakness alongside one real strength is a player a coach uses. Nine adequate numbers is a player a coach rotates.

It is worth remembering what kind of thing you are optimising. Every league, team and player here is fictional, the game plays offline, and the numbers are model inputs rather than claims about real hockey. But the model rewards the same thing the sport does, which is knowing what your job is before you decide how to get better at it.
