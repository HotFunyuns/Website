Marathi is the language of Maharashtra, the state on India's western coast whose largest cities are Mumbai and Pune. That is the one-line answer. The more useful answer is about standing and about situations: what official recognition Marathi holds, and which specific interactions change when you can speak it — because the honest version includes the fact that plenty of interactions will not.

## The official picture

Three separate facts describe where Marathi sits formally, and they are often blurred together.

**It is a scheduled language.** The Department of Official Language, part of the Ministry of Home Affairs, publishes the list of languages included in the Eighth Schedule to the Constitution of India. There are twenty-two, and Marathi is entry number twelve. Inclusion in the Eighth Schedule is the national-level recognition that carries obligations around development and representation.

**It is the statutory language of a state.** Glottolog, the linguistic catalogue maintained as a scholarly reference, records Marathi with ISO 639-3 code `mar`, classifies it as Indo-European and Indo-Aryan, lists India as the country in which it is spoken, and describes its status as a statutory provincial language in Maharashtra State, referencing the 1950 Constitution and its Eighth Schedule. It records the language as not endangered.

**It is a Classical Language.** On 3 October 2024 the Union Cabinet approved conferring Classical Language status on Marathi together with Pali, Prakrit, Assamese and Bengali. The Ministry of Culture's own explainer, issued the following day, sets out the background: the category was created by the Government of India in 2004, with criteria requiring high antiquity of early texts or recorded history over a thousand years, a body of ancient literature or texts regarded as valuable heritage by generations of speakers, and a literary tradition that is original rather than borrowed from another speech community. Six languages — Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam and Odia — held the status before 2024; the decision took the total to eleven.

None of those three facts is a claim about how many people you will hear speaking Marathi on a given street. They describe the language's institutional position, which is the part a prospective learner can actually verify.

## Why the "but everyone speaks English" objection is half right

Anyone considering Marathi runs into the same discouragement: Mumbai is an international city, Hindi is widely used across northern and western India, English is everywhere in business and higher education, so why bother.

The half of that which is true is structural. India's constitutional arrangements make provision for Hindi and English at the level of the Union. Article 343 establishes Hindi in Devanagari script as the official language of the Union while providing that English would continue to be used for all official purposes for fifteen years from the commencement of the Constitution, and that Parliament may by law provide for its continued use thereafter. Article 120 provides that business in Parliament shall be transacted in Hindi or English, with a provision allowing a member who cannot adequately express themselves in either to use their mother tongue.

So a visitor conducting formal, commercial or administrative business in English is not doing anything unusual, and will frequently succeed.

The half that is wrong is a category error. The question "can I get by without it" is not the question "what am I missing". Those come apart in every multilingual society. The language people use with each other, at home, in a market, when the transaction stops being a transaction, is not the language selected for an outsider — and it does not switch back while you are standing there.

## Where knowing it changes the interaction

Concretely, the situations where Marathi does something English does not:

- **Everyday commerce outside tourist infrastructure.** Prices, quantities, substitutions and the small negotiation around them.
- **Signage and documents.** Devanagari is used for Marathi, so reading is a skill with immediate daily returns — street names, bus destinations, notices, forms.
- **Anywhere hospitality is involved.** Being offered something, refusing politely, accepting gracefully. Register matters and English flattens it.
- **Family contexts.** The most common reason people learn Marathi is not travel; it is relatives.
- **Living in Maharashtra rather than visiting.** Neighbours, building committees, schools, tradespeople and the long tail of practical life.

And the situations where it changes very little: airport and hotel transactions, international business meetings, higher education in English-medium institutions, and short trips confined to those.

That is a real trade-off and it deserves stating plainly rather than being argued away. If your contact with Maharashtra is a week in a business hotel, Marathi is an interest, not a requirement. If it is family, relocation, or any intention to be somewhere as a participant rather than a visitor, the calculation reverses.

## What this means for how you start

Because the practical payoff is skewed toward daily life rather than formal registers, the sensible starting order is different from a classroom syllabus.

The script comes first, and it repays itself fastest. Marathi is written in Devanagari, which is shared with Hindi and Sanskrit — and the shared script is exactly what makes the two languages easy to confuse. [Marathi vs Hindi](/blog/marathi-vs-hindi-differences/) is the piece to read before assuming that knowing one gives you the other; the script overlaps far more than the grammar does.

After that, topic selection matters more than volume. Greetings and introductions, family, food, shopping and prices, transport and directions, numbers and time cover most of what the first six months of real exposure will actually throw at you, and they are the same topics that matter whether you are visiting or settling. [Learn Marathi for beginners](/blog/learn-marathi-beginners-guide/) sets out a workable first-month route through them.
