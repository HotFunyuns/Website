An n-back task asks you one question over and over: does the thing on screen right now match the thing that appeared n steps ago? Items arrive in a continuous stream, you answer on every item, and there is no recall phase at the end. That single design choice is what makes the task feel unlike every other memory game, and it is also why it became the standard laboratory tool for studying working-memory updating.

## The task, step by step

Suppose letters appear one per second and you are running a 2-back. Take this sequence, which is short enough to check by hand:

**K, M, K, P, M, P, P**

You cannot answer on the first two items — there is nothing two steps back yet. From the third item onward, compare each letter with the one two places earlier:

- Item 3 is K; item 1 was K. **Match.**
- Item 4 is P; item 2 was M. No match.
- Item 5 is M; item 3 was K. No match.
- Item 6 is P; item 4 was P. **Match.**
- Item 7 is P; item 5 was M. No match.

Two matches out of five decisions. Now run the same sequence as a 3-back and the answers change completely: item 5 (M) matches item 2 (M), item 7 (P) matches item 4 (P), and the two matches you found before are no longer matches at all. Same letters, different task. That is worth sitting with, because it shows that nothing about the items themselves is being tested. What is being tested is your ability to keep a moving window open at a particular width.

## Why the load stays flat instead of growing

Most memory games get harder by giving you more. A digit-span trial adds a digit. A [Simon-style sequence adds a step](/blog/growing-sequence-memory-games/). The demand accumulates, you hit a wall, and the run ends.

N-back does not work that way. At 2-back you are holding two items at the start of the run and two items at the end of it. The quantity never changes. What changes on every single step is the *contents*: you add the new item, drop the item that just fell out of the window, and keep the order straight — all while making a yes/no decision that itself takes attention.

This is continuous updating, and it is a genuinely different demand from storage. It is also why people describe n-back as exhausting rather than as difficult. There is no moment of relief, no phase where you get to unload, and no natural place to rehearse. [Working memory is a small resource to begin with](/blog/working-memory-and-training/) — Nelson Cowan's review puts the central store at roughly three to five meaningful chunks in young adults — and n-back spends that resource on maintenance and replacement at the same time.

## Single versus dual

The version that made the task famous is dual n-back, and it is worth being precise about what "dual" means. Two independent streams run at once: commonly a square moving around a grid and a letter spoken aloud. Each has its own match decision at the same value of n, and the two streams are uncorrelated, so knowing that the position matched tells you nothing about whether the sound did.

That is not twice the work; in practice it is considerably more, because the two queues compete for the same limited store and both need updating on every step. Most people who can hold a comfortable 3-back on one stream find dual 3-back close to unplayable at first.

## What the training studies actually found

This is the part the marketing usually skips, and the picture is genuinely mixed rather than uniformly negative.

The clearest negative result comes from Thompson and colleagues, writing in *PLoS One* in 2013. Participants completed 20 sessions of adaptive dual n-back — about 40 minutes a day, five days a week, for four weeks — compared against an active control group who trained on a multiple-object-tracking task for the same schedule, plus a passive no-contact group. Both training groups showed substantial and specific improvements on their trained tasks that persisted for at least six months. Transfer to anything else did not appear: no improvement on complex span measures, and none on the fluid-intelligence tasks relative to either control group.

A different framing comes from Gathercole and colleagues in the *Journal of Memory and Language*, who argue that transfer happens only when training causes you to learn a new complex cognitive skill that can then be applied to an untrained activity. On that account transfer is real but narrow: it follows shared structure between tasks, not general strengthening. Where a task can already be handled by routines you have, no new routine gets built, and nothing transfers even between tasks that look similar.

Above both sits the 2014 consensus statement issued jointly by the Stanford Center on Longevity and the Max Planck Institute for Human Development, signed by dozens of cognitive scientists and neuroscientists, which holds that the scientific literature does not support claims that software-based brain games improve general cognitive performance in everyday life or prevent cognitive decline. Our own cornerstone piece on [whether brain training apps work](/blog/do-brain-training-apps-work/) sets out both sides of that argument, including the meta-analysis that reached a more positive conclusion about n-back specifically.

## Playing an n-back round without the marketing

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and it includes **N-Back Lite** as one of the twelve challenge modes in its memory catalogue — a short round of the task alongside things like Flash Recall, Rapid Recognition and Dual Attribute. The name is deliberately modest. A brief round inside a memory game is not the multi-week adaptive protocol the studies above used, and we do not present it as one. It is there because the task is interesting and unusual, not because it does something to you.

If you want to play it well, three things help:

- **Do not try to visualise the whole run.** Hold exactly n items and let everything older go. Trying to keep a longer history is the most common way people fail a run they could have passed.
- **Say the window out loud, or sub-vocally.** Verbalising the two or three current items gives the queue something to hold onto during the gap between stimuli.
- **Expect the errors to cluster after a match.** Registering a match takes a beat, and the next item usually arrives during that beat. Knowing this does not fix it, but it stops you reading the following mistake as a collapse.

## Is it worth doing?

If you find the task interesting, yes — as practice and as entertainment, which are honest categories. You will get better at n-back, probably quite quickly, and that improvement is real.

What you should not do is buy the older promise. Getting better at holding a two-item window while a stream moves is a skill that applies to holding a two-item window while a stream moves. The strongest well-controlled test of the training protocol found exactly that and nothing more, and the broad scientific consensus is against the general-improvement claim. An n-back round is a game mode with an unusual demand. That is enough of a reason to play it, and it is the only reason we offer.
