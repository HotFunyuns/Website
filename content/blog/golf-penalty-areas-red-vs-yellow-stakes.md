A penalty area is any body of water on the course, plus any other part of the course the Committee defines as one, usually marked with red or yellow stakes or lines. If your ball is in one you have a choice: play it as it lies, which costs nothing, or take relief outside the area for one penalty stroke. **Yellow gives you two relief options and red gives you three**, because only red allows lateral relief within two club-lengths of where the ball last crossed the edge. Water that nobody has marked counts as red.

That is Rule 17 of the 2023 Rules of Golf, which remains the current edition as of 2 October 2026; The R&A has said the next edition will be published on 1 January 2028.

| Your situation | Yellow penalty area | Red penalty area |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Play the ball as it lies | Allowed, no penalty | Allowed, no penalty |
| Stroke-and-distance relief | One stroke | One stroke |
| Back-on-the-line relief | One stroke | One stroke |
| Lateral relief within two club-lengths | Not available | One stroke |
| Unplayable, embedded-ball or cart-path relief | Not available | Not available |

## What counts as a penalty area

The definition starts with water. Every body of water on the course is a penalty area whether or not anyone has marked it: seas, lakes, ponds, rivers, ditches, surface drainage ditches and other open watercourses, even when they are dry. On top of that, the Committee may define other ground as a penalty area, and the definition mentions features such as a beach or desert area forming an edge. The Committee may also declare a usually-dry drainage ditch to be general area instead.

How the edge works matters more than most golfers realise:

- **Stakes:** the edge runs between the outside points of the stakes at ground level, and the stakes themselves are inside the area. Penalty-area stakes are obstructions, not boundary objects.
- **Lines:** the edge is the outside edge of the painted line, so the line is part of the area.
- **Up and down:** the edge extends above and below the ground. A ball resting in a branch over the water is in the penalty area.

Your ball is in the area when any part of it touches the ground or anything else inside the edge, or sits above the edge or any part of the area (Rule 17.1a). Touching the line is enough.

## Why a course chooses yellow

The Committee Procedures that accompany the Rules say most penalty areas should be marked red, precisely so players have the extra lateral option. Yellow is for holes where the carry is the point. The example given is a stream crossing the front of a green: if it were red, a ball that cleared the stream and then rolled back in could be dropped on the green side under lateral relief, and the carry would stop mattering. Marked yellow, that player must go back.

The same guidance asks Committees marking an area yellow to make sure back-on-the-line relief is always possible, or to add a dropping zone, and allows them to mark everything red for simplicity. Where an area changes colour, red and yellow stakes should sit next to each other at the transition point. Stakes with green tops mark a no play zone inside a penalty area, and Committees are told to avoid red or yellow when marking boundaries.

So the colour is information about the hole. Yellow at the front of a green tells you the Committee treats the carry as part of the hole's challenge and has chosen not to let a ball that fails it be dropped on the far side.

## In, or only probably in?

If you can see your ball in the area, Rule 17.1a settles it. If you cannot find it, the standard is "known or virtually certain": either conclusive evidence, such as someone watching it splash, or all reasonably available information showing it is at least 95% likely to be in there. Only information found during the three-minute search counts.

Fall short of that and the ball is simply lost, which means stroke and distance under Rule 18 instead. That is why a provisional ball is allowed when a shot might be in a penalty area but might also be lost somewhere else. And once you put another ball in play under Rule 17, the original is out of play even if someone finds it inside the three minutes.

## Playing it as it lies

Rule 17.1b treats a ball in a penalty area exactly like one in the general area: there are no special restrictions on how you play it. You may also stand inside a penalty area to play a ball that lies outside it. The single exception is a no play zone within the area, from which you must take relief.

## The three relief options, step by step

### Stroke and distance: yellow or red

Play again from where you made the previous stroke (Rule 14.6). From the teeing area you may tee the ball anywhere in it. From the general area, a bunker or a penalty area you drop within one club-length of that spot, in the same area of the course and no nearer the hole.

### Back-on-the-line: yellow or red

Find point X, the estimated point where the ball last crossed the edge. Drop on a line that runs from the hole through X and keeps going back, as far as you like. The spot where the ball first touches the ground creates a relief area one club-length in every direction, no nearer the hole than X, outside that penalty area, and in the same area of the course the ball first touched.

The line is strict. A clarification of the Rules gives the example of a player who drops almost a club-length to the side of the line: that is a wrong place even if the ball rolls onto the line, and the ball must be dropped again.

### Lateral relief: red only

Take X as the reference point and drop within two club-lengths of it, no nearer the hole and outside that penalty area. If more than one area of the course lies within those two club-lengths, the ball must come to rest in the same area it first touched when dropped. A club-length means your longest club other than the putter. For players who use a wheeled mobility device, Rule 25 extends lateral relief to four club-lengths.

## Point X is an estimate, and a reasonable one is enough

X is where the ball **last crossed** the edge. It is not where the ball splashed down and not where it came to rest. A ball that crosses into the area, bounces out and rolls back in uses its final crossing.

Making that estimate precisely is often impossible, and the Rules accept it. Under Rule 1.3b(2), if you do what can reasonably be expected to estimate accurately, your judgment stands even if video later shows you were wrong. If you realise your mistake before you play, you must correct it.

## Worked example: a lake down the right

Assume a par 4 with a red-staked lake running along the right of the fairway. Your drive slices and last crosses the lake's edge about halfway down the hole. Every relief option costs one stroke, and every one leaves you playing your third.

- **Stroke and distance:** tee it up again. You lose the length of the drive but get a perfect lie and a fresh look at the hole.
- **Back-on-the-line:** the line from the hole through X angles backwards and further right, so beside a lake that runs along a hole it may track back down the lake itself, or into whatever lies beyond it, before it reaches ground you would want to play from.
- **Lateral:** two club-lengths from X, no nearer the hole, which usually means the rough beside the lake at roughly the distance the drive reached.

Now turn the lake into a stream crossing the fairway and mark it yellow. Lateral relief disappears, but back-on-the-line becomes a precise tool: because there is no limit on how far back you go, you can choose to drop at the distance you hit a full swing best. ## When the next shot stays in, or ends up somewhere worse

Play from the penalty area and leave the ball in the same or another penalty area, and Rule 17.2a gives you the normal options measured from where it last crossed into the area it is now in, plus an extra one: going back to where you last played from outside a penalty area. The R&A's own example is a tee shot into an area, a second shot that stays in, and four options for one stroke, all leaving you playing your fourth.

Rule 17.2b covers the nastier case. If your shot out of a penalty area finishes out of bounds or lost, stroke and distance would put you back in the area. You may instead go directly to relief outside it, for two penalty strokes in total.

## What a penalty area takes away

Rule 17.3 removes three kinds of relief while your ball is in a penalty area: free relief from abnormal course conditions such as a cart path, relief for an embedded ball, and unplayable-ball relief. Rule 17 is your only route out. The one exception is a dangerous animal condition, which allows free relief inside the area or penalty relief outside it.

## Local Rules can add options

Three Model Local Rules change the picture, and the scorecard or local rules sheet will tell you if they apply.

- **Opposite-side relief (Model Local Rule B-2):** for a red area beside a course boundary, or where it can be genuinely unclear which side the ball crossed, the Committee may allow lateral relief on the far side at the same distance from the hole. It may mark those stakes with a different-coloured top.
- **Dropping zones (Model Local Rule E-1):** an extra relief area the Committee sets up where the normal options are impractical.
- **Not E-5:** the Local Rule that offers a two-stroke alternative to stroke and distance cannot be used for a ball known or virtually certain to be in a penalty area.

## Choosing between options

Because every relief option costs the same single stroke, the Rules have reduced the decision to geography. Ask three questions:

1. **Which options exist?** The colour at X and any Local Rules decide that.
2. **What is my realistic next shot from each spot?** Distance, lie, angle and what stands between you and the green.
3. **Is playing it as it lies better than the best of those, once you add the stroke?** Occasionally it is.

The common mistakes all come from skipping step one: dropping nearer the hole than X, measuring lateral relief from where the ball sank instead of where it crossed, using lateral relief at a yellow area, and treating a ball that is merely probably wet as known to be.
