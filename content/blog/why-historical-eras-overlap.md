Historical eras overlap because they are not events. Nobody in 476 or 1500 woke up in a new period; the boundaries were drawn centuries later by historians who needed a vocabulary for talking about change. Different historians, asking different questions about different regions, drew them in different places — and those schemes are all still in use at once. So a single century can legitimately carry two or three era labels, and a timeline that shows this is reporting a real feature of the discipline rather than contradicting itself.

## An era is a claim about what changed

A period label bundles a stretch of time and asserts that something coherent holds across it. "Classical antiquity" asserts a Mediterranean world organised around city-states and then Roman power. "Early modern" asserts printing, long-distance shipping, gunpowder states and confessional conflict. Each label is an argument, and the boundaries are where the person making the argument thinks the thing they care about started or stopped mattering.

That is why the boundary moves when the question moves. Michael Lang's chapter in *The Cambridge World History* sets the study of world history inside a longer history of periodisation itself, treating the schemes as objects of analysis rather than as background furniture. Peter Stearns, writing on periodisation in world history, starts from the premise that the historian's effort to manage time is a scholarly choice open to discussion, and argues that the themes defining each period should be made explicit so they can be debated. Neither treats a boundary as something you look up.

Our article on [the major eras of world history](/blog/major-eras-of-world-history/) walks through the standard scheme and the arguments embedded in each label. This one is about what happens where two of those labels cover the same years.

## Three ordinary reasons two eras cover one century

**A transitional period was added later.** The most common cause. A scheme originally ran straight from "ancient" to "medieval", historians decided the centuries at the join had their own character, and a new label was inserted without the old ones being retired. Late antiquity is the standard example: it occupies ground that older schemes had already assigned to the end of antiquity and the start of the Middle Ages, and all three labels remain in circulation.

**The same label describes regions that changed at different times.** "Medieval" carries a set of western European associations — successor kingdoms, manorial agriculture, Latin Christendom — that do not transfer cleanly elsewhere. Dumbarton Oaks defines the field of Byzantine studies as covering the civilisation of the Byzantine Empire from the fourth to the fifteenth century, a single continuous span that European periodisation cuts into three. Applying the western sequence to the eastern Mediterranean produces boundaries that fall in the middle of nothing.

**Thematic schemes run alongside political ones.** Bronze Age, Iron Age and Neolithic are technological and archaeological categories. Classical, medieval and early modern are political and cultural ones. They were built for different evidence and they do not nest. A region can be in the Iron Age and in classical antiquity simultaneously, because the two statements are about different things.

## A worked case: the third to the seventh centuries

Take the years 224 to 658. UNESCO's inscription for the Sassanid Archaeological Landscape of Fars Region describes the Sasanian Empire as stretching across that region from 224 to 658 CE, with fortified structures, palaces and city plans from the earliest and latest periods of the dynasty, and notes the influence of Achaemenid, Parthian and Roman traditions on architecture that in turn shaped the Islamic era. A single state, a single continuous archaeological record — and European periodisation runs a major boundary straight through the middle of it.

The same span looks different again from the Horn of Africa. UNESCO dates the massive ruins at Aksum, in northern Ethiopia, to between the first and the thirteenth century AD, describing the Kingdom of Aksum as the most powerful state between the Eastern Roman Empire and Persia. That is one site whose active life crosses ancient, late antique, early medieval and high medieval boundaries without interruption.

Meanwhile the western Mediterranean story that generates the standard boundary — the end of the western Roman imperial office — is itself contested, as [the arguments about why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/) show. When the anchor event is disputed, every boundary drawn from it inherits the dispute.

## How to read a timeline that files one year twice

Treat an era chip as a filter, not a container. If you select a period and a year appears that you expected under a different label, the useful question is which framing the label encodes, not which entry is wrong.

Three habits make this manageable:

- **Ask what the label is about.** Political structures, technology, religion, art and economy each generate different boundaries.
- **Ask whose region it describes.** A label built for one region imported into another usually marks nothing local.
- **Prefer a date range to a label when precision matters.** "Third to seventh century" travels between schemes; "late antique" does not.

That is worth using deliberately — run a century through the era filter, then through the region filter, and the overlap stops looking like a glitch. ## When two sources disagree, check the basis

A disagreement about an era boundary is almost never a factual dispute about what happened. It is a disagreement about which change counts as decisive. A book that starts the Middle Ages in 476 and one that starts it in 800 usually agree entirely about the events of those three centuries.

So the resolution is not to find the right date. It is to find out what each author is tracking, and then to decide which framing answers your question.
