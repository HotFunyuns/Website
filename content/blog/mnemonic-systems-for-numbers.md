Every mnemonic system for numbers does one job: it converts digits, which mean nothing, into material that means something, so the knowledge you already have can carry the weight. The four systems below differ only in what they convert digits into — sounds, shapes, rhymes or fixed hooks — and in how much work you have to do before the conversion is free. That set-up cost is the honest difference between them, and it is the part most guides leave out.

Its technique library teaches arithmetic methods and number chunking, and its memory modes give you somewhere to rehearse. Anything in this article, you bring yourself.

## The phonetic system: digits become sounds

The oldest and most capable of the four maps each digit to one or more consonant sounds, leaving vowels, *w*, *h* and *y* free to be inserted wherever you like. A common assignment runs: 0 an *s* or *z*, 1 a *t* or *d*, 2 an *n*, 3 an *m*, 4 an *r*, 5 an *l*, 6 a soft *ch*, *sh* or *j*, 7 a *k* or hard *g*, 8 an *f* or *v*, and 9 a *p* or *b*.

Work an example. `9 4 1 0` gives the consonant skeleton p–r–d–s, and inserting vowels produces **parades**. Going the other way is equally mechanical: the word *flake* has the consonant sounds f, l, k, which read back as `8 5 7`. Note that the mapping is to sounds rather than letters, which is why *flake* is three digits and not four — the silent *e* contributes nothing.

The system scales indefinitely, preserves order inside each word, and is fully reversible, which is why it underlies most serious digit memorisation. It has also been formalised well enough for researchers to automate: Fiorentini, Shao and Medero built encoding models for generating mnemonic sentences from numbers and evaluated them in a password memorability study, reporting that combining part-of-speech sentence templates with a language model produced the most memorable representations.

Its weakness is the run-in period. Until the digit-to-sound mapping fires without thinking, converting a number takes longer than rehearsing it, which means weeks in which the system costs you more than it returns. Most people quit inside that window, and it is a reasonable thing to quit if the numbers in your life are short.

## Number-shape and number-rhyme: fast to learn, quick to run out

Both of these trade capability for speed of acquisition.

A **number-shape** system uses the visual form of the written digit: 1 as a pen or candle, 2 as a swan, 6 as an elephant's trunk, 8 as an hourglass. You will have most of these within ten minutes because the resemblance does the teaching.

A **number-rhyme** system uses sound instead: one is a bun, two is a shoe, three is a tree, four is a door, five is a hive. Equally quick, for the same reason.

Both are genuinely useful for short, fixed lists, and both stop dead at ten. They give you one image per digit rather than per pair, so a twelve-digit string becomes twelve images — no compression at all, which is the thing you actually wanted. Treat them as a way of understanding how encoding feels before investing in something that scales.

## Peg systems: solving order rather than content

A peg system is a pre-learned list of hooks that never changes. The rhyme list above is the simplest one. Whatever you need to remember gets attached to peg one, peg two, peg three, and the peg list supplies the ordering for free.

This solves a problem the other systems do not. Encoding tells you *what*; pegs tell you *where*. If someone asks for the sixth item, a peg user goes straight to it, while a chain user has to replay the whole sequence to reach it. For anything you will be interrogated about out of order, that is the whole game.

The spatial version replaces the fixed list with a route through a place you know. Sandberg and colleagues, testing a smartphone-delivered method of loci, describe participants visualising things and "imagined placing them at specific places (loci) along familiar paths", with instructional videos using the body, a house and a car as memory palaces. The same technique appears in studies of competitive memorisers: a neuroimaging study of world-ranked memory athletes characterises them as mentally navigating a familiar route and associating material with distinct landmarks.

Pegs and encoding combine rather than compete. Serious digit work usually means converting pairs of digits into images with a phonetic system, then placing those images along a route.

## The set-up cost, stated plainly

This is where an honest article has to slow down. The systems are real and they work, and they are also a project.

The Sandberg training study asked participants to watch six instruction videos and then "practice with the app for a duration of 3 months, preferably each day." That is the dose the researchers judged necessary before measuring what people had gained. It is not a weekend.

The returns are also narrower than the enthusiasm suggests. Norris, Hall and Gathercole, testing extensive span training, concluded that it "does not substantially improve the capacity of verbal STM" and found little transfer even to closely related tasks. A mnemonic system is a strategy layered on top of a capacity, not an expansion of it — you get much better at the material you encode, and nothing about the underlying limit moves. [Our guide to working memory](/blog/working-memory-and-training/) sets out that distinction in full, and it is the single most important thing to keep straight before starting.

The reasonable conclusion is not "don't bother". It is: pick the smallest system that covers your actual numbers.
