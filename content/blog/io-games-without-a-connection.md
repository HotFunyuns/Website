"Offline io game" is very close to a contradiction. The format was built on browsers and servers: the whole novelty in 2015 was that the blobs around you were other people. Strip the connection out and you have not made a clever engineering decision — you have replaced the other people with software.

That is a perfectly reasonable thing to build, and we build one. It is also a different product from what the label originally promised, and the thing worth checking before you install anything in this shelf is whether the developer says which they are.

## The genre's own description of itself

It is worth reading what the well-known titles say on their own front pages, because they are unambiguous about it.

Agar.io's site describes playing online with players around the world as you try to become the biggest cell of them all, controlling a tiny cell and eating other players to grow, with splitting, shrinking and dodging tactics used to catch other players or avoid them. Slither.io's site describes playing with millions of players around the world and trying to become the longest of the day.

Those are not incidental marketing lines. They are the product. Agar.io is described as a massively multiplayer online game, and the mechanic that made it spread — the tension of a shared space where every other shape has a person behind it — does not survive the network being removed.

So when a game in this style runs without a connection, it has made a substitution. The honest ones say so.

Two rows in that table are straightforwardly in the other game's favour and we are not going to bury them. **Mode variety** is one: a single arena is a smaller offer than a set of modes, and if you want team play or a battle-royale variant, we do not have one. **Platform reach and player base** is the other, and it is the bigger of the two — a game with a live worldwide population offers something an AI arena structurally cannot, which is the possibility of a genuinely unexpected run.

It is also worth noting that bots are not a Reign Creative invention or a budget substitute. Agar.io's own mobile version includes a Relax mode in which all of the other players are bots, which tells you the genre worked out some time ago that there is real demand for the arena shape without the arena population.

What our approach gives in exchange is availability and consistency. A run starts the moment you tap it, at any hour, with a full field, in a tunnel with no signal. Nothing waits on matchmaking, and nothing disconnects halfway through an encounter.

## Why ours is AI-filled, and why we say so

[Jellyfish Arena Survivor io](/apps/jellyfish-arena-survivor-io/) fills its arena with up to 100 AI-controlled rivals. It plays like a multiplayer arena and it is a solo experience.

We could have left that vague. Every word the genre uses — rivals, arena, leaderboard, crown — is compatible with an AI field, and a listing that simply never mentions the question lets most readers assume live players. That is the conventional move and we think it is a small dishonesty with a predictable ending: the player works it out inside one run and trusts nothing else the listing said.

So the store FAQ answers it directly, and so does this page: the rivals are AI-controlled.

Three consequences follow, and only two of them are good.

**Instant starts.** There is no lobby to fill, so a run begins immediately regardless of time of day or how many other people are playing. For a five-minute game, that is most of the value.

**More learnable tactics.** Bots are consistent. A cut-off angle that worked once tends to work again, which makes the mid-size stage practisable in a way that a lobby of unpredictable humans is not — the tactical detail is in [how to stop dying at medium size](/blog/eat-and-grow-arena-strategy/).

**A lower ceiling.** This is the cost. A bot field will not produce the extraordinary run where the top three eliminate each other and the map suddenly opens up. If that specific kind of chaos is what you want from an io game, we are honestly not it.

## Checking any of this for yourself

The store listing will not tell you whether a game runs offline; there is no field for it. The reliable method is the same cold-start test that works for every Android game, set out in [what "offline" actually means and how to test it](/blog/best-offline-arcade-games-android/): install, play one normal session with a connection, force-stop, enable airplane mode, cold-launch.

For an io-styled game, add one check. **Start a run offline and look at the leaderboard.** If the arena populates instantly with named rivals and a board fills up while you have no network at all, the answer to "am I playing people" is settled, whatever the listing implied.

The same structural question — whether a game's opposition is simulated locally or fetched from somewhere — comes up across genres, and the management titles face a near-identical version of it, covered in [sports GM games without internet](/blog/sports-gm-games-without-internet/).

## The question that actually matters

Not "is it offline". Not even "is it bots".

**Does the game tell you which, before you install it?**

An AI-filled arena that says so is a clear offer you can accept or decline. A server-backed game that says so is the same. The one to avoid is the listing that carefully never mentions it, because a developer who is vague about the single most basic fact about their game is unlikely to be precise about anything else.
