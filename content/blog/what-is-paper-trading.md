Paper trading is practising trading without real money. You place buy and sell orders in a simulator, or once upon a time on paper, and track the results as if they were real. It is the obvious first step for anyone who wants to understand how trading works, and it has genuine value. It also has limits that are easy to forget, and those limits all push in the same direction: simulated results tend to look better than real ones would.

## What paper trading teaches well

**The vocabulary and mechanics.** Placing market, limit and stop orders, watching how a position is opened and closed, and seeing how profit and loss is calculated are much easier to learn by doing than by reading. Our guide to [market versus limit orders](/blog/market-order-vs-limit-order/) makes far more sense after you have placed both.

**Reasoning before acting.** A simulator lets you practise a useful habit: writing down why you are making a trade and what would make you exit, before you place it. The listing for [Learn to Trade Stocks Beginner](/apps/learn-to-trade-stocks/) builds its routine around the same idea: practise with virtual money, then compare the result with your reason for buying or selling.

**Reviewing mistakes without cost.** A bad decision in a simulator costs nothing but time. That makes it the right place to find out that you tend to sell too early, or to ignore your own exit plan.

## What paper trading cannot reproduce

| Real-world factor | What a simulator may do | Effect on results |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commissions and fees | Ignores or simplifies them | Simulated returns look higher |
| The bid–ask spread | May fill at the last price or midpoint | Simulated fills look better |
| Order execution | Assumes fills that may not happen in a real market | Hard-to-fill trades look easy |
| Taxes | Usually ignored | Real after-tax results are lower |
| Emotion | Absent: the money is not yours | Real decisions under pressure can be worse |

Each row matters. Investor.gov's guide to how orders are executed explains that prices can change before an order reaches the market, and that quotes apply only to a limited number of shares, so real fills can differ from the screen. The [bid–ask spread](/blog/bid-ask-spread-explained/) is a cost on every round trip. FINRA's guidance on frequent trading points out that frequent trading comes with higher costs that can erode returns, and with tax implications; [capital gains on stocks](/blog/capital-gains-tax-on-stocks/) explains how gains are taxed in the US.

Simulators differ in how they handle each of these, so check yours. Emotion is the hardest to simulate: the app's own description says practising with virtual money does not reproduce the emotions of real risk, and decisions such as sticking to an exit plan can feel very different when the money is yours. The app's own listing is direct about the overall limit: simulated trading results cannot predict what will happen in the real stock market.

## Making practice more realistic

1. **Use realistic amounts.** If you would invest $1,000, practise with $1,000 positions, not the whole virtual balance.
2. **Charge yourself costs.** Subtract an estimate for spreads and fees from every simulated round trip.
3. **Use the order types you would really use,** and note when a limit order would not have filled.
4. **Write the plan first.** Reason, entry, exit and the most you are willing to lose, recorded before the trade. [How to keep a trading journal](/blog/trading-journal-how-to/) suggests a format.
5. **Review losses as seriously as gains.** A trade that went against you is as useful to compare with its reason as one that worked.
6. **Think about [risk tolerance](/blog/risk-tolerance-explained/)** honestly, not about what a simulator lets you get away with.

## When to move on, if ever

There is no rule for how long to paper trade, and no simulated record shows that someone is ready to risk real money. A more useful test is whether you can explain each order type, each figure on your screen and each loss in your journal without looking anything up. Some people find they would rather invest for the long term through diversified funds than trade actively at all, and a simulator is a cheap way to discover that too. Whatever you decide, the decision belongs to you, ideally with professional advice where your own money is concerned.

## How to judge your practice

A large virtual profit says little on its own. It may reflect a few lucky trades, unrealistic position sizes or costs left out. Better questions:

- Did I follow my written plan on each trade?
- Were my reasons specific and checkable?
- Do my losses come from bad reasoning or ordinary uncertainty?
- Would the results survive realistic costs?
