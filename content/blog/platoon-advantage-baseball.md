**The platoon advantage is the edge a hitter usually has against a pitcher who throws with the opposite hand.** A left-handed hitter facing a right-handed pitcher, or a right-handed hitter facing a left-hander, has the platoon advantage; when the two share a side, the pitcher has it. FanGraphs' library describes "a long history of research and observation" showing most hitters do better against opposite-handed pitching, and the gap in performance between the two situations is called a platoon split.

How big is it? A FanGraphs analysis of 2007–2009 major-league data, measured with weighted on-base average and described by its author as a basic demonstration, put the average split at about 8.6 percent for left-handed hitters and about 6.1 percent for right-handed hitters. Real, consistent and modest — which is why managers plan matchups around it, and why individual players' splits are so easy to over-read.

## Why the same side favours the pitcher

FanGraphs' splits entry gives two reasons.

**Seeing the ball.** It appears to be harder to pick the ball up out of the hand of a same-side pitcher, and particular arm slots make that worse.

**How the pitches move.** FanGraphs' rule of thumb is that a pitch breaking away from the hitter is harder to handle. A slider thrown to a same-handed hitter breaks away from him; to an opposite-handed hitter it breaks in. That is why, in FanGraphs' words, a slider works best against same-handed hitters and a changeup works best against opposite-handed ones.

The pitch mix matters enough that FanGraphs offers a basic guide to platoon effects by pitch type. In it, the slider carries a large "normal" effect — it plays better against same-handed hitters — while the changeup and the curveball carry small "reverse" effects, playing slightly better against opposite-handed ones. A pitcher who lives on sliders will tend to have a large split. One built around a changeup may have a much smaller one.

## What a split measures, and why it misleads

A platoon split compares a player's results against left-handers with his results against right-handers. FanGraphs separates the meaningful kinds of split — handedness is one — from trivial ones such as day of the week, which differ only through random variation. But even a meaningful split has three traps.

### The smaller side is small

FanGraphs' example: a split over 300 total plate appearances is likely to include only 75 to 100 against left-handers, "which is simply not enough to tell you much about the hitter." The sample that matters is the weaker side's, not the season total.

### The opponents are not random

Left-handed relievers are the clearest case. They come in to face left-handed hitters and often leave before a good right-hander bats, so the right-handers they do face are weaker than average. Their stats against right-handers look better than their true ability. FanGraphs makes the same point from the hitter's side: if you are terrible against left-handed pitching, you will rarely be allowed to face the best of it.

### Observed is not true talent

The split you see is one part skill and one part noise. FanGraphs' Matt Klaassen, applying the regression rule of thumb from *The Book*, shrinks each hitter's observed split toward the league average: left-handed hitters against 1,000 plate appearances versus left-handed pitching, right-handed hitters against 2,200. Left-handers need less regression because, he writes, they show more variation in platoon skill. His summary is worth remembering: "splits in the retrospective mirror are almost always smaller than they appear."

A worked example using that method, with hypothetical hitters who have each shown a 20 percent split over 300 plate appearances against left-handed pitching:

| | Right-handed hitter | Left-handed hitter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observed split | 20% | 20% |
| League average split used | 6.1% | 8.6% |
| Regression weight | 2,200 PA | 1,000 PA |
| Estimate | (0.20 × 300 + 0.061 × 2,200) ÷ 2,500 ≈ 7.8% | (0.20 × 300 + 0.086 × 1,000) ÷ 1,300 ≈ 11.2% |

Both hitters' "huge" splits shrink to something close to average, and the right-hander's shrinks more. Neither estimate says the observed split was fake; it says that 300 plate appearances is not enough to tell how much of it was real.

## Reverse splits

A reverse split is the opposite pattern: a hitter who does better against same-handed pitching, or a pitcher who does better against opposite-handed hitters. Two explanations compete.

**The pitch mix.** The changeup and the curveball carry small reverse platoon effects in FanGraphs' guide, so a pitcher who relies on them can genuinely play better against opposite-handed hitters than a slider-first pitcher would.

**The sample.** In Klaassen's worked examples, a right-handed hitter with an observed reverse split of 6.3 percent came out at a normal split of 2.7 percent after regression. Many observed reverse splits are small samples doing what small samples do.

The working rule: believe a reverse split when there is a mechanism (the pitch mix) and a large sample; otherwise assume it is noise.

## How the rules shape the matchup game

**The three-batter minimum.** Rule 5.10(g)(1) of the 2026 Official Baseball Rules requires any pitcher to face at least three consecutive batters, or finish the half-inning, unless injured or ill. MLB.com's glossary explains it was introduced to reduce pitching changes, replacing rules in effect through 2019 that required only a single batter. A reliever brought in for one same-handed hitter now has to face two more, whatever their handedness, unless the half-inning ends first.

**Ambidextrous pitchers.** Rule 5.07(f) requires a pitcher who can throw with either hand to show the umpire, batter and runners which hand he will use — for example by wearing his glove on the other hand — and bars him from switching until that batter's plate appearance is over, barring injury or a pinch-hitter.

**Switch-hitters.** MLB.com's cutter entry notes that a switch-hitter typically bats from the side opposite the pitcher's throwing arm, taking the platoon advantage every time, with a rare exception for pitchers who throw mostly cutters, whose late movement can jam opposite-handed hitters.

## Batting left or right: what to weigh

Treated as an analytical question rather than a matter of how a hitter grew up swinging, the sources above point to three considerations:

1. **Average split size.** The FanGraphs analysis found a larger average split for left-handed hitters (8.6 percent) than for right-handed hitters (6.1 percent).
2. **Variation.** Left-handed hitters show more variation in platoon skill, so a lefty's split says more about him individually than a righty's does.
3. **The pitchers you will face.** A split only matters as often as the matchup happens, so its cost depends on the mix of left- and right-handed pitching in your league and how managers deploy their relievers.
