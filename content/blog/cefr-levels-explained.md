**The CEFR describes what a learner can do, not what they have studied.** That is the design decision that makes it useful, and it is why a CEFR level is a better study target than a number of hours or a number of completed lessons.

## The six levels

| Band | Level | Roughly, what you can do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A — Basic user | A1 | Handle very basic phrases; introduce yourself; ask and answer simple personal questions |
| | A2 | Communicate in routine tasks on familiar matters; describe your background and immediate environment |
| B — Independent user | B1 | Deal with most situations while travelling; produce simple connected text on familiar topics; describe experiences and plans |
| | B2 | Interact with fluency and spontaneity; produce detailed text on a range of subjects; follow complex argument in your field |
| C — Proficient user | C1 | Express ideas fluently without obvious searching; use the language flexibly for social, academic and professional purposes |
| | C2 | Understand virtually everything heard or read; summarise and reconstruct arguments coherently |

The official descriptors are published by the [European Commission through Europass](https://europa.eu/europass/en/common-european-framework-reference-language-skills), and they are worth reading directly, because the phrasing is deliberate: every descriptor is a "can do" statement about a task.

## Why "can do" matters

Compare two ways of describing a learner:

- "Has completed 120 lessons and knows 2,000 words."
- "Can handle most situations likely to arise while travelling in an area where the language is spoken."

The first describes input. The second describes capability, and it is testable — you can put someone in that situation and find out.

That distinction is the reason CEFR is used for admissions, employment and immigration decisions. It answers the question those decisions actually need answered.

## The levels are not equally sized

This is the most practically important fact about the framework, and it is often left out.

**Each level takes substantially longer than the one before.** Roughly speaking, the gap between A1 and A2 is much smaller than the gap between B2 and C1, because the range of situations expands as you climb.

For a learner, that produces a predictable experience: **rapid, satisfying early progress, then apparent stalling.** You have not stopped improving. The unit of measurement got larger.

Understanding this in advance prevents a great deal of unnecessary discouragement. [The language learning plateau](/blog/language-learning-plateau/) covers this in detail.

## Why B1 to B2 is where people stop

The transition from B1 to B2 is where a large share of learners plateau, and the reason is structural rather than motivational.

Below B2, improvement means **handling familiar situations more accurately**. You already know the topics; you get better at them. Study material is well matched to what you are being asked to do.

At B2 and above, improvement means **handling unfamiliar material**. That requires vocabulary breadth, exposure to varied registers, and comfort with not understanding everything. Courses are less helpful here, because the requirement is volume of authentic input rather than instruction.

The practical implication: the study method that got you to B1 is not the method that gets you to B2. At that point, [comprehensible input](/blog/comprehensible-input-explained/) and volume of reading and listening matter more than lessons.

## How to use levels as a target

Three suggestions:

**Pick a level, not a timeframe.** "Reach B1" is achievable and testable. "Be fluent in six months" is neither.

**Read the descriptors for your target.** They tell you what to practise. If B1 requires describing experiences and plans, practise describing experiences and plans — not more vocabulary lists.

**Do not chase the certificate unless you need it.** Formal examinations cost money and time. If nobody is asking for proof, the descriptors are useful as a map without taking the test.

## CEFR is not the only framework

The [ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines](https://www.actfl.org/educator-resources/actfl-proficiency-guidelines) describe similar ground with different terminology and a different assessment tradition, and are more common in the United States.

Mappings between the two exist and are approximate. If you need a specific level for a specific purpose, use whichever framework that purpose asks for rather than converting between them.

## What the levels do not tell you

Two honest limitations:

**They do not describe how long it takes.** The framework is deliberately silent on this, because the answer depends on the learner, the language and the study method. [How long does it take to learn Italian](/blog/how-long-to-learn-italian/) covers why published estimates disagree so widely.

**They do not measure vocabulary size directly.** Vocabulary is implied by the tasks rather than specified — see [how many words do you need to be conversational](/blog/how-many-words-to-be-conversational/) for what the research on that question actually supports.
