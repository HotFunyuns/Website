Plan fewer meals than there are days.

That is the single change that turns meal planning from a thing people try for two weeks into a thing people keep doing. A plan that fills all twenty-one slots is a timetable, and a timetable fails the first time something runs late. A plan with four dinners and three gaps survives a bad Wednesday, because the gap was already there.

Everything below is downstream of that.

## Count the nights before you choose the food

The order most people use is: browse recipes, get enthusiastic, assign one to every evening, shop, then discover on Tuesday that the week has other ideas.

Reverse it. Before any food is chosen, answer one question: **how many evenings will you realistically cook this week?** Not aspirationally. Count the nights you are actually home, not out, not exhausted, not eating with someone else.

The number is usually three to five. Plan for that number, and let the remaining evenings be undefined — leftovers, something simple, whatever is around.

This sounds like defeatism and it is the opposite. A plan sized to reality gets followed, and a plan that gets followed is the only kind that saves any money or effort.

## Start at the cupboard, not the recipe site

The second habit worth building: **look at what you already have before you look at what you might make.**

Ingredients already in the house are free in the sense that the money is spent, and they are perishable in a way that a recipe idea is not. A plan built from a recipe site tends to generate a shopping list that ignores half a fridge, which costs twice — once on duplicates, and again when the originals are thrown out.

So the sequence is: what needs using, what can be made from it, what is missing. The list comes last and it comes out of the plan rather than the other way round. Keeping the inventory side of that workable is its own problem, covered in [pantry inventory and grocery lists that stay accurate](/blog/pantry-inventory-and-grocery-lists/).

## The constraint that is not negotiable

Meal planning bumps into food safety, and this is the part where a rule of thumb is not good enough.

The FDA's published storage guidance is specific: keep the refrigerator at or below **40°F (4°C)** and the freezer at **0°F (-18°C)**, check the temperatures periodically with an appliance thermometer, and apply the **two-hour rule** — perishable items should not sit at room temperature longer than that. It also notes that refrigerated ready-to-eat foods should be used as soon as possible, and that food can make you ill even when it looks, smells and tastes fine, because the bacteria that cause illness are different from the ones that make food obviously spoil.

MedlinePlus frames the same territory as four steps: **clean, separate, cook, chill** — wash produce, hands, counters and utensils; keep raw foods apart; get food properly hot; and refrigerate fresh food promptly.

None of that is advice about what to eat. It is a constraint on *when* a planned meal can still be cooked, and it is the reason "cook on Sunday, eat all week" needs more thought than it usually gets. For the specifics of what keeps and what does not, see [batch cooking that survives four days in the fridge](/blog/batch-cooking-for-the-week/).

## Why our planner has fixed slots

[Easy Recipes & Meal Planner](/apps/easy-recipes-meal-planner/) puts recipes into fixed **breakfast, lunch, dinner and snack** slots for each day, rather than offering a free-form list you can arrange however you like.

That was a deliberate constraint and it was argued about, because a free-form list is obviously more flexible. Three reasons won.

**A slot makes an omission visible.** A free-form list of nine meals for a week looks complete. A grid with empty Thursday and Friday dinner slots tells you something at a glance that a list hides.

**A slot is a stable target for moving things.** If Wednesday's dinner did not happen, it has somewhere specific to go. Dragging a meal into an identified empty slot is a two-second decision; reordering a list is a small editing task, and small editing tasks are where planning apps get abandoned.

**A slot carries context.** Breakfast, lunch and dinner are not interchangeable in most households, and a planner that treats them as one undifferentiated stream produces plans nobody follows.

The honest cost is that the structure does not fit everyone. If you eat two meals a day, or graze, or work shifts, a fixed four-slot grid has empty space in it that is not really empty — it is just not your shape. We think the trade is right for most weeks and we are not going to pretend it is right for all of them.

### The behaviour that design has to support

Fixed slots only work if **replanning is cheap**. That is the whole bargain.

A planner with rigid slots and awkward editing is worse than a paper list, because it adds friction to the exact moment — Thursday evening, plan already slipping — when friction makes people quit. So the work went into moving a planned recipe to a different day and a different slot, and into letting the week be reshuffled rather than rebuilt.

The test we used internally was not "does a finished week look tidy". It was "can a week that has already gone wrong be fixed in under a minute". If the answer is no, the fixed-slot design is not paying for itself.

## A method that takes about fifteen minutes

1. **Count the cooking nights.** Three to five is normal. Write the number down before anything else.
2. **Open the fridge and the cupboard.** Note what needs using this week.
3. **Choose that many meals**, weighted towards using what you found in step two.
4. **Decide the serving size for each**, because that determines both the shopping list and whether there are leftovers. Scaling is less straightforward than multiplying — [why doubling a recipe is not just doubling](/blog/recipe-scaling-and-serving-sizes/) covers the parts that do not scale linearly.
5. **Place them in slots**, putting the most perishable ingredients on the earliest nights.
6. **Build the shopping list from the gap** between what the plan needs and what you already have.
7. **Leave the remaining evenings blank on purpose.**

Step seven is the one people delete, and it is the one that makes the other six survive.

## On budgets, honestly

Meal planning is often sold as a money-saving technique and it can be one, through two specific mechanisms: fewer duplicate purchases, and less thrown away. Both come from shopping against a list derived from an inventory.

What it does not do is make food cheaper on its own. A plan assembled from ambitious recipes without reference to the cupboard will comfortably cost more than shopping with no plan at all. The saving is in the sequence, not in the act of planning.
