Seven decisions. Six players and a coach, and the season is set.

That number is the whole design. In a sport where you draft dozens of players, a bad pick is absorbed — it becomes a small drag on a large system. Here, one poor slot is a substantial fraction of everything you own, and no amount of quality elsewhere hides it. [84-0 Hockey Draft & GM Manager](/apps/hockey-draft-gm-manager/) is built to reward balance between scoring and defence and to punish thin spots, and with a roster this small it does not have to try hard to do either.

## The smallest puzzle in the genre

It is worth seeing the roster sizes side by side, because they explain why the same drafting instincts produce different results across sports.

| Game | Picks that make the roster |
| --- | --- |
| 84-0 Hockey Draft & GM Manager | Six players and a coach |
| [162-0 Baseball Draft & GM Team](/apps/baseball-draft-gm-team/) | Nine players and a coach |
| [38-0 Football Draft XI Soccer](/apps/soccer-draft-gm-xi/) | A full starting XI |

As the roster shrinks, two things happen at once. Each individual pick matters more, and the number of ways to compensate for a mistake falls. A nine-player club has slack; a six-player roster does not. That is why a drafting habit that works perfectly well in the [baseball draft simulator](/blog/baseball-draft-gm-guide/) — taking a flyer on a slot you can fix later — is expensive here.

The corollary is more encouraging: with seven decisions, you can actually think about all of them. A hockey draft is short enough to plan properly, which makes it the best entry point in the genre for anyone who wants to learn drafting theory without managing a large board at the same time.

## Scoring and defence are not the same currency

The obvious way to draft is to take the best available player at each position and let the balance take care of itself. It does not take care of itself, and the reason is worth understanding rather than just accepting.

Scoring and defensive quality do different jobs. More scoring raises how many games you can win by outscoring the opponent. More defensive quality raises how many games stay winnable in the first place. A roster with excellent scoring and thin defence spends the season in high-variance games; a roster with the reverse spends it in tight ones it cannot always close out.

Neither is wrong. What is wrong is buying your fourth unit of a strength you already have while a genuine gap sits open, because the return on the strength is diminishing and the cost of the gap is not.

**A working rule:** at each pick, ask what the roster is currently worst at, and require a clear reason before you draft anything else. "This player is rated higher" is not a clear reason on its own — it is the input, not the argument.

## The goaltending problem

Every small-roster sport has one position that carries disproportionate weight, and in hockey it is the goaltender. It is also the position where drafters make the most self-inflicted errors, in both directions.

Under-spending is the common one. Goaltending is a single slot among seven, and it is easy to treat it as one-seventh of the decision. It is not, because nothing else on the roster substitutes for it — a skater who is having a poor run can be offset by the others, and there is no equivalent redundancy behind a weak goaltender.

Over-spending is the subtler one. Committing your best resources to that slot and then filling five skating positions with whatever is left produces exactly the lopsided roster the projection penalises. The game weighs defence and goaltending as inputs alongside everything else; it does not let one exceptional slot carry an otherwise hollow lineup.

The honest answer is that goaltending should be solved early and solved *properly* — not maximised, and never deferred.

## Search versus judgement

There are two rulesets, and they are not difficulty tiers.

**Unlimited Draft** keeps your options open. You can keep looking until you find the combination you want. What it tests is whether you know what a good roster looks like when nothing prevents you from building one — and the honest failure mode is spending a long time confirming a decision you had already made, then producing a roster no better than a decisive one.

**Limited Draft** gives you one pass at every position with zero refreshes. Search is off the table, and what replaces it is inference. Each option you see is evidence about the quality level of this run, and a good limited drafter is continuously updating a sense of what "good" means here rather than applying a fixed standard from previous games.

The classic limited-draft failure is holding out at an early position for a quality that was never in this pool, then accepting something genuinely poor at the end because there is nothing left. Calibrate on the first two or three slots, then decide quickly.

## The pick that is not a player

The coach completes the roster, and it is the pick most likely to be made carelessly after the effort of assembling six players.

Coaching is one of the factors the projection weighs. More usefully, it is the one decision you make with complete information about your own roster — by the time you get there, you know exactly what you built, where you compromised, and what the lineup will struggle with. Treating it as the final adjustment rather than a formality is free improvement.

## Reading an 84-game projection

The simulator turns the finished lineup into a complete regular-season record over 84 games.

Be clear about what that is. It is the output of a model, computed from the roster you assembled according to the game's own rules. It does not describe, predict or relate to any real hockey game, club, player or season — the entire pool is fictional and generated inside the game. It is not a forecast of anything, and it carries no information about any real-world event.

What it *is* is a score, and the most productive way to use it is comparatively. Most rosters land short of perfect, which is by design; the number to beat is not the perfect record but your own previous best. Replaying with a different approach and improving on your last result is how the game is meant to be played, and it also happens to be a decent experimental method: change one thing, run it again, see whether the projection moves.

That discipline — one variable at a time — is worth more than any individual piece of drafting advice, because it lets you learn the model rather than trusting somebody's rules of thumb about it.

## Franchise GM Mode

Beyond a single draft, Franchise GM Mode organises forwards, defenders and goaltending across multiple seasons, weighing strengths against weak positions and guiding the club forward.

The change this introduces is a time dimension. A one-off draft asks what is best now. A franchise asks what stays good, and the two answers differ. A roster optimised for a single projection can be a poor foundation, while a roster built to remain solid may never post the single best season you are capable of.

The other thing multi-season play gives you is a cure for a bad draft. In a one-shot format, an imbalanced roster is simply the roster. Across seasons, shoring up a weak position before it costs you is a move you can actually make — which is the same corrective role trades play in the larger-roster sports, covered in the [basketball draft and GM guide](/blog/basketball-draft-gm-guide/).

## The supporting formats

Timed challenges, roster-building tasks, quizzes, achievements and side-by-side player comparisons fill in around the management.

The comparison tool deserves a mention, because it addresses the single hardest part of small-roster drafting: deciding between two players who are close. When each pick is roughly a seventh of your team, "close enough, take either" is not good advice, and looking at two profiles directly against each other is usually how you find the attribute that actually separates them.

## A drafting checklist

1. **Solve goaltending properly and early.** Not maximised, not deferred.
2. **At each pick, name the roster's current worst area** and require an argument to draft anywhere else.
3. **Calibrate in the first three picks** if refreshes are off, then commit without agonising.
4. **Make the coach a considered decision**, with the roster's known weakness in mind.
5. **Change one thing per run.** The projection is only informative if you can attribute the change to something.
