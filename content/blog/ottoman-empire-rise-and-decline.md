The Ottoman Empire began as a small principality in north-western Anatolia in the early fourteenth century and ended six centuries later as a defeated belligerent of the First World War, succeeded by the Republic of Türkiye. In between it governed territory that UNESCO describes as reaching from Anatolia to Yemen and including parts of Europe and North Africa.

Compressing that into "the sick man of Europe" tells you nothing about five and a half of those six centuries, and the phrase was a nineteenth-century diplomatic slur rather than an analysis.

## Bursa: how a frontier state built a capital

The empire's first capital was Bursa, on the slopes of Uludağ. UNESCO's inscription for Bursa and the nearby village of Cumalıkızık describes them as representing the creation of an urban and rural system that established the first Ottoman capital and the sultan's seat in the early fourteenth century.

The mechanism is the interesting part. The city grew from five focal points, mostly on hills, where five sultans — Orhan Ghazi, Murad I, Yıldırım Bayezid, Çelebi Mehmed and Murad II — established public *kulliyes*: complexes of mosque, madrasa, public bath, public kitchen and tomb. Each was financed through the *waqf* endowment system, and the endowments determined how the city expanded. Cumalıkızık itself was a waqf village whose purpose, recorded in historical documents, was to generate income for the Orhan Ghazi complex.

UNESCO's assessment is that this method allowed the fast establishment of a viable new capital, because the public infrastructure went in before the neighbourhoods did. It also notes that Ottoman architecture from the outset integrated Byzantine, Seljuk, Arab, Persian and other influences — which matters, because the empire that emerged was a synthesis rather than an import.

## 1453 and the acquisition of a world city

Constantinople fell to Sultan Mehmed II on 29 May 1453, after a siege the Byzantine and Christian Museum in Athens describes as two suffocating months.

The museum, which is a Greek national institution and reports the event from that vantage point, lists the consequences it considers most important: Europe felt the threat of Ottoman expansion for the first time, that fear drove population movements, and the Balkan peoples already under Ottoman rule suffered enormous losses. Those are the judgements of one national historiography, and they should be read as such — which does not make them wrong, and the date itself is not disputed.

What the Ottomans acquired was a city with a thousand years of imperial infrastructure. UNESCO's inscription for the Historic Areas of Istanbul describes a skyline built up over many centuries by both Byzantine and Ottoman architects: the sixth-century dome of Hagia Sophia, the fifteenth-century Fatih complex, Topkapı Palace extended into the nineteenth century, the Süleymaniye and Şehzade complexes by the chief architect Sinan that UNESCO calls the climax of sixteenth-century Ottoman architecture, the seventeenth-century Blue Mosque, and the New Mosque completed in 1664.

Bursa kept its prestige. UNESCO records that sultans and courtiers continued to choose Bursa for burial even after the conquest of Istanbul, out of loyalty to their ancestors and to the city.

## Governing a religiously plural empire

The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum's phrase for the Ottoman state is "the multiethnic Ottoman Empire", and that is the fact any account of its governance has to start from.

In the standard scholarly account, the empire administered its non-Muslim populations through recognised communal structures — the arrangement usually called the millet system — which gave religious communities substantial self-government over their own legal and personal-status affairs while also defining them as a distinct and subordinate legal category, with specific fiscal obligations attached.

Popular writing pulls this in two directions: a model of pre-modern tolerance, or uninterrupted persecution. Both are too simple for an arrangement that ran for centuries, varied by community and province, and changed substantially under nineteenth-century reform. It was neither equality nor uniform oppression, and saying which it more closely resembled in a given place and decade requires evidence from that place and decade.

That is a case where the distinctions in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) do real work: much of what circulates about Ottoman religious policy comes from nineteenth-century European commentary written to justify intervention, or from later national historiographies written to justify separation.

## The long nineteenth century

From the late eighteenth century the empire faced military defeat, territorial loss, separatist nationalism and European financial pressure simultaneously, and it responded with a sustained programme of legal, military, fiscal and educational restructuring — the reform era usually called the Tanzimat, followed by constitutional experiments later in the century.

Two points are worth holding onto. First, reform was not a symptom of paralysis: it was an active state project that rebuilt the army, the bureaucracy, the tax system and the law over decades. Second, it did not save the empire, and historians argue about why — whether reform came too late, conceded too much, or accelerated the very nationalisms it was meant to contain.

This is the same shape of argument as the one examined in [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/). Where a field offers several partial explanations and no decisive one, the right output is the set of competing accounts with their evidence attached, not a verdict.

## 1915-16: the Armenian genocide

This has to be stated directly.

From spring 1915 through autumn 1916, Ottoman authorities carried out the physical annihilation of Armenian Christian communities living in the empire. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum records that approximately 1.5 million Armenians lived in the Ottoman Empire in 1915, and that at least 664,000 and possibly as many as 1.2 million died — in local massacres and individual killings, and through systematic ill-treatment, exposure, dehydration, starvation and disease during deportation.

The museum is specific about responsibility and intent. Ottoman authorities, supported by auxiliary troops and at times by civilians, perpetrated most of the persecution and mass killing. The Ottoman government of the period was controlled by the Committee of Union and Progress, also called the Young Turks, and its aim was to solidify Muslim Turkish dominance in central and eastern Anatolia by eliminating the sizeable Armenian presence there. Deportations began in spring 1915 from the north-eastern border regions, on the stated fear that Armenians would join invading enemy troops, and then expanded to almost all provinces regardless of their distance from any combat zone. Victims died in or en route to the desert regions of the southern empire — today northern and eastern Syria, northern Saudi Arabia and Iraq.

The casualty range given above is a range for a reason, and it should always be quoted as one.

**On the word itself.** The United Nations Office on Genocide Prevention records that "genocide" was coined by the Polish lawyer Raphael Lemkin in 1944, from the Greek *genos* and the Latin *cide*, developed partly in response to Nazi policies but also in response to earlier instances. The Holocaust Memorial Museum is more specific: Lemkin repeatedly stated that early exposure to newspaper reports of Ottoman crimes against Armenians was key to his thinking, and the museum locates the origin of the term and its codification in international law — the UN Genocide Convention of 1948 — in the mass murder of Armenians in 1915-16.

**On the dispute.** The Republic of Türkiye's Ministry of Foreign Affairs publishes an official position rejecting the characterisation. Its overview states that the final years of the empire were a tragic period in which Turks, Armenians and many others suffered immensely, and that "the Armenian view of history, however, selects the Armenian suffering, embellishes it in several ways and presents it as a genocide — a crime defined in international law — perpetrated by Turks against Armenians."

Those are the two positions, stated in each side's own words. They are not equivalent in evidentiary weight: the overwhelming weight of academic scholarship on the period, together with many governments and research institutions, uses the term genocide, and the documentary record the Holocaust Memorial Museum draws on includes contemporaneous documentation by outside observers such as Armin T. Wegner, a German medical orderly who photographed the deportations in 1915 and 1916. But a state's official position is a fact about the world, and reporting it with attribution is not the same as endorsing it.

## The end, and what replaced it

The empire did not survive the First World War. It was succeeded by the Republic of Türkiye, whose foreign ministry now dates the "Century of Türkiye" from the Republic's centenary.

That transition is often blurred into a single moment. The Ottoman state's defeat and dismemberment, the war that followed in Anatolia, the abolition of the sultanate and the founding of the Republic were distinct events over several years. The care about endings that [major eras of world history](/blog/major-eras-of-world-history/) applies to periodisation applies here too: an empire's end is usually a sequence, and picking one date always encodes an argument.
