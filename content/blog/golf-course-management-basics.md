**Most strokes are lost to trouble, not to poor striking.** A player who hits the ball adequately and never finds a penalty area scores better than one who strikes it beautifully and visits two per round. That asymmetry is the whole argument for course management.

## The centre of the green is usually the right target

The single highest-value habit in golf is aiming at the middle of the green rather than at the flag.

The reasoning is about the distribution of your shots rather than their average. Every player has a spread — a range of outcomes around where they aimed. Aiming at the centre means that spread lands mostly on putting surface. Aiming at a flag tucked near an edge means the same spread, unchanged, now includes bunkers, run-offs and thick rough.

You have not become a worse ball-striker. You have moved your distribution over worse ground.

| Target | Good shot | Average shot | Poor shot |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Centre of green | Birdie chance | Two putts | Long putt or fringe |
| Tucked flag | Birdie chance | Short-sided recovery | Penalty area or bunker |

The gain from attacking the flag shows up in the "good shot" column, which is the column you occupy least often. The cost shows up in the other two.

## Short-siding is the expensive miss

Being **short-sided** means missing the green on the side where the hole sits close to the edge, so there is almost no green between your ball and the target. It converts a routine up-and-down into a difficult one, and it is almost always a target-selection error rather than an execution error.

The practical rule: **when the flag is close to one side, miss to the other.** Aiming at the centre does this automatically, which is a large part of why the centre works.

## When aggression is priced correctly

Conservative play is not always right, and a player who never takes on a shot leaves scoring on the table. Two conditions have to hold together:

1. **The safe option cannot achieve what you need.** Trailing late in a tournament, a two-putt par does not change your position.
2. **A failure is affordable.** Early in a round, with a wide bail-out area and no penalty in play, a poor result costs a stroke rather than a card.

If only the first holds, you are gambling out of desperation. If only the second holds, you are taking on risk for a reward you did not need.

Where those conditions are met most often is on par 5s with a reachable green and a safe miss. Where they are met least often is on long par 3s over water — the classic hole that looks like an opportunity and is a trap.

## Playing the tournament, not the hole

The pricing changes across a tournament, and that context is the piece amateurs most often ignore.

- **Early rounds, before a cut:** failure is expensive because a missed cut is a lost week. Play the percentages. [How a golf cut works](/blog/how-a-golf-cut-works/) covers exactly why this asymmetry exists.
- **Middle of a round, in position:** ordinary pricing. Take the shots that are genuinely on.
- **Late, needing something:** conservative play has a ceiling that is too low. This is when aggression is correct even at poor odds.

## A working checklist

Before every shot, three questions:

1. **What is the worst realistic outcome from this line?** Not the worst possible — the worst *realistic*, meaning what a bad-but-normal version of this shot does.
2. **Can I afford that right now?** Given the round, the tournament and my position.
3. **Is there a target that keeps most of my spread out of trouble?**

If question two is "no", question three is not optional.
