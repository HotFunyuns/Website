There is one reliable test for whether a dish will batch, and it does not involve any equipment: **does it taste as good or better the day after you make it normally?**

If yes, it batches. If the appeal depends on something crisp, something freshly emulsified, or something delicate that was perfectly cooked thirty seconds before it reached the plate, no amount of container technology will save it.

What follows is the categories that hold up, the ones that do not, and the handling that decides which you end up with.

## Seven categories that reheat reliably

**1. Stews and braises.** The archetype. Everything has already been cooked slowly in liquid, so a second heating is more of the same process rather than a new insult. Beef, lamb, chicken thigh, root vegetables.

**2. Thick soups and broths.** Same logic, more liquid. Blended soups are particularly forgiving because there is no texture left to lose. Thin broths keep well too; anything with pasta or rice already in it does not, because the starch keeps swelling.

**3. Long-cooked sauces.** Tomato sauces, ragùs, curry bases. These genuinely improve, and they have the additional advantage of being a component rather than a meal — the same sauce becomes three different dinners depending on what you put it with.

**4. Pulses and beans.** Cooked from dried in a large pot, then portioned. Cheap, forgiving, and a base rather than a finished dish.

**5. Chillis and dals.** Spiced, thick, already integrated. These are in the same family as stews and benefit from the same resting effect.

**6. Roasted root vegetables**, with one caveat: they soften on reheating and never regain their edges. Fine if you are adding them to something. Disappointing if you wanted the roast.

**7. Grain bases kept plain.** Rice, barley, couscous, cooked and stored without dressing. Dressing the grain is a thirty-second job at the point of eating and keeps the batch versatile.

Notice the pattern: the successful categories are **components and integrated dishes**, not plated meals. That is the actual insight. Batch cooking works best when what you produce is an input rather than an output.

## Five that do not, and what to do instead

**Anything crisp or breaded.** Coatings go soft. Crisp skin goes leathery. Cook the component and crisp it fresh, or accept a different dish.

**Freshly emulsified sauces.** Hollandaise, mayonnaise-based dressings, butter-mounted pan sauces. These split, and reheating accelerates it. Make the base ahead, finish at the end.

**Delicate proteins.** White fish, scallops, anything with a narrow window between done and overdone. Reheating pushes it through the window.

**Salad leaves and most fresh herbs.** They collapse. Store separately and add at the end.

**Pasta cooked in its sauce.** It keeps absorbing liquid in the fridge. Cook the pasta fresh — it takes ten minutes — and batch the sauce, which was the slow part anyway.

The general principle: **batch the slow component, finish the fast one.** Almost every dish in the "does not batch" list has a slow part that batches beautifully.

## Cooling and storing, which is not optional

This is the part where guidance matters more than preference, so here is what the published guidance actually says.

The FDA's advice is that **leftovers need to be refrigerated or frozen within two hours**, and — addressing a widespread belief directly — that **putting hot food in the refrigerator does not harm the appliance**. To help hot food cool faster, it recommends **dividing leftovers into smaller containers** before refrigerating them.

Alongside that: keep the refrigerator at or below **40°F (4°C)** and the freezer at **0°F (-18°C)**, check those temperatures periodically with an appliance thermometer rather than trusting the dial, store refrigerated food in **covered containers or sealed storage bags**, and **check leftovers daily for spoilage**. Bacteria that cause foodborne illness can double in number roughly every twenty minutes at room temperature, which is why the two-hour window is the number it is.

MedlinePlus summarises the same territory as four steps — **clean, separate, cook, chill** — with "chill" meaning fresh food goes in the refrigerator promptly.

For how long any specific batch-cooked food keeps, check current official guidance for that food rather than a rule of thumb from an article. It varies by dish, and the honest answer is that we are not in a position to give you a number for your stew.

The practical upshot for batch cooking is neat: **the safety advice and the convenience advice are the same advice.** Dividing into small containers cools food faster *and* gives you portions.

## Portion before it goes in, not after

This is the habit that separates batch cooking that works from batch cooking that gets abandoned.

Portioning at the moment of cooking is calm, unhurried and takes two minutes. Portioning later means standing in front of one large container while hungry, making a judgement about how much is a serving, and being wrong — the first two servings come out generous and the last one is a disappointment.

It also means each container cools faster, which is the point above.

Use containers you would actually eat from, label them with what they are and when they went in, and put the oldest at the front. The labelling sounds fussy for about two weeks and then becomes the reason you stop discovering unidentified tubs.

## Where batch cooking starts creating waste

The failure mode is not spoilage. It is **repetition fatigue**.

Eight portions of one dish assumes an appetite for that dish eight times. By portion five, most people have lost interest, and the last three get pushed to the back and eventually thrown out. The batch that was supposed to reduce waste created it.

The fix is variety over volume: **two dishes at four portions each**, not one at eight. The cooking effort is barely different — much of it is heat-and-wait — and the proportion actually eaten is far higher.

The second fix is to batch components rather than meals, for the reason given above: a sauce becomes three dinners, a stew stays a stew.

## How scaling and portions interact in the planner

[Easy Recipes & Meal Planner](/apps/easy-recipes-meal-planner/) stores recipes at a one-person serving and scales up, with ingredient quantities and available nutrition adjusting to the serving size you choose. For batch cooking that base is useful, because a batch is just a serving count: choosing six means the ingredient list is already a shopping list for six portions.

The design detail that matters here is the **rounding**. Scaling a one-serving recipe by six produces exact quantities that are not kitchen quantities — fractions of teaspoons, weights to the odd gram. Those get rounded to amounts you can actually measure, which keeps a scaled ingredient list readable and keeps a batch dividing evenly into whole containers rather than leaving an awkward remainder.

The trade-off is a small rounding error that accumulates slightly across a long list, and where nutrition figures are shown they follow the same scaling. We think a batch that divides into six equal containers beats one that is arithmetically perfect and portions into five and a bit. The parts of scaling that are genuinely not linear — cooking time, seasoning, pan size — are covered in [why doubling a recipe is not just doubling](/blog/recipe-scaling-and-serving-sizes/).

## Fitting it into a week

Batch cooking is a tactic inside planning rather than a replacement for it. Two or three components cooked on one day, slotted into a week that also has simple nights and gaps in it, is the version that survives — [how to meal plan for the week](/blog/how-to-meal-plan-for-the-week/) covers the structure, and building the batch around what is already in the cupboard is the cheapest possible version, covered in [pantry inventory and grocery lists](/blog/pantry-inventory-and-grocery-lists/).
