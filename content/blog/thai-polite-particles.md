Two of the first words any Thai learner meets do not translate at all. *Khrap* and *kha* add politeness to the end of what you say to show respect to the listener. This guide covers who says which, the two forms of *kha*, where the particles go, and the casual particles that sit alongside them.

## What polite particles are

Wikipedia describes Thai particles as **often untranslatable words added to the end of a sentence** to show respect, a request, encouragement or another mood, much as intonation does in English, and to vary formality. Its article on Thai honorifics adds that they are added to the end of an utterance or clause **to show respect to the addressee**, and that they are **not used in written language** or alongside the royal and formal registers.

## Who says which

The choice follows **the speaker**, not the listener. Wikipedia gives *khrap* as the particle used **when the speaker is a man** and *kha* **when the speaker is a woman**.

| Particle | Tone | Speaker | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ครับ khrap | high | men | statements, requests and questions |
| ค่ะ kha | falling | women | statements and requests |
| คะ kha | high | women | questions |

The table follows Wikipedia's summary of common polite particles in its Thai honorifics article.

## The two forms of kha

Women's *kha* changes with the sentence. **ค่ะ**, with a falling tone, ends statements; **คะ**, with a high tone, ends questions. Wikipedia's Thai language article also puts requests with high-tone คะ, while its honorifics table and Wiktionary place requests with ค่ะ, so treat questions as the clear case. *Khrap* does not change.

## Where they go

At the end. The greeting **sawatdi** (สวัสดี), Wikipedia's article on the wai notes, is usually followed by *kha* from a woman and *khrap* from a man: **สวัสดีครับ** and **สวัสดีค่ะ**. The particle closes the sentence, after everything else.

## A polite yes

On their own, the particles do more work. Wiktionary describes ครับ as used by men **to express affirmation or assent**, and ค่ะ as used **to express affirmation, agreement or acknowledgement**. So a lone *khrap* or *kha* can mean "yes", "I see" or "understood". Wiktionary's example, from a Thai Bible translation, answers "Are you really my son?" with **ใช่ครับ**: "Yes, I am."

## A worked example: buying something

A man asks a shopkeeper, a woman, for a price:

| Speaker | Thai | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer | สวัสดีครับ | Hello |
| Shopkeeper | สวัสดีค่ะ | Hello |
| Customer | เท่าไรครับ | How much? |
| Shopkeeper | ร้อยบาทค่ะ | One hundred baht |

If the customer were a woman, her question would end in high-tone **คะ**: เท่าไรคะ. Her other lines would take falling-tone ค่ะ. [Thai numbers](/blog/thai-numbers/) covers the prices.

## Particles on everyday phrases

The same pattern makes any short phrase polite. **ขอบคุณครับ** or **ขอบคุณค่ะ** is "thank you", and **ขอโทษครับ** or **ขอโทษค่ะ** is "sorry" or "excuse me". Wiktionary adds a nuance worth knowing: plain ขอบคุณ and ขอโทษ suit people of equal or lower status, and more formal versions, such as ขอบพระคุณ, exist for thanking someone senior. The particle adds politeness; the word choice adds formality.

## Where the particles come from

Wiktionary traces ครับ to **ขอรับ**, roughly "I am willing to receive (your command)", and ค่ะ to **ข้า**, "servant" or "I", in the phrase เจ้าข้า, "my lord". It notes that ค่ะ was **formerly used by noblemen** and is now often employed by women. Wikipedia's honorifics article dates polite particles as a type of honorific to the **Bangkok period**. The older forms survive at the formal end: ขอรับ for men and เจ้าขา for women, both listed for addressing a superior in formal or royal settings.

## The friendlier particles

Wikipedia lists other common particles that change mood rather than politeness:

- **นะ na**: softens a sentence, makes a request or sounds friendlier.
- **จ้ะ, จ้า, จ๋า cha**: emphasis in less formal speech, with friends or someone younger.
- **สิ si**: emphasis or an instruction; Wikipedia notes it can come across as ordering someone to do something.
- **ละ, ล่ะ la**: emphasis.

## Three particle slips

- **Matching the listener's gender.** The particle reflects the speaker.
- **Using คะ for statements.** High-tone คะ is for questions.
- **Writing them in formal Thai.** Wikipedia notes they are not used in elegant, written Thai.
