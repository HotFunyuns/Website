Most of the words on a slot screen are never defined anywhere the player can see them. A machine announces a roaming wild, a breakable layer or a Grand meter with an animation and a sound, and the player is left to infer the rule from what happened. This is a glossary for that problem: what each mechanic is, what it changes, and what it costs elsewhere.

One principle runs underneath all of it, so it is worth stating before the list. A machine's designed return is fixed over its full cycle of outcomes before the mechanics are chosen. Every feature that creates extra paying combinations is therefore paid for somewhere else in the paytable. Features do not add return; they redistribute it.

## The structural terms: reels, positions and paylines

A **reel** is a column of symbol positions. A **reel layout** is the arrangement of those columns — how many, and how many positions each holds. The layout sets the total number of possible outcomes, and therefore the granularity available to the designer when building a paytable.

A **paytable** is the part of the machine's program that holds its characteristics, and Pennsylvania's regulatory definitions make the scope explicit: it contains the theoretical payout percentage, the reel strips and the awards. That is worth noticing, because it means the reel strips — the ordered lists of symbols that can appear in each column — are part of the same object as the awards. Changing which symbols are available and how often is the same kind of act as changing what they pay.

A **payline** is a pattern of positions across the reels that the game evaluates as a unit. A **ways-to-win** layout dispenses with fixed patterns and pays for matching symbols in adjacent columns regardless of row. Neither is more generous by nature; they are different ways of counting the same outcome space.

## Wilds, and the four things people mean by the word

A **wild** is a substitution rule. When a wild occupies a position, the game evaluates the line as though that position held whichever symbol would complete a paying combination. That is the entire mechanic. Every named variant differs only in placement and persistence.

- A **roaming wild** changes position between spins or between steps of a cascade, so its contribution moves around the board rather than sitting in one column.
- An **expanding wild** grows from a single position to cover a full reel or column, which multiplies the number of lines it can complete at once.
- A **sticky wild** holds its position while other positions re-spin, usually for a fixed number of spins.
- A **stacked wild** is simply several wild positions adjacent on one reel strip, which is a reel-strip property rather than a separate behaviour.

The interaction between wilds and other mechanics is where designs get genuinely intricate. A patent granted to International Game Technology for cascading symbols with wild features describes a cascade in which, once winning symbols are removed and the board has shifted, the machine can generate designated wild symbols into the empty positions when a triggering event has occurred — drawn from a pool described as independent of and in addition to the ordinary symbol set. The significant detail is that the wilds in that design are unavailable during normal play and exist only as a consequence of the cascade. A mechanic can be gated behind another mechanic.

## Cascades: one spin, several evaluations

**Cascading symbols** (also called tumbling or avalanche reels) convert a single spin into a chain. The sequence is: evaluate the board, pay any combinations, remove the symbols that formed them, drop the remaining symbols into the gaps, fill the top with new symbols, and evaluate again. The chain ends when an evaluation finds nothing.

Two consequences follow, and they pull in opposite directions. A cascade raises the number of paying events per spin, which makes a session feel busier. But because the designed return is fixed, the individual awards must be smaller to compensate. A cascading machine and a single-evaluation machine with the same return figure are trading frequency against size — which is exactly the trade covered in [why one machine pays small and often while another almost never pays](/blog/slot-volatility-explained/).

**Breakable layers** and **split boards** belong to the same family of board-state mechanics: instead of resolving one static grid, the machine carries state across evaluations, whether by clearing an obstruction or by treating parts of the board as separate regions. Our own app uses both terms, and the general principle is the one above — a board with memory produces chains, and chains change the distribution rather than the average.

## Triggers, features and what the base game gives up

A **scatter** or trigger symbol is one evaluated by count rather than by position, so three of them anywhere can start something even though they form no line. What they start is a **feature** or **free games** round: a bounded sequence played under modified rules.

Here is the part that is rarely explained. A designer can allocate a share of the machine's designed return specifically to the feature. When they do, the base game necessarily returns less than the headline figure, and the machine will feel drier than its number suggests until a feature lands. Researchers who obtained manufacturers' design documents through freedom-of-information requests catalogued exactly this kind of structural choice, alongside near misses and the related problem of wins that are smaller than the amount staked but presented with the full apparatus of a win. Those are design decisions with observable psychological consequences, and naming them is more useful than pretending the reels are neutral.

## Meters, and what a jackpot display actually is

**Mini, Major and Grand** meters are an accumulation display: a running figure that increases with play and resets when it is awarded. Three tiers exist so that the shortest tier can land often enough to be seen while the longest tier remains rare.
