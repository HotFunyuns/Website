**The differences between plant and animal protein are in amino acid profile, density per calorie, and what else arrives in the package.** Not in whether one "works" and the other does not. Framing it as a quality contest obscures the differences that actually affect a person trying to eat a certain way.

## The three real differences

### 1. Amino acid profile

Most animal-derived proteins contain all nine essential amino acids in useful proportions. Most single plant foods are lower in at least one — legumes in one, grains typically in a different one.

A varied plant-based diet across a day generally covers this. The older advice to combine complementary sources *within a meal* has been relaxed in mainstream guidance. [Complete and incomplete proteins explained](/blog/complete-vs-incomplete-proteins/) covers the mechanism in detail.

### 2. Density per calorie

This is the difference that matters most in daily practice and gets discussed least.

Lean animal proteins tend to deliver a lot of protein for relatively few calories. Most plant sources carry protein alongside carbohydrate or fat, so the same protein arrives with more energy attached.

The practical effect: **someone aiming at a high protein target on a plant-based diet usually has to eat more food and more calories to get there.** That is a logistics problem, not a nutritional deficiency, and it is solvable — but it is real, and pretending otherwise sets people up to fail.

Per-food figures rather than category generalisations are available in [USDA FoodData Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/), which is the right reference for "how much protein is actually in this".

### 3. What comes with it

| | Typically arrives with | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plant sources | Fibre, carbohydrate, various micronutrients | Higher-fibre patterns; people managing saturated fat |
| Animal sources | Fat, some micronutrients found less readily in plants | Lower-carbohydrate patterns; hitting high protein targets efficiently |

Neither package is universally better. They suit different goals, and for someone on a low-carbohydrate pattern in particular the carbohydrate that comes with many plant proteins is a real constraint — [net carbs explained](/blog/net-carbs-explained-keto-tracking/) covers why that arithmetic matters on that kind of diet.

## The digestibility question

Some analyses suggest plant proteins are somewhat less digestible than animal proteins, and that plant-based eaters might therefore aim for a modestly higher total intake.

We are flagging this rather than resolving it. Recommendations differ between sources, the measurement methods have known limitations, and the size of any adjustment is debated. It is a reasonable thing to raise with a registered dietitian if you are eating entirely plant-based and have a specific protein target.

## What this means for tracking

**Track grams, not sources.** Your tracker counts protein. That is the actionable number.

**Expect plant-based days to need more planning.** Not more virtue — more planning. Density is the constraint, and hitting a target usually means deliberately including a dense source rather than hoping the total assembles itself.

**Watch the whole macro picture, not just protein.** If plant protein is arriving with substantial carbohydrate, and carbohydrate is something you are managing, the protein target and the carbohydrate target pull against each other. That tension is worth seeing in the numbers rather than discovering at the end of the day.

**Variety handles the amino acid question.** For anyone eating a range of protein sources, it is not something to manage actively.
