Add the same amount to both numbers. The difference does not change, and the borrowing disappears. For 803 − 467, add 33 to each: **836 − 500 = 336**. There is no correction to apply afterwards and no digit to carry, because the pair moved together.

## The rule: move both numbers, not one

Pick the amount that makes the number you are taking away land on a round figure, then add it to both.

- **614 − 298.** Add 2 to each: 616 − 300 = **316**.
- **5,002 − 1,876.** Add 124 to each: 5,126 − 2,000 = **3,126**.
- **9,000 − 4,357.** Add 643 to each: 9,643 − 5,000 = **4,643**.
- **452 − 189.** Add 11 to each: 463 − 200 = **263**.

In every case the second number becomes something you can subtract by inspection, and the first number absorbs an addition that never requires a carry past the digit you are working on.

The amount to add is always the complement of the smaller number to the next benchmark: 1,000 − 876 = 124, 5,000 − 4,357 = 643, 200 − 189 = 11. So the method costs one complement and one easy subtraction, and the complement is a fact you want fluent anyway.

## Why the difference does not change

A subtraction measures the gap between two positions on the number line. Slide both markers 33 to the right and the gap between them is untouched — you have translated the whole picture, not stretched it.

The algebra is one line:

(a + k) − (b + k) = a + k − b − k = a − b

The two *k* terms cancel, for every value of *k*, positive or negative. That is why no correction step exists. It is also the precise difference between this method and rounding just one number, where the *k* does not cancel and has to be paid back at the end.

Worth seeing the contrast concretely. Take 803 − 467 and add 33 only to the 467: 803 − 500 = 303, which is 33 short. Add the 33 back and you reach 336 — the same answer, by a route with an extra step and an extra chance to push the correction the wrong way. [The compensation strategy](/blog/compensation-strategy-in-arithmetic/) is worth having for cases where you cannot conveniently move both numbers, but when you can, moving both is strictly cheaper.

## Running the identity backwards

Subtracting the same amount from both numbers is the same rule with a negative *k*, and it is the better move when the *larger* number is the awkward one.

- **712 − 305.** Subtract 5 from each: 707 − 300 = **407**.
- **5,000 − 1,876.** Subtract 1 from each: 4,999 − 1,875 = **3,124**. Every digit of 4,999 is large enough that no borrow can arise anywhere along the number.

That second example is the one worth remembering. Any subtraction from a string of zeros becomes trivial after taking one off both numbers, because 999…9 cannot borrow. It converts the single most borrow-heavy shape in arithmetic into the single least.

## Choosing between the two directions

Ask which number is closer to a benchmark and move the pair so that number lands on it.

- If the number being taken away is just below a round figure — 298, 467, 1,876 — add.
- If the number you are starting from is just above one — 712, 5,000, 1,003 — subtract.
- If both are awkward, this method has nothing to offer, and counting the gap in hops is usually the better route. [Counting up as a subtraction strategy](/blog/counting-up-subtraction/) covers that case, and gives an independent way to check any answer produced here.

## The mistake that turns this into a different method

Sliding the numbers in opposite directions destroys the difference rather than preserving it. Adding 33 to 803 while subtracting 33 from 467 gives 836 − 434 = 402, which is 66 away from the correct 336 — the two changes did not cancel, they doubled.

The check is the same one that protects every mental subtraction: estimate first. 803 − 467 is roughly 800 − 470, so the answer sits somewhere near 330. An answer of 402 fails that test immediately, and an answer of 303 fails it by a smaller but still visible margin.

A second habit worth building is simply noticing when a written method is being used out of reflex. NRICH's *Subtraction Surprise* task makes the point neatly in passing: in the worked video the presenter uses column subtraction for all three calculations even though one of them had a quicker mental route, which the task flags as a discussion point about choosing a method rather than defaulting to one.

## What the research does and does not settle

The equal-addition idea has a long history as a *taught written algorithm*, in competition with decomposition — the familiar crossing-out-and-borrowing method. A comparison published in *Arithmetic Teacher* in 1979 tested the two approaches on subtraction problems requiring regrouping and reported results favouring decomposition.

That is a real finding and it is worth stating, but it is narrow. It concerns which algorithm to teach children for written computation, measured by accuracy on written problems. It says nothing about whether an adult who already has a written method should add a mental shortcut for the specific shape where the smaller number sits just below a round figure. Those are different questions, and the honest position is that the classroom evidence points one way about instruction while leaving the mental-shortcut case untouched.

England's national curriculum takes the same practical line for adults' purposes: mental methods are expected alongside written ones, with pupils drawing on known facts to calculate mentally, rather than treating the column algorithm as the only legitimate route.
