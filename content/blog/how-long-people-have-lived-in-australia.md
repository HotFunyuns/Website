The most widely cited archaeological figure is around 65,000 years, from excavations at Madjedbebe in northern Australia published in *Nature* in 2017. The authors of that paper present it as a minimum age rather than a final answer, and open by stating that the time of arrival of people in Australia is an unresolved question. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have lived on the continent continuously ever since, and that continuity — unbroken occupation, language, law and connection to Country through to the present — is not in dispute in any part of the literature.

## What the excavated sites show

**Madjedbebe.** The 2017 report describes artefacts found in primary depositional context, concentrated in three dense bands, with the integrity of the deposit demonstrated by refitting broken artefacts back together and by optical dating and other analyses of the sediments. Occupation began around 65,000 years ago, associated with a distinctive stone tool assemblage: grinding stones, ground ochres, reflective additives and ground-edge hatchet heads. The authors frame the result as setting a new minimum age for human arrival in Australia and, by extension, for the dispersal of modern humans out of Africa.

**Lake Mungo.** In the Willandra Lakes region of New South Wales, the Mungo remains include what the published literature describes as the world's oldest ritual ochre burial and the first recorded cremation. Their dating history is a useful lesson on its own. Before the 2003 study, the burial known as Mungo III had been variously estimated at 30,000 years, at 42,000–45,000 years, and at 62,000 ± 6,000 years; radiocarbon estimates had placed the Mungo I cremation near 20,000–26,000 years. A new series of 25 optical ages published in *Nature* concluded that both burials occurred at 40,000 ± 2,000 years ago and that people were present at Lake Mungo by 50,000–46,000 years ago. The paper records that the work was carried out with the support of the three traditional tribal groups of the Willandra Lakes World Heritage Area.

That is a spread of more than thirty thousand years across successive published estimates for one site. Anyone quoting a single number for Australian occupation should know that this is what the underlying literature looks like.

## Why optical dating changed the picture

Radiocarbon dates organic material. Optically stimulated luminescence dates the sediment itself.

Mineral grains such as quartz accumulate a trapped-charge signal from low-level background radiation while they are buried. Exposure to daylight drains that signal to zero. So if a grain is buried in the dark, the signal starts building again from the moment of burial, and measuring how much has accumulated gives an estimate of how long ago that grain last saw the sun.

Two features matter for archaeology. It dates layers rather than objects, so a site with no surviving bone or charcoal can still be dated. And in single-grain form it can detect mixing: if grains within one apparent layer return wildly different ages, the deposit has been disturbed, and the excavator can say so rather than averaging the problem away. The Madjedbebe chronology rests on a statistical model of single-grain optical ages for exactly this reason.

The trade-off is that it dates burial, not human activity. Connecting the two is a separate inferential step, which is why the artefact refits and stratigraphic checks in that paper carry as much weight as the ages themselves. [How historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) covers that gap between what a method measures and what a claim asserts, across methods.

## Why institutional figures differ

UNESCO's World Heritage inscription for Kakadu National Park describes the reserve as having been inhabited continuously for more than 40,000 years, with cave paintings, rock carvings and archaeological sites recording the skills and way of life of the region's inhabitants. Parks Australia's own page for the same park, written in the voice of the Traditional Owners, states that Kakadu is a living cultural landscape and has been home to Aboriginal people for more than 65,000 years, and identifies the Bininj people of the park's north and the Mungguy people of its south.

Both are official. They differ because they were written decades apart, and the archaeological figure moved in between. This is a good habit to carry generally: when two authoritative sources disagree about a prehistoric date, check the vintage of each document before concluding that anyone is wrong.

## Continuity, oral tradition and the Budj Bim case

The Budj Bim Cultural Landscape, in the traditional Country of the Gunditjmara people in south-eastern Australia, is inscribed by UNESCO for containing one of the world's most extensive and oldest aquaculture systems: channels, weirs and dams built into the lava flows to trap, store and harvest kooyang, the short-finned eel. The inscription states that this system provided an economic and social base for Gunditjmara society for six millennia.

It also does something unusual for a heritage document. It records that the landscape is the result of a creational process narrated by the Gunditjmara as a deep time story, referring to the idea that they have always lived there, and then states separately that from an archaeological perspective deep time represents a period of at least 32,000 years. The two framings are set side by side rather than one being translated into the other, and the ongoing relationship between the Gunditjmara and their land is described as carried by knowledge systems retained through oral transmission and continuity of cultural practice.

That is the model worth copying. Oral tradition and excavation are different kinds of evidence, produced under different conditions, and the honest presentation names both rather than ranking them.

## What the dates do not decide

Chronology answers a narrow question. It does not settle the ones that follow from it.

British colonisation, which UNESCO dates to the establishment of penal settlements across Australia in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — around 166,000 people were transported there between 1787 and 1868 — dispossessed Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples of land held under their own law, through frontier violence, forced removal from Country and the taking of children. The consequences of that dispossession are present rather than historical.

The Uluru Statement from the Heart, issued by the 2017 National Constitutional Convention and described by its authors as the largest consensus of First Nations peoples on a proposal for substantive recognition in Australian history, states the position directly: that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander tribes were the first sovereign nations of the continent, possessing it under their own laws and customs "according to science more than 60,000 years ago", and that this sovereignty "has never been ceded or extinguished". Whatever view a reader takes of the constitutional reforms the Statement calls for, that is the position held, stated by the people holding it. It is not a question archaeology can answer, and a date for first occupation is not evidence for or against any of it.
