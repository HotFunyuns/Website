A categorical syllogism is an argument with two premises and a conclusion, each of which says something about how two groups overlap. There are only four ways a categorical statement can be built, and validity can be settled mechanically by drawing circles — which means this is one of the few reasoning tasks where you never have to rely on whether an argument *sounds* right.

The four forms carry traditional labels. Writing S for the subject group and P for the predicate group, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on the traditional square of opposition sets them out as follows.

| Label | Form | Name | What it asserts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | Every S is P | Universal affirmative | The whole of S sits inside P |
| **E** | No S is P | Universal negative | S and P do not overlap at all |
| **I** | Some S is P | Particular affirmative | At least one thing is both |
| **O** | Some S is not P | Particular negative | At least one S is outside P |

"Some" in this tradition means *at least one*, not *some but not all*. That single convention removes a large fraction of the confusion people bring to the subject, because ordinary English usually implies the second meaning.

## The square of opposition, and the free moves it gives you

Arrange the four forms in a square and three relationships appear, each of which is a shortcut you can use without drawing anything.

**Contradictories.** A and O cannot both be true and cannot both be false; the same holds for E and I. So if you learn that "every S is P" is false, you immediately have "some S is not P", and vice versa. This is the most useful of the three, because negating a universal is something people routinely get wrong: the denial of "all swans are white" is not "no swans are white" but "at least one swan is not white".

**Contraries.** A and E cannot both be true, though they can both be false. "Every S is P" and "no S is P" cannot hold together, but if S is split down the middle, neither holds.

**Subalternation.** If A is true then I is true, and if E is true then O is true — on the traditional reading. That reading depends on affirmative statements carrying existential import, which the entry describes as the traditional position: affirmatives required their subjects to have instances, negatives did not. Modern treatments generally drop that assumption, which is why "all unicorns are white" does not license "some unicorn is white" in a contemporary logic course. Neither convention is wrong; they answer different questions, and the only error is switching between them mid-argument.

## Drawing it: the method that settles validity

Three circles, one per term, overlapping so that every combination of memberships has its own region. Then two rules:

- A **universal** premise is drawn by **shading** the region it says is empty. "No S is P" shades the whole overlap of S and P. "Every S is P" shades the part of S that lies outside P.
- A **particular** premise is drawn by placing a **mark** in a region it says is occupied.

The practical rule is to draw every universal premise first. Shading frequently removes all but one of the regions where a mark could go, which settles where the mark belongs without guesswork. If you place the mark first, you often find you have to move it, and moving it is where mistakes creep in.

Then read the diagram. If the conclusion is already true of every diagram consistent with the premises, the argument is valid. If you can draw a consistent diagram in which the conclusion fails, it is not.

## Four valid arguments, worked

> **1.** Every member of the choir is a volunteer. Every volunteer is insured. Therefore every member of the choir is insured.

Two universals. Shading puts the choir circle inside volunteers and volunteers inside insured, so the choir circle ends up inside insured. Valid.

> **2.** No member of the choir is under sixteen. Every soprano is a member of the choir. Therefore no soprano is under sixteen.

Shade the overlap of choir and under-sixteens, then place sopranos inside choir. The soprano circle now lies entirely in shaded-free territory outside under-sixteens. Valid.

> **3.** Every cellist is a string player. Some volunteers are cellists. Therefore some volunteers are string players.

Universal first: shade the part of the cellist circle outside string players. Now the mark for "some volunteers are cellists" has only one legal home — inside cellists *and* inside string players. The conclusion is forced. Valid.

> **4.** No cellist is a drummer. Some volunteers are cellists. Therefore some volunteers are not drummers.

Shade the cellist–drummer overlap, then place the mark in the volunteer–cellist region, which is now necessarily outside drummers. Valid.

Notice how much work the ordering did in the third and fourth arguments. That is the whole technique.

## Three failures that feel valid

> **Undistributed middle.** Every accountant is numerate. Every actuary is numerate. Therefore every accountant is an actuary.

Draw a large "numerate" circle and put two separate smaller circles inside it. Both premises are satisfied and the conclusion is false. The shared term is the *larger* category in both premises, so it never connects the two groups.

> **Converting an A statement.** Every poodle is a dog, therefore every dog is a poodle.

The A form is not symmetric. E and I are — "no S is P" and "no P is S" say the same thing, as do the two I forms — but A and O are not, and treating them as if they were is a common slip in longer arguments.

> **Two particular premises.** Some cyclists are commuters. Some commuters are lawyers. Therefore some cyclists are lawyers.

Two marks, placed independently, with nothing forcing them into the same region. No conclusion follows from two particular premises, ever.

## Why a believable conclusion feels like a valid one

There is a persistent interference between content and structure here. A meta-analysis by Dries Trippas and colleagues, published in *Psychonomic Bulletin and Review* in 2018, pooled 22 studies covering 993 participants and reported that syllogisms with believable conclusions are endorsed more often than structurally identical ones with unbelievable conclusions. Their analysis attributed the effect to a shift in people's general willingness to endorse arguments rather than to a change in their ability to tell valid from invalid.

The countermeasure is the diagram. Circles are indifferent to whether a conclusion is agreeable, which is the point of using them on any argument where you have a stake in the answer. Note also that validity says nothing about whether the premises are true; where those premises come from is a separate question, and our guide to [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) covers how to weigh them.
