Greeting someone in Cantonese is mostly about picking the phrase that fits the time of day and the favour involved. There is a textbook hello, a separate morning greeting, two different thank-yous and two goodbyes at different levels of formality. This guide gives each in traditional characters and Jyutping, with usage notes from Wiktionary. Each syllable's final digit is its tone number; [Jyutping explained](/blog/jyutping-explained/) shows how to read it, and [Cantonese tones explained](/blog/cantonese-tones-explained/) covers the sounds.

## Nei5 hou2 and asking how someone is

**你好** *nei5 hou2*, "hello", is one of the examples Wikipedia's Jyutping article uses. Wiktionary adds a useful note: it is often used as a polite way of **getting someone's attention**, much like "hello?" at a shop counter.

Wiktionary also lists **你好嗎** *nei5 hou2 maa3*, "how are you?", but its usage note calls it a literal translation of the English phrase that is not commonly used among native speakers, though sometimes said when asking about someone's health. It simply adds *maa3* to the hello; [Cantonese sentence-final particles](/blog/cantonese-sentence-final-particles/) explains how these small words change a sentence.

## Zou2 san4 in the morning, zou2 tau2 at night

**早晨** *zou2 san4* means "early morning" in general Chinese, but Wiktionary lists **"good morning"** as a Cantonese sense, with the example 王小姐，早晨, "Good morning, Miss Wong". Note the order there: name and title first, then the greeting.

At night, **早敨** *zou2 tau2*, literally "rest early", is "good night". Be careful with it. Wiktionary also records a rude sense, roughly "shut up" or "drop dead", using sleep as a metaphor for death. Said warmly at bedtime it is a good-night; snapped in an argument it is not.

## M4 goi1 or do1 ze6: choosing the thank-you

Cantonese has two common thank-yous, and Wiktionary is explicit about the difference:

| Phrase | Jyutping | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 唔該 | m4 goi1 | thanks for something you requested or a small favour; also "please" and "excuse me" |
| 多謝 | do1 ze6 | thanks for a gift, including patronage or applause; a bigger favour |

Wiktionary warns that using 唔該 to thank someone for a greater favour, such as a gift, **may be offensive**, and points to 多謝 instead. Its examples for 唔該 show all three jobs: 唔該，可唔可以要杯冰水呀？ "Excuse me, can I have a glass of ice water?"; 唔該你同我睇住啲嘢, "Please keep an eye on my stuff"; and 唔該嗮！ "Thank you very much!".

## Saying sorry: deoi3 m4 zyu6 and m4 hou2 ji3 si1

Wiktionary lists two Cantonese apologies:

- **對唔住** *deoi3 m4 zyu6*: "sorry; excuse me".
- **唔好意思** *m4 hou2 ji3 si1*: literally a feeling of embarrassment, and also "sorry; excuse me". Wiktionary gives *ji3 si3* as an alternative reading of the last syllable.

Both contain **唔** *m4*, which Wikipedia's Cantonese grammar article gives as the word for "not", written with Jyutping's syllabic *m*. The same article notes that verbs are negated by putting 唔 in front of them, and that 唔好 *m4 hou2* placed before a verb forms the negative imperative, "don't". The 唔好 at the start of 唔好意思 is not that "don't", though: Wiktionary treats 唔好意思 as one word meaning "embarrassed" or "sorry", so it is best learned whole.

## Goodbyes: zoi3 gin3, baai1 baai3 and the dim sum invitation

**再見** *zoi3 gin3* is labelled **polite** by Wiktionary: "goodbye; see you later", literally "see again". The informal one is **拜拜**, which Wiktionary describes as a phono-semantic match for English "bye-bye". Wiktionary gives its Cantonese reading as *baai3-1 baai3*, meaning the first syllable changes from tone 3 to tone 1, so it is said *baai1 baai3*. It also lists a Hong Kong Cantonese euphemistic sense, "to die".

A parting phrase worth recognising is **得閒飲茶！**, which Wiktionary translates as "Let's have dim sum if you're free!" and describes as a polite phrase similar to "You must come to dinner!" in English. It is friendly rather than a fixed appointment. [Dim sum in Cantonese](/blog/cantonese-dim-sum-phrases/) covers what happens if you do go.

## A worked example: one morning at a café

Here is a short exchange built only from the phrases above, with the reason for each choice:

1. **Arriving**: *zou2 san4*. It is morning, so the time-of-day greeting rather than *nei5 hou2*.
2. **Getting attention**: *m4 goi1*, the "excuse me" for something you are about to request.
3. **Bumping a chair**: *m4 hou2 ji3 si1*, "sorry; excuse me".
4. **Receiving your drink**: *m4 goi1* again, because you asked for it.
5. **A friend pays for you**: *do1 ze6*, because a treat is a bigger favour than a service.
6. **Leaving**: *baai1 baai3* to the friend, *zoi3 gin3* if the setting is more formal.

Six moments use six distinct phrases, and *m4 goi1* appears twice because it covers both "excuse me" and thanks for a service.
