The Meiji Restoration was the 1868 transfer of political authority in Japan from the Tokugawa shogunate to a government ruling in the emperor's name, followed by about two decades of deliberate institutional reconstruction. Columbia University's Asia for Educators puts the moment simply: "In 1868 the Tokugawa shôgun … lost his power and the emperor was restored to the supreme position." The word *restoration* describes how the change justified itself. It does not describe what the change did, which was to abolish the domains, dissolve the hereditary status order, conscript commoners into a national army and rebuild taxation in cash. That is why the same event is also called a revolution, and why both labels are defensible.

## What came before: the opening of the country

The Tokugawa order had governed Japan for roughly two and a half centuries under tightly restricted foreign contact. The National Diet Library's archival history of the period dates the disruption to Commodore Matthew Perry's arrival on 8 July 1853, carrying a letter from President Millard Fillmore seeking trade and friendship.

The library holds the documents that followed: the 1853 *Gasshukoku Shokan Wage*, a translation of the American letters, and a copy of the U.S.-Japan Treaty of Peace and Amity, negotiated for the shogunate by Hayashi Fukusai, signed in 1854 and ratified on 21 February 1855. That treaty opened the ports of Shimoda and Hakodate and ended the isolation policy.

Two things follow from this, and they need to stay distinct. Foreign pressure was the *precondition* — it exposed the shogunate's inability to defend the terms of its own order and split the political class over how to respond. It was not the *trigger* of 1868, which came out of a domestic political crisis among domains, court factions and the shogunate itself. Confusing the two produces the familiar and wrong story in which Perry's ships caused the Meiji state.

## The reform programme, in order

The sequence matters more than any single measure, because each step made the next one possible.

**The domains go first.** Asia for Educators records that "by July 1869 the feudal lords had been requested to give up their domains, and in 1871 these domains were abolished and transformed into prefectures of a unified central state." Until that happened there was no single fiscal or administrative territory to reform.

**Then the status order.** The samurai "lost their class privileges, when the government declared all classes to be equal", and "by 1876 the government banned the wearing of the samurai's swords". The sword ban is the image everyone remembers; the abolition of hereditary stipends was the part that actually dismantled the class.

**Then the army.** "A national army based on universal conscription was created in 1872, requiring three years' military service from all men, samurai and commoner alike." A conscript army is only possible once the domains no longer command their own retainers.

**Then the money.** A national land tax system "required payment in money instead of rice, which allowed the government to stabilize the national budget". This is the least dramatic item on the list and arguably the most important: a state with a predictable cash revenue can borrow, plan and build; a state paid in fluctuating quantities of grain cannot.

The new government's declared posture is quoted by MIT's Visualizing Cultures in two lines: "evil customs of the past shall be broken off" and "knowledge shall be sought throughout the world". Read alongside the measures above, they are a fair summary of the programme.

## Why a warrior elite abolished itself

This is the question that makes the period interesting, and it does not have a single tidy answer.

Part of it is competitive: the leading figures came from domains that had watched the Qing empire's treatment in the wars described in our article on [the Opium Wars and the treaties that followed](/blog/opium-wars-and-unequal-treaties/), and concluded that a state which could not defend itself would be dismantled by others. Part of it is factional: the reformers were not the whole samurai class but a particular cohort within it, and the losers resisted — sometimes in arms. Part of it is practical: hereditary stipends were a crushing fiscal burden on a new government that needed the money for railways, schools and arsenals.

Historians weigh these differently, and the disagreement is genuine rather than decorative. What should be resisted is the retrospective tidiness that treats the outcome as inevitable because it happened. The transformation was contested at every stage, and a great deal of it was improvised.

That is the normal shape of a large historical change: several pressures acting together, with the balance between them still argued over long afterwards. Our article on [what caused the Bronze Age collapse](/blog/what-caused-the-bronze-age-collapse/) works through the same problem at the other end of recorded history, and the reasoning transfers.

## The part that is usually separated out, and should not be

The Meiji state's institutions were built for capability, and capability was used.

Visualizing Cultures records the sequence plainly: Japan defeated China in 1894-95, defeated Russia in 1904-05, and by 1919 sat at Versailles among the Big Five alongside the United States, Britain, France and Italy. The conscript army of 1872 and the fiscal machinery of the land tax are the same institutions that made those wars possible. Japanese colonial rule in Taiwan and Korea, and the imperial expansion of the following decades, grew from this programme rather than despite it.

Saying so is not a verdict on the reforms. It is a refusal to tell the modernisation story and the expansion story as though they happened to different countries. The consequences of that expansion belong in their own articles, not as a footnote here.

## What to take away

**The name describes the justification, not the content.** Authority was restored to the emperor; almost everything else was replaced.

**The order of the reforms is the explanation.** Abolish the domains, then the status order, then raise a conscript army, then fund it with a cash land tax. Each step depends on the one before it.

**Modernisation and expansion are one programme.** The institutions that built schools and railways built the army, and an account that separates them is telling half a story.
