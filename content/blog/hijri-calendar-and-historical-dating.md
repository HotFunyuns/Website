A Hijri year runs about eleven days shorter than a Gregorian one, so almost every AH year begins in one Common Era year and ends in the next. That single fact means a Hijri date given only to the year cannot be converted to a single CE year at all — the honest answer is a span of two. Add three further points where reputable authorities legitimately differ, and you have a complete explanation for why two well-edited books can print different Common Era years for the same event.

## What the calendar is

The Islamic calendar is purely lunar, with twelve months and no intercalated extra month to keep it aligned with the solar year. The US Naval Observatory describes months as beginning when the thin crescent Moon is actually sighted in the western sky after sunset, a day or so after the new moon, and notes that for calendar purposes the sighting must be made with the unaided eye.

Years are counted from the Hijra — the migration of the Prophet Muhammad and his followers from Mecca to Medina — and are marked AH. The standard reference account of calendars credits the caliph ʿUmar I with establishing the era in AH 17, and notes that the form of the calendar as a lunar calendar without intercalation was laid down in the Qurʾan and in the Prophet's sermon at the Farewell Pilgrimage. The Islamic day begins at sunset of the previous day.

## Why one AH year maps onto two CE years

Twelve lunar months are, on average, eleven days shorter than the Gregorian civil year. So each Islamic year begins about eleven days earlier in the civil year than the one before, and the whole cycle regresses through the seasons over roughly 33 years.

The practical consequence for historical writing is immediate. If a chronicle says only "in AH 350", the corresponding Common Era period straddles a boundary, and a writer who picks one of the two years has made a silent choice. Some make it consistently, some make it silently, and different books make it differently.

## The four places a conversion can legitimately differ

**1. The epoch.** There are two accepted starting points, one day apart. The reference account of calendars records that 1 Muharram AH 1 is generally taken by astronomers as Thursday, 15 July 622 in the Julian calendar — the astronomical Hijra epoch — while chronological tables generally use Friday, 16 July 622, the civil epoch. The US Naval Observatory gives the civil value.

**2. Observed versus tabular months.** The religious calendar depends on sighting. Chronologists working on historical material generally use a tabular calendar instead: a regular scheme with a thirty-year cycle in which eleven years are leap years, odd-numbered months of thirty days and even-numbered months of twenty-nine, with an extra day added to the final month in a leap year. The tabular scheme is reproducible and can be a day away from what was actually observed at the time.

**3. Julian or Gregorian on the other side.** For dates before the Gregorian reform, "the CE equivalent" depends on which Western calendar is meant. That difference grows over time and is separate from anything to do with the Hijri calendar. [How calendars and dating systems work](/blog/how-calendars-and-dating-systems-work/) covers the reform and why a naive interval across it is wrong.

**4. How much of the date survives.** A full day-month-year Hijri date converts to a single day. A year alone converts to a two-year span. Sources rarely mark which situation they were in.

## The crescent problem is not a historical curiosity

Deciding when a lunar month begins is an active technical question, not a solved one.

The reference account of calendars puts it bluntly: because of weather, a new month may be declared thirty days after the start of the previous one, various predictive procedures for first visibility have always had an equivocal status, and there is disagreement among countries, religious leaders and scientists about whether to rely on observations, which are subject to error, or on calculations, which may rest on poor models.

The astronomy behind this continues to be refined. A study in *Experimental Astronomy* established a visibility criterion from 737 observations — almost half of them collected by the Islamic Crescent Observation Project — based on the topocentric arc of vision and the topocentric crescent width, covering both naked-eye and optically aided sightings, and reporting a Danjon limit of 6.4 degrees. That is a live measurement programme producing new criteria, which is exactly why historical month starts cannot simply be back-calculated with confidence.

## Where the dates come from in the first place

Many Islamic-world dates reach us through manuscripts that record their own completion, usually in the colophon at the end — the note in which a copyist states what was copied, by whom, and when. The colophon is a genuine object of scholarly study in its own right, examined in the *Journal of Islamic Manuscripts* among other venues, and digitised collections such as the Cambridge Digital Library's Islamic Manuscripts holdings let a reader look at the primary object rather than only at a catalogue entry.

This matters for dating because a colophon date is a date for the *copy*, not for the text. Treating one as the other is a common error, and it is the same category of mistake as reading a regnal date without knowing the accession convention. [How historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) covers the general version of that problem.

## A rough conversion, honestly labelled

Because the lunar year slips a full year against the solar year roughly every 33 years, a serviceable mental approximation is: take the AH year, subtract the AH year divided by 33, and add 622. The result is the Common Era year in which that Hijri year began, give or take one.

Use it for orientation. Do not use it for citation. For anything you intend to publish, convert from the full Hijri date with a tool that states its epoch and its calendar, and say in your text which convention you used.
