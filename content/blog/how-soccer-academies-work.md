**In many countries, a professional soccer (association football) career starts inside a structured youth programme — a club academy or a national performance programme — that a player joins young and moves through by being selected, year after year, until a contract decision is made.** There is no single route and no route with a guarantee: at each stage, coaches choose who stays, and the research on those choices shows they depend on far more than how good a player looks with the ball today.

This article explains how those pathways work and what studies have found about who is selected, how many reach a contract, and what happens to those who do not. It is not career advice, and nothing here can predict any individual's chances.

## How the pathway is structured

Countries organise youth development differently, but two British examples show the main models.

**Club academies.** In England, the [Premier League](https://www.premierleague.com/en/about) says its Elite Player Performance Plan (EPPP) was produced after consulting the Football League and The Football Association "to promote the development of a world-leading Academy system", with the aim of producing more, and better, home-grown players. Players move through age-group teams at a professional club, with reviews along the way, towards a decision on whether to offer a professional contract.

**National performance schools.** Scotland's football association runs [performance schools](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2025.2545700): a centrally driven talent-development system inside an educational setting, which the researchers who evaluated it describe as a novel concept in soccer.

Beyond Britain, selection philosophies vary. A 2026 [systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13045062/) of 38 studies found European systems generally emphasise data-driven precision and standardised procedures, while South American and African contexts more often prioritise spotting latent potential and performance in game-like conditions — though only a small share of the studies it reviewed came from the Americas or Africa.

## What selectors look for — and how that has changed

The same review traces talent-identification criteria through three stages:

| Period | What dominated selection |
| --- | --- |
| 1976–1999 | Body measurements and basic physical fitness |
| 2000–2015 | Technical performance metrics added to physiological testing |
| 2016–2024 | Multidimensional assessment — psychological attributes and social adaptability alongside physical, technical and physiological measures |

Its authors argue that good selection systems should "balance standardization with contextual sensitivity", rather than relying on measurement alone.

### What one academy's data showed

A study at an English academy by [Kelly and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9611883/) looked at 98 players from under-9 to under-16 over the 2014–2016 seasons, then at 18 under-18 players at the point of a professional-contract decision. Among the younger players, higher coach review ratings went with being further towards predicted adult height, older relative age within the year group, more match-play hours and specific technical skills such as passing and dribbling. At the under-18 decision, psychological characteristics — including coping with pressure and organising quality practice — stood out among the eight players offered contracts compared with the ten who were not.

The authors are clear about the limits: one academy, a small under-18 group, and the risk that a model trained on so little data memorises the sample rather than producing transferable lessons. Their conclusion is still worth knowing: the factors associated with progressing "are not always technical and tactical in nature". In plain terms, a younger player's ratings can reflect when they were born in the selection year and how early they matured, which is a reason for caution before reading too much into any one assessment.

## How many reach a contract?

The most direct figures come from the Scottish study by Dugdale and colleagues, published in the Journal of Sports Sciences. Of 604 players who had attended one of the seven performance schools, 338 (56.0%) achieved a professional contract after graduating, and 165 (27.3%) represented Scotland at age-group level at least once. The rates were consistent across years of entry and across the schools, and the authors conclude the findings support a centrally managed school-based programme.

Read those numbers carefully. They describe players who had already been selected into a national programme, in one country's system. They are not the chances facing young players in general, and they say nothing about how long the contracts lasted or what happened afterwards. No study of a selected group can tell an individual what will happen to them.

## Release: the part of the pathway nobody advertises

Every selection point also produces players who are let go. A 2022 study by [McGlinchey and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9354464/) interviewed four young men who had completed two-year scholarships at English academies and then been released. One summed it up as going "from everything to nothing in a split second". The players described the release meeting as traumatic and a sudden loss of identity, feeling sidelined before the decision, little aftercare from their clubs, and serious psychological distress in the months that followed; several held back from leaning on family for fear of being a burden. Over time, they also described growth — resilience, and a broader sense of life beyond soccer.

It is a small, in-depth study rather than a survey, but its recommendations are practical: build psychological skills before release decisions, involve previously released players as mentors, give tailored support to players released after injury, and formalise aftercare from clubs, including access to counselling. For families, it is a reminder that the support around a young player matters as much at the end of an academy pathway as at the start.

## Reading academy research without being misled

Three patterns recur in the studies above, and they matter more than any single number.

- **Selected samples.** The Scottish figures describe players a national programme had already chosen; an academy's under-18 group has survived years of earlier cuts. Results from a filtered group cannot be read back onto everyone who started.
- **Small numbers.** Kelly's contract-decision group had 18 players; McGlinchey's study interviewed four. Small studies can show what an experience is like or which factors deserve attention, not how common something is.
- **One place, one time.** Each study covers one system, academy or set of seasons. Selection criteria have shifted over the decades, as the systematic review shows, and they differ between regions.

None of this makes the research wrong. It defines what the research can say — and it is why a figure from a programme that had already picked its players should never be turned into a forecast for a child starting out.
