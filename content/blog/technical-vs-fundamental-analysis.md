Ask two analysts about the same stock and you may get answers that do not even address the same question. One talks about revenue growth, debt and competitors; the other about trend lines, moving averages and momentum. They are using the two main schools of stock analysis, which start from different assumptions and look at different evidence. StockCharts' ChartSchool describes each one, including where it falls short.

## Fundamental analysis: what is the business worth?

ChartSchool defines fundamental analysis as examining the underlying forces that affect the well-being of the economy, industry groups and companies. It often works **top-down**:

1. **The economy.** Growth, interest rates and inflation affect almost every company. ChartSchool's image: the economy is the tide and companies are boats.
2. **The industry.** Supply and demand for an industry's products, and how it is changing.
3. **The company.** Financial statements, management, the business concept and competition.

The raw material is the company's own reporting: the 10-K and 10-Q filings described in [how to read an earnings report](/blog/how-to-read-an-earnings-report/). FINRA's guide to evaluating stocks lists ratios commonly derived from them, including earnings per share, the [price-to-earnings ratio](/blog/pe-ratio-explained/), price-to-sales and debt-to-equity, and advises comparing them within an industry, because averages vary widely between industries.

The goal, in ChartSchool's description, is to estimate a stock's fair value; if fair value differs from the price, fundamental analysts consider the stock over- or undervalued and expect the price to move toward fair value over time. ChartSchool also notes that comparisons are relative: a telecom company is compared with other telecom companies.

## Technical analysis: what is the price doing?

Technical analysis studies the market's own record. ChartSchool describes its raw material as price data, any combination of the open, high, low, close and volume over a given timeframe, and notes it can be applied to anything traded where price is set by supply and demand.

Its toolkit includes [candlestick charts](/blog/how-to-read-candlestick-charts/), trend lines, [support and resistance](/blog/support-and-resistance-explained/) and indicators such as [moving averages](/blog/moving-averages-explained/) and the [RSI](/blog/rsi-indicator-explained/). A central belief, in ChartSchool's words, is that prices trend, although most technicians also acknowledge periods when they don't.

## Side by side

| | Fundamental analysis | Technical analysis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main question | What is the business worth? | What are price and volume doing? |
| Evidence | Financial statements, industry, economy | Price and volume history |
| Typical tools | Ratios, cash flow, competitive position | Charts, trend lines, indicators |
| Comparison | Company against peers and its own history | Price against its own past |
| Main weakness | Forecasts of future earnings can be wrong | Signals can lag or fail |

## The limits of each

**Fundamental analysis** depends on forecasts. A fair-value estimate is only as good as the assumptions about future earnings, and the market can disagree with an analyst for a long time. Accounting choices also shape the numbers; the 10-K's management discussion explains the key judgments.

**Technical analysis** has its own problems, which ChartSchool lists frankly. It has been criticised for being too late: by the time a trend is identified, a substantial portion of the move may have already taken place. And not all technical signals and patterns work; what works for one stock may not work for another. Indicators, being calculations from past prices, describe rather than predict.

## One company, two readings

Imagine a company whose latest annual report shows revenue up 15%, steady margins and falling debt, while its share price has drifted down for six months.

- **A fundamental reading** might conclude that the business is improving and ask whether the price now undervalues it, checking the [P/E ratio](/blog/pe-ratio-explained/) against competitors and reading the risk factors for anything the market might be worried about.
- **A technical reading** might note that the price is below its falling moving averages and has broken a previous support level, and describe the trend as down.

Both readings can be accurate at once. They answer different questions, and the disagreement between them is information in itself: it may mean the market expects something the reported numbers do not yet show, or simply that it has not caught up. Neither reading tells you which.

## Using both

ChartSchool argues that even committed fundamental analysts can learn from a price chart, because it is an easy-to-read history of how the market has treated a stock. Some investors use fundamentals to understand what they own and charts to see how the shares have traded. Combining the two adds perspectives, but it does not add certainty, and neither approach turns a stock into a sure thing.
