Memorising the times tables buys you capacity, not speed. A product you retrieve arrives with almost no cost; a product you work out occupies the same limited holding space that the rest of the calculation needs. That is the whole mechanism, and it explains why people who know their tables are better at *multi-step* arithmetic rather than merely faster at single facts.

## What recall actually buys

Take a two-digit multiplication done in a single pass, as in [vertically and crosswise multiplication](/blog/cross-multiplication-in-one-pass/). The middle column needs two products summed, plus an incoming carry, before you can write a digit. If both products are retrieved, the only thing being held is the carry. If either has to be derived — counting up in sevens, or splitting 7 × 8 into 7 × 4 doubled — that derivation occupies the space where the carry was sitting.

The research picture is consistent with that. A study of multi-step mental addition found that children with faster single-step performance were faster on multi-step problems, and that verbal working memory capacity predicted multi-step speed. The reading is not that single-step speed causes multi-step speed directly, but that fluent components leave room for the steps built on top of them.

The capacity side of this — what working memory is, how much of it there is, and what does and does not change it — is treated directly in [what working memory is, and whether training changes it](/blog/working-memory-and-training/), which is careful about the limits of the evidence.

## How many facts there really are

The grid looks intimidating and mostly is not. A twelve-by-twelve table has 144 cells, but multiplication is commutative, so 7 × 8 and 8 × 7 are one fact rather than two. NRICH's article on illustrating number properties with arrays makes this visible: a rectangle turned on its side has the same number of squares, which halves the quantity of facts requiring memorisation.

Counting properly for the 2-to-12 range, where order does not matter, gives 66 distinct products.

Now remove the ones that follow a rule rather than needing storage:

- Everything involving **2** is doubling.
- Everything involving **10** is a shift.
- Everything involving **5** is a shift and a halving, as covered in [multiplying by 5, 25 and 50 by dividing instead](/blog/multiplying-by-five-and-twenty-five/).
- Everything involving **11** has its own digit pattern.

Pairs touching 2, 5 or 10 account for 30 of the 66. Of the remaining 36, eight involve 11. What is left is **28 products** drawn from 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 12 — and even several of those are derivable, since 4 is double-double, 6 is double the threes, 8 is three doublings and 9 is a shift minus one lot.

Twenty-eight facts is a week's work, not a year's. The perception that the tables are an enormous body of material comes from counting the grid rather than counting the facts.

## Retrieval is an end point, not an instruction

You cannot decide to recall something. Research on how arithmetic facts develop describes a sequence: children begin with counting strategies, move to decomposition strategies that derive an answer from a related fact, and gradually shift to memory-based retrieval as repeated correct pairings of problem and answer build associations in long-term memory. Retrieval emerges from the earlier strategies rather than replacing them by instruction.

Two consequences follow for an adult relearning the tables.

First, a fact you currently derive is not a failure. It is the normal precursor, and deriving it *correctly and repeatedly* is what produces the recall. Deriving it wrongly is the problem, because the wrong pairing gets stored just as readily.

Second, speed drills applied too early rehearse the derivation. If 7 × 8 is still being reached by doubling 7 × 4, a clock trains you to double faster. Accuracy first, then speed — an ordering that holds for most arithmetic practice.

## A schedule an adult can keep

Nothing exotic is required, and the constraints are practical rather than theoretical.

- **Work in small sets.** Ten or twelve facts at a time, drawn from the 28 hard ones, is enough. A whole table at once mostly practises the ones you already know.
- **Self-test rather than re-read.** Cover the answer, attempt it, then check. Reading a table through feels productive and produces recognition rather than recall.
- **Mark the misses and return to them sooner.** A fact you missed should reappear within the same session and again the next day; a fact you got instantly can wait.
- **Five minutes most days beats an hour on Sunday.** Distributed practice is the one scheduling finding robust enough to act on without qualification.
- **Say the whole fact.** "Seven eights are fifty-six", not "fifty-six". The association being built runs from the question to the answer, so the question has to be present.

England's national curriculum sets the expectation that pupils have memorised the multiplication tables up to and including 12 by the end of Year 4, with a statutory check of 25 timed questions taken in a two-week window in June. Adults relearning the material have no such deadline, which is an advantage: the pressure that makes the school version stressful is optional here.

## Timed or untimed?

The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide on mathematics intervention in the elementary grades includes timed activities among its six recommendations as one way to build fluency, alongside systematic instruction, mathematical language, representations, number lines and word problems. That is a recommendation about building fluency once the material is known, not about how to introduce it.

The practical translation: untimed while a fact is being learned, timed once it is reliably correct. Timing has a real function — it makes derivation impractical and so pushes the answer toward retrieval — but only after the derivation is dependable.

It is also worth saying plainly that timed arithmetic makes some people anxious, and that anxiety costs working memory of its own. If a clock produces worse answers rather than faster ones, that is information about the practice design, not about the person. Untimed rounds are a legitimate permanent choice.

## What memorising the tables does not do

The benefit described above is specific and bounded: retrieved facts free capacity for the arithmetic built on them. That is what the cited research addresses, and it is all this article claims.

It does not follow that memorising tables raises intelligence, improves reasoning in general, or transfers to anything outside arithmetic. No evidence offered here supports that, and it should not be inferred. Practising a skill improves that skill; the further claim is a different claim, and it needs different evidence.
