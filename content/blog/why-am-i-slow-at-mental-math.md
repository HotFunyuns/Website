**"Slow at mental math" describes a symptom, not a cause.** At least five distinct things produce it, they are distinguishable from your own record of correct answers and answer times, and the fix for each one is different. Practising more without identifying which you have is the reason people plateau.

What follows is a way to read your own numbers, then five causes with a specific next step each. It is a self-diagnosis of a practice habit, not of a person — the closing section says where that boundary sits.

## Read two numbers, not one

A score total hides everything. What separates the causes is the *pair*: how accurate you are, and how long each answer took. Hold the problem type and difficulty constant while you compare, or you are comparing nothing.

The table below maps the four basic combinations of accuracy and speed onto the cause each one usually indicates.

| What you see | Most likely cause | First thing to change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fast answers, low accuracy | Committing before the calculation finishes | Remove the timer until accuracy is stable |
| Slow answers, high accuracy | Facts are being computed, not recalled | Drill the facts themselves, in small sets |
| Accuracy fine on one-step, poor on multi-step | Intermediate values are being dropped | Restructure the method to carry fewer values |
| One operation far worse than the others | A single weak area, not general slowness | Isolate that operation and drill it alone |

A fifth cause does not show up in this table at all, because it looks like the second one: using a method that is fine on paper and unsuitable in your head. It is covered below.

## Cause one: you are guessing under time pressure

Fast and wrong is a pacing problem, not a knowledge problem. Under a countdown, people commit to an answer before the calculation is finished, because submitting something feels better than watching the clock. The tell is that accuracy rises sharply the moment the timer is removed.

The fix is to train the two things separately. Run untimed sets until accuracy sits reliably high, then reintroduce time. The What Works Clearinghouse [practice guide on mathematics intervention](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/26) lists regular timed activities as one way to build fluency, which is worth taking seriously — but fluency means fast *and* correct, and a timer applied to an unreliable method simply produces errors sooner.

## Cause two: the facts are not automatic

Slow and accurate usually means you are reconstructing facts rather than retrieving them. If 7 × 8 arrives via 7 × 4 doubled, the answer is right and the route is long, and every such detour inside a larger problem multiplies the delay.

The fix is unglamorous: short, frequent drilling of a small set of facts until retrieval replaces reconstruction. The argument for why this matters beyond speed is in [the mental math methods guide](/blog/mental-math-training-guide/) — a recalled fact costs almost nothing to hold, while a reconstructed one occupies the same attention you need for the rest of the problem.

## Cause three: working-memory load on multi-step problems

If you are fine on single-step items and fall apart on anything requiring carrying, borrowing or partial products, the bottleneck is what you are holding, not what you know.

This is the part of the picture with the clearest research behind it. Han and colleagues, in [Memory Updating and Mental Arithmetic](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4735758/), found that a task requiring numerical updating predicted performance on multi-digit mental multiplication, and that the effect showed up on harder problems rather than easy ones — consistent with multiplication requiring accurate maintenance and updating of intermediate results. Cragg and colleagues, in [When is working memory important for arithmetic?](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5724815/), found something that complicates the usual story: a working-memory load slowed and degraded performance across retrieval, decomposition and counting to a similar extent, rather than sparing retrieval as had been expected.

The practical reading is that reducing what you carry helps regardless of which strategy you favour. Work left to right so the largest part of the answer is settled first and the rest is an adjustment; round to a friendly number and correct afterwards; and hold one running total rather than a list. Those moves are set out with worked examples in [mental math tricks that actually work](/blog/mental-math-tricks-that-work/). If holding values is the persistent problem, [what working memory is and what training does to it](/blog/working-memory-and-training/) is the honest account of what can and cannot be changed there.

## Cause four: one operation is dragging the average

People rarely turn out to be uniformly slow. Far more often, three operations are fine and one is not — division and subtraction with borrowing are the usual culprits — and the overall average makes the whole thing look worse than it is.

The fix is diagnosis by isolation. Run each operation separately, at the same difficulty, and compare. If one is clearly behind, the general practice you have been doing is spending most of its time on skills you already have. Division in particular has its own set of methods, covered in [how to divide in your head](/blog/mental-division-tricks/).

## Cause five: you are running a paper method in your head

The written algorithms are designed for a medium that remembers things for you. Column addition works right-to-left because the page holds the carries. Do the same thing mentally and you are storing digits in the one place you cannot afford to fill.

The symptom is being accurate but exhausted, and losing your place on anything with more than two parts. The fix is a change of method rather than more practice: adopt approaches built for a head rather than a page, which generally means working from the largest place value downwards, replacing subtraction with counting up, and using rounding with a correction. [Estimation techniques that make mental math useful](/blog/estimation-techniques-explained/) covers the checking side of the same shift.

## What this article is not

Everything above is about practice design. Two limits are worth stating plainly.

The first is that discomfort with numbers changes performance independently of skill, and a person who dreads the task will look slower than they are. That is a separate subject with its own research, and it is treated on its own terms in [math anxiety in adults](/blog/math-anxiety-in-adults/).

The second is that a difficulty which is persistent, severe and out of proportion to the rest of your learning is a matter for a qualified professional, not for an app or an article. Nothing here is an assessment, and no score from a practice game identifies a condition of any kind.
