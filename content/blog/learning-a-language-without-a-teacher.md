**Self-study handles most of language learning well and three specific things badly.** Knowing which three lets you source them separately instead of assuming you need a full course to get them.

## What self-study does well

**Vocabulary.** Better than a class, in fact. You control the pace, the selection and the review schedule, and vocabulary is exactly the kind of material that responds to distributed practice — the effect synthesised across a large literature by [Cepeda and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/).

**Grammar explanations.** A book or a course explains a rule as well as a person does, and you can reread it.

**Reading.** Entirely doable alone, and the skill that develops fastest in self-study.

**Listening.** Also doable alone, given material at the right level. [How to practise listening in a new language](/blog/listening-practice-in-a-new-language/) covers the routine.

That is most of the work. It is worth saying clearly, because self-study is often framed as second-best, and for these components it is not.

## The three things it cannot do

**1. Notice your errors.** You cannot correct an error you do not know you are making. Self-study has no mechanism for surfacing them, which is why fossilised mistakes are the characteristic signature of a self-taught learner.

**2. Hear your pronunciation.** You cannot hear your own production as a listener does — attention and expectation interfere. This is most acute in tonal languages, where you can perceive a distinction accurately and still produce the wrong one, as [what is a tonal language](/blog/what-is-a-tonal-language/) covers.

**3. Force unscripted production.** Real conversation requires producing language you did not prepare, at speed, with consequences for failing. No study routine reproduces that, because the essential feature is that you cannot control it.

## Source those three separately

The mistake is to conclude that you therefore need a teacher for everything. You do not. You need a *speaker*, for a small proportion of your time.

**Language exchange.** Someone learning your language, in exchange for you helping with theirs. Free, and the reciprocity makes it sustainable.

**Community groups.** Many languages have diaspora communities, cultural centres and conversation meet-ups. For under-resourced languages this is often the only realistic option — and speakers are frequently delighted to meet a learner, precisely because there are so few. [Learn Lao for beginners](/blog/learn-lao-beginners-guide/) covers studying a language in that situation.

**Paid conversation practice.** Considerably cheaper than formal tuition, because it needs a speaker rather than a trained teacher. For fixing errors and pronunciation, a speaker is usually sufficient.

**Recording yourself.** An imperfect substitute for the pronunciation problem, and much better than nothing. The gap between what you thought you said and what you actually said is the information you are missing.

## A routine with the right proportions

Roughly, by time:

| Component | Share | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Input (reading and listening) | Largest | Builds recognition, vocabulary in context, intuition |
| Retrieval practice | Substantial | Converts recognition into recall |
| Production | Moderate | Converts recall into use |
| Feedback from a person | Smallest, not zero | Fixes what the other three cannot |

Two notes on that table.

**Input should dominate.** Most self-study routines under-weight it, because studying feels more productive than reading. [Comprehensible input explained](/blog/comprehensible-input-explained/) covers pitching the difficulty.

**Retrieval, not review.** Producing an answer from memory before checking is substantially more effective than re-reading — one of the clearest findings in the [Dunlosky review of learning techniques](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/).

## Solo production practice

Between conversations, two techniques do real work:

**Shadowing.** Speaking along with a recording almost simultaneously. It trains rhythm, timing and articulation — the motor side of speaking. [The shadowing technique explained](/blog/shadowing-technique-explained/) covers how to do it without drilling errors.

**Writing.** Slower than speech and therefore easier, but it is genuine production: you construct sentences rather than recognise them. Writing a few sentences daily about what you did is unglamorous and effective.

## Consistency beats method

The single most reliable predictor of whether a self-study project succeeds is not which method was chosen. It is whether the person kept going.

Distributed practice outperforms massed practice, which means **a mediocre routine done daily beats an excellent one done occasionally.** Design for the days when you do not feel like it: a fifteen-minute minimum you can hit reliably is worth more than an hour-long plan you abandon.
