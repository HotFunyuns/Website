**Italian gives you pronunciation almost for free and charges you for grammar.** That trade shapes what a sensible first month looks like, and it is the opposite of the Southeast Asian languages we also teach, where the sounds are the obstacle and the grammar is the relief.

## What you get cheaply

**Transparent spelling.** Italian orthography is close to phonemic: letters map to sounds with few exceptions, and stress is largely predictable. A few hours of rules and you can read any written Italian aloud recognisably. Compare English, where "though", "through" and "tough" share four letters and no logic.

This matters more than it sounds. It means **written material is usable immediately**, which is a large advantage for independent study.

**A manageable sound inventory.** Italian has fewer vowel qualities than English and no tones. The consonants that give English speakers trouble — the rolled r, the double consonants that are genuinely held longer — are a short list rather than a general problem.

**Shared vocabulary.** English absorbed an enormous amount of Latin-derived vocabulary, much of it through French. The consequence is that an English speaker reading Italian recognises far more than they can produce. Your reading comprehension will run well ahead of your speaking ability, which is encouraging and slightly misleading.

## What you pay for

**Grammatical gender.** Every noun is masculine or feminine, and articles and adjectives agree with it. There is no gender-neutral option to fall back on.

The good news is that Italian gender is largely **predictable from the ending**, unlike German where it must be memorised per noun. Learn the patterns and most nouns sort themselves. [Italian articles and gender explained](/blog/italian-articles-and-gender/) covers the system, including why the article system has more forms than a beginner expects.

**Verb conjugation.** Verbs change for person, and the common verbs are irregular — as they are in every language, because frequency preserves irregularity. [Italian verb conjugation basics](/blog/italian-verb-conjugation-basics/) covers what to learn first.

**Agreement generally.** Adjectives agree with nouns in gender and number; some past forms agree with their subject or object. This is the thing that takes longest to become automatic, because it requires tracking information across a sentence.

## A realistic first month

**Week 1 — Pronunciation and reading aloud.**
Learn the spelling-to-sound rules, including where stress falls and what double consonants do. Read simple text aloud daily. This week produces the largest return per hour of any week in Italian.

**Week 2 — Present tense and the verb "to be".**
The regular present-tense patterns plus the handful of high-frequency irregulars. Enough to describe what is happening and what you do.

**Week 3 — Nouns, gender and articles.**
The gender patterns, the article forms, and adjective agreement. This is the week that feels like work.

**Week 4 — Vocabulary in context and simple past.**
Around 300 words grouped by situation, plus a way to talk about what already happened. Start listening to material you partly understand.

## The present tense does more than you expect

A useful and under-taught fact: **the present tense, combined with time expressions, covers a great deal of ordinary conversation.**

"Tomorrow I work", "later we eat", "I live here since three years" — the last is not idiomatic English but is a normal Italian construction. A beginner who has the present tense and a set of time words can express far more than one who has three tenses and no vocabulary.

The practical implication: **do not rush into tenses.** Spend the time on vocabulary and on making the present tense automatic. The rest arrives more easily once it is.

## What to expect from levels

The [Common European Framework of Reference](https://europa.eu/europass/en/common-european-framework-reference-language-skills) describes ability in terms of what a learner can do rather than how many hours they have studied, which is a much more useful frame for setting expectations than a time estimate.
