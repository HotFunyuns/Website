The fastest way to build a bad roster is to build a good one five times over. Take the highest-rated player available at every turn, end up with four excellent players who do the same thing, and discover in the season results that a team is not a leaderboard.

Balance is the fix, and it is routinely misunderstood as "spread your ratings evenly". It is not that. It is coverage.

## The jobs, not the positions

A lineup has to be able to do a small number of things every single possession. If it cannot do one of them, that gap is exposed constantly, and no amount of quality elsewhere hides it.

**Offensively:**

- **Advance and initiate.** Someone has to get the ball up the floor against pressure and start the action. If nobody can, the offence never begins.
- **Space the floor.** Enough shooting threat that defenders cannot all collapse toward the basket. Without it, every drive meets a crowd.
- **Finish inside.** Someone who converts close to the rim, so the defence pays for spreading out.

**Defensively:**

- **Protect the rim.** A last line when a drive beats the first defender.
- **Rebound.** Ending the possession. A defence that gets a stop and then gives the ball back has not got a stop.
- **Guard the perimeter.** Someone who can stay in front of the opponent's best ball-handler.

Six jobs, five players. That arithmetic is the whole design problem: somebody has to double up, which means every lineup involves a deliberate compromise about where to be merely adequate.

Notice that not one of these is a position. Positions are a shorthand for who usually does which job, covered in [basketball positions explained](/blog/basketball-positions-explained/), but the jobs are the thing the lineup is actually tested on.

## Playing time is a fixed budget

This is the constraint people forget, and it is the one that makes duplicate strength expensive.

Five players are on the court for a team at any moment. Under FIBA's Official Basketball Rules a game is four periods of ten minutes, so the total available is **5 × 40 = 200 player-minutes** per team per game. Competitions with longer periods produce a proportionally larger number — the arithmetic is the same, the total is just bigger.

Divide that budget:

| Rotation size | Average minutes each |
| --- | --- |
| 8 players | 25.0 |
| 9 players | 22.2 |
| 10 players | 20.0 |
| 12 players | 16.7 |

Two things follow immediately.

**Adding a player to the rotation takes minutes from someone already in it.** There is no expansion of the budget. A tenth man is not free; he is paid for out of everybody else's time.

**A second star at a position you have solved is competing with your own player.** If your best guard is playing most of the available guard minutes, the excellent guard you drafted next is not adding their full value — they are splitting a fixed allocation with someone who was already good. The improvement is the difference between them and the player they displace, and if the displaced player is yours and also good, that difference is small.

This is the mechanism behind the advice everyone repeats and few people follow: fill your worst slot before upgrading your best one.

## What depth is actually for

Depth is misunderstood as "having more good players". It is better understood as **insurance against your worst minutes**.

Every roster has a floor: the quality of the lineup you are forced to field when your preferred one is unavailable. Over a long season — 82 games in [Basketball Draft GM Franchise](/apps/pro-basketball-draft-gm-mode/), for instance — you will spend a meaningful share of the time somewhere other than your best five.

Depth raises that floor. That is all it does, and it explains why depth looks wasteful when things go well and decisive when they do not. You are not buying more good minutes at the top. You are buying fewer bad ones at the bottom.

The game itself is explicit that balance and depth carry more weight than any single marquee pick. That is a design choice worth taking at face value, because it tells you what the season is scoring.

## Top-heavy versus balanced, honestly stated

Neither approach is correct in general. They buy different things, and pretending otherwise is how strategy discussions go in circles.

**Top-heavy** concentrates quality in a few players. It raises your ceiling, gives you units nobody can match, and concentrates your risk — the roster is only as good as those few slots. In shorter formats and in knockout scenarios, that concentration can be exactly right.

**Balanced** spreads quality across the roster. It raises your floor, survives adversity better, and rarely produces a dominant unit. Over long seasons, floors tend to matter more than ceilings, simply because there are more games in which something can go wrong.

The useful move is not to pick a side but to **find out what your format rewards** by running the same board twice, once each way, and comparing across several runs rather than one. A single season is one sample of a model you cannot see, which is covered properly in [understanding sports sim probability](/blog/understanding-sports-sim-probability/).

## Building it during the draft

The principles above translate into a sequence.

**1. Cover the jobs before you upgrade anything.** Early picks should ensure every team-level job on the list has an owner. Not a good owner necessarily — an owner.

**2. Take the scarce jobs early.** Some of these are harder to find late than others. Rim protection and reliable ball-handling tend to dry up; general scoring tends not to. Spend early picks where waiting costs you the most.

**3. Ask what each pick displaces.** Before confirming, name the player they would take minutes from. If that player is already good, the pick is worth far less than the rating on screen.

**4. Prefer complementary to similar.** Two players who do the same thing well are worth less together than the sum of their ratings; two who cover each other's weaknesses are worth more.

**5. Leave one slot for the board.** Boards develop unpredictably. A plan with no flexibility cannot take advantage when something falls further than it should.

**6. Re-read the roster as a lineup, not a list.** Before the draft closes, mentally put your best five on the floor and walk through the six jobs. Anything unowned is the thing your season will be about.

## After the draft: rotations, trades, contracts

Drafting sets your ceiling. The front office decides how much of it you reach, and each tool solves a different problem.

**Rotations are the distribution problem.** You are allocating that fixed minute budget across a roster that is not uniformly good. Concentrating minutes raises your average and thins you out; spreading them protects against a weak spot and dilutes your strengths. There is no neutral choice — declining to decide is just a different allocation.

**Trades are the only real fix for a structural mistake.** If the draft left you long at one job and short at another, that is exactly the imbalance a trade exists to correct: converting surplus you cannot use into coverage you need.

**Contracts add time.** A contract is not only a price but a duration, and committing years to a player also commits the flexibility you might have wanted later. The cap side of that is covered in [salary cap basics for GM games](/blog/salary-cap-basics-for-gm-games/).

## Diagnosing a season that went badly

When the record disappoints and the roster looked fine, work through this in order:

1. **Is a team-level job unowned?** Walk the list. This finds most problems.
2. **Is quality duplicated?** Two players doing the same thing means one is largely redundant.
3. **Is the rotation too narrow or too wide?** Check whether your minute distribution matches how steep the quality drop-off actually is.
4. **Is the roster built for a different format?** Depth-rewarding and ceiling-rewarding formats are not the same puzzle.
5. **Was it just a draw?** Repeat the run before concluding anything. One result is a sample, not a verdict.

And keep the interpretation straight: the record a simulator returns is a model's score for how your roster fits together. It is not a forecast, and it says nothing about any real team or player.
