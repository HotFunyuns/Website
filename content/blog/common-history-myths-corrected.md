Four widely repeated history claims are false, and each one is corrected below by a museum, a national archive, an excavation project or NASA. The more useful part comes after the corrections: myths persist because they do a job that the correction often fails to do, and knowing that changes how you should learn and retell them.

## Vikings did not wear horned helmets

The National Museum of Denmark is direct about this. Only one preserved helmet survives from the entire Viking Age, recovered at Gjermundbu in Norway, and it has no horns. Fragments found in Denmark include brow ridges from helmets, again without horns.

Horned headgear does appear in some Iron Age and Viking-era imagery — the Golden Horns, the Oseberg tapestry — and the museum suggests it belonged to ritual or display contexts rather than to fighting. Its practical objection is worth repeating: horns would catch on rigging, on shields and on other men, and no contemporary source describes Vikings wearing them.

**Why it sticks:** the image is theatrical, and it arrived through nineteenth-century stage design and illustration rather than through evidence. A costume is easier to remember than an absence.

## The Great Wall of China is not visible from space with the naked eye

NASA's own caption on an image of the wall taken from the International Space Station states that "Despite myths to the contrary, the wall isn't visible from the moon, and is difficult or impossible to see from Earth orbit without the high-powered lenses used for this photo." The photograph in question was taken by Expedition 10 commander Leroy Chiao.

The geometry explains it. The wall is enormously long but only a few metres wide, and much of it is built from earth and stone quarried on the spot, so it matches the colour of the ground around it. Length does not help visibility; width does.

**Why it sticks:** it is a satisfying superlative, it was printed in school textbooks for decades, and it feels like it ought to be true.

## Nobody was burned at the stake at Salem

The Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive, edited by Benjamin Ray at the University of Virginia, records that nineteen people were executed by hanging in 1692, that one more was tortured to death, and that at least five died in jail. That single outbreak produced more executions than all the previous witchcraft trials in New England combined.

Burning was used in some European jurisdictions, and the conflation of European and colonial North American practice is the likely source of the error. The distinction matters because it changes what kind of legal proceeding Salem was: these were convictions in a colonial court operating under English law, not an inquisitorial process.

**Why it sticks:** the burning image carries the emotional weight the story seems to demand, and films supply it.

## The pyramids were not built by enslaved labour

Excavation at Heit el-Ghurab, the settlement beside the Giza pyramids investigated by Ancient Egypt Research Associates since 1988, has uncovered an urban site of more than seven hectares containing bakeries, breweries, kitchens, craft workshops, warehouses, small houses and residences for administrators. Royal silos distributed grain that was turned into bread and beer for the workforce, and graffiti and gang marks record how labour was organised into named groups.

That is the archaeology of a provisioned, administered, state-organised workforce. It does not look like the mass chattel slavery of the popular image, and the popular image itself derives from much later retellings rather than from Egyptian evidence. Who built which monument, and how confidently we can say so, is the subject of [who built the great monuments](/blog/who-built-the-great-monuments/).

**Why it sticks:** it was absorbed into religious and cinematic storytelling long before the workers' settlement was excavated, and the correction is younger than most people's schooling.

## Why corrections fail, and what to do about it

A retraction removes a claim but leaves a gap where an explanation used to be, and people go on using the retracted claim to fill it. Psychologists call this the continued influence effect. In a 2021 study in *Memory & Cognition*, Ullrich Ecker and Luke Antonio found that retractions from sources rated low in trustworthiness were entirely ineffective, that perceived expertise of the correcting source made no measurable difference, and — most soberingly — that "substantial continued influence effects can still occur with retractions designed to be and rated as highly credible."

The practical implication for anyone learning history is to correct *with* a replacement rather than a deletion. "Vikings did not wear horned helmets" is weak. "Only one Viking-age helmet survives at all, from Gjermundbu in Norway, and the Danish finds are fragments of brow ridges made to protect the face" is strong, because it gives memory something to hold.

## Building the correction into how you study

Treat every memorable historical claim as a candidate myth until you have seen where it comes from. The habit is cheap: note the claim, note who first recorded it, note how long after the event. The procedure in [analysing a primary source](/blog/how-to-analyse-a-primary-source/) is the long version, and [studying history on your own](/blog/how-to-study-history-on-your-own/) covers keeping notes that let you find the answer again a year later.

## A short rule for retelling

If you repeat a corrected myth in order to correct it, lead with the true version. Repetition is what fixes a claim in memory, and the first thing a listener hears is what they are most likely to keep. Say what the evidence shows, then say what the story got wrong, and name the source that settles it.
