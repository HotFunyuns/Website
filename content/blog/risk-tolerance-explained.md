Every investment answers two questions at once: how much might this earn, and how much might it lose along the way? **Risk tolerance** is about the second. Investor.gov defines it as an investor's ability and willingness to lose some or all of an investment in exchange for greater potential returns. That definition contains two separate tests, and they do not always point the same way.

## Ability and willingness

| | Ability to take risk | Willingness to take risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it measures | Whether your finances can absorb a loss | Whether you can live with one |
| Shaped by | When you need the money, income, savings, debts | Temperament and experience |
| Investor.gov's framing | When you will need the money, and your goals | Whether you will be able to sleep at night holding a risky investment |

The two can conflict. A 25-year-old saving for retirement has decades of ability but may panic at the first fall. A cautious saver close to buying a home may be willing to gamble but has no ability to recover if the deposit shrinks.

## Time horizon changes the answer

Investor.gov's asset allocation guidance defines the time horizon as the number of months, years or decades you plan to invest to reach a goal, and notes that investors with longer horizons may be more comfortable with riskier or more volatile investments.

Its "gauge your risk tolerance" page gives two contrasting cases:

- **35 years to retirement.** You may want to consider riskier investments, because sticking only to savings products or low-risk investments may make your money grow too slowly, and inflation and taxes may erode its purchasing power. Investor.gov calls putting long-term money in low-interest investments a frequent mistake.
- **A goal five years away or less.** You do not want risky investments, because when it is time to sell, you may have to take a loss.

Splitting money by goal helps: an emergency fund, a house deposit and a retirement account can each have a different risk level.

## A drawdown test in dollars

Percentages feel abstract until they are dollars. Investor.gov notes that large-company stocks as a group have lost money on average about one out of every three years, and it generally defines a [bear market](/blog/bull-vs-bear-market/) as a fall of 20% or more in a broad index over at least two months. Imagine a $10,000 portfolio:

| Fall | Portfolio becomes | Gain needed to get back to $10,000 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10% | $9,000 | 11.1% |
| 20% | $8,000 | 25.0% |
| 33% | $6,700 | 49.3% |
| 50% | $5,000 | 100.0% |

Two lessons. First, ask honestly what you would do at each row: hold, add money or sell. Selling at the bottom turns a temporary fall into a permanent loss. Second, losses are asymmetric: the deeper the fall, the larger the percentage gain needed to recover, which is why limiting large losses matters. [Volatility explained](/blog/what-is-volatility/) covers how the size of typical swings is measured.

## Questions to ask before any investment

Drawing on Investor.gov's guidance:

1. **When will I need this money?** Investor.gov's guidance is that money for a goal five years or less away does not belong in risky investments.
2. **What happens if I lose some or all of it?** That is the definition of risk tolerance in practice.
3. **Do I understand it?** If you cannot explain how it makes or loses money, you cannot judge how much it might lose.
4. **How do the risks compare with the rewards?** Higher potential returns come with greater risk, and pitches of high returns with little or no risk are a hallmark of fraud; see [investment fraud red flags](/blog/investment-fraud-red-flags/).
5. **Is it spread out?** [Diversification](/blog/diversification-explained/) and a mix of assets such as [stocks and bonds](/blog/stocks-vs-bonds/) reduce the damage any one holding can do.

## Questionnaires and professional advice

Investor.gov notes that some investment websites offer free online questionnaires to help assess risk tolerance, but warns that results may be biased towards the products or services of whoever sponsors the website.

When a broker makes a recommendation, the firm is expected to know your situation. FINRA's suitability rule, which applies where the SEC's Regulation Best Interest does not, lists what a customer's investment profile includes: age, other investments, financial situation and needs, tax status, investment objectives, investment experience, time horizon, liquidity needs and risk tolerance. Working through the same list yourself is a sound way to prepare.
