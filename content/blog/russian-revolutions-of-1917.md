Russia had two revolutions in 1917, eight months apart, and they need separate explanations. The first, in February by the Russian calendar, ended the monarchy and was not planned or led by any political party. The second, in October, was the Bolshevik seizure of an authority that had been divided since February. Between them lies a period in which the outcome was genuinely undecided, and compressing the year into a single arc — tsar falls, Bolsheviks rise — deletes precisely the part that explains anything.

## The calendar trap, resolved

Before the sequence, deal with the thing that confuses every reader once: the October Revolution happened in November.

Russia was still using the Julian calendar in 1917 and changed to the Gregorian in 1918. The United States Naval Observatory sets out why the two diverge: the Julian calendar, introduced by Julius Caesar in 45 BC, "introduced a simple leap year rule: insert an extra day every four years", and the observatory notes that this "does not produce a precise match to the solar year", so "the date of the astronomical vernal equinox slowly drifted". The Gregorian reform of 1582 corrected the accumulated drift and changed the rule, dropping the leap day in century years not divisible by 400.

The consequence is arithmetic you can do yourself. The 1582 reform opened a ten-day gap. The Julian calendar then kept leap days in 1700, 1800 and 1900 that the Gregorian dropped, widening the gap by one day each time. By the early twentieth century it stood at thirteen days. That is why Russian dates from this period are given Old Style and New Style, and why 25 October and 7 November name the same day. Our guide to [how calendars and dating systems work](/blog/how-calendars-and-dating-systems-work/) covers the general mechanism; the point here is only that nothing mysterious is happening.

## February: a collapse nobody organised

The February revolution began with bread queues, strikes and demonstrations in Petrograd in a winter of acute shortage, in the third year of a war going badly. What turned protest into revolution was the garrison. Soldiers ordered to suppress the demonstrations refused and joined them, and at that point the state had no instrument of coercion left in its own capital.

Nicholas II abdicated. Two bodies emerged with competing claims: a Provisional Government drawn largely from the existing parliamentary elite, and the Petrograd Soviet, a council of workers' and soldiers' deputies. This is the arrangement usually called dual power — the government had legal authority, the Soviet had practical authority over the soldiers, the railways and much of the industrial workforce.

Note what is absent from this account. No party organised February. The Bolshevik leadership was largely in exile or abroad. Reading October back into February — treating the first revolution as the opening act of the second — is the single most common distortion in popular narratives of the year.

## The eight months in between

The Provisional Government made one decision that shaped everything after it: continuing the war.

That choice is defensible on its own terms — treaty obligations, the expectation of Allied credit, fear of German terms — and it was fatal, because the demands driving the February crisis were peace, land and bread, and continuing the war made all three harder to deliver. A summer military offensive failed. Inflation accelerated. Peasant seizures of land proceeded without waiting for legislation. An attempted move against the government from the military right in the late summer was defeated with the help of the very forces the government most feared, which armed and legitimised them.

By the autumn the Provisional Government had very little support left to lose, and the Bolshevik slogans mapped directly onto the unmet demands. The October seizure of power in Petrograd was, at the moment it happened, a comparatively small operation. What followed — a civil war lasting years — was not.

## Why the historiography matters here more than usual

Three broad traditions read this year, and they do not merely differ in emphasis.

Soviet historiography presented October as the culmination of a party-led proletarian revolution, with February as its prologue. Émigré and much Western writing of the Cold War decades presented October as a coup by a disciplined minority against a nascent democracy. Social historians from the 1970s onward argued from below, looking at factory committees, soldiers' assemblies and village politics, and found more popular initiative than either of the older framings allowed. Post-1991 archival work has complicated all three.

Those archives are the reason the field moved. The Library of Congress's *Revelations from the Russian Archives* exhibition, shown in 1992, drew on Central Committee, presidential and KGB materials that became accessible after the failed coup of August 1991, and displayed 500 of them. The Hoover Institution Library and Archives at Stanford, founded in 1919, holds nearly 7,000 archival collections "pertaining to war, revolution, and peace", including a project to microfilm and publish Soviet archival material. Seventeen Moments in Soviet History, produced by Michigan State University and Macalester College with National Endowment for the Humanities support, assembles primary sources, images and multimedia around seventeen years of which 1917 is the first.

An account of 1917 should tell you which tradition it is working in. Ours is that the evidence supports a genuinely open eight months, and that both the inevitability story and the pure-coup story compress it. ## What to take away

**Two revolutions, eight months, one year.** February ended the monarchy without a party organising it; October transferred power to one.

**The calendar gap is arithmetic**, not a puzzle: thirteen days by 1917, which is why October is November.

**Name your historiography.** Soviet, émigré and post-Soviet readings of 1917 disagree about fundamentals, and a confident single narrative is usually one of them wearing a neutral tone.
