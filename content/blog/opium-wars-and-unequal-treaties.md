The Opium Wars were fought to keep an illegal drug trade open. China had prohibited opium outright in 1796, according to The National Archives; British merchants went on smuggling it in from British-controlled India at volumes the archive puts at over 1,000 tonnes a year by the early nineteenth century; and when Qing officials enforced their own law in 1839 by seizing and destroying stock, Britain went to war and won. The settlement that followed ceded Hong Kong Island, opened Chinese ports on British terms and required China to pay for the opium its own government had confiscated. None of that is a controversial reconstruction — the treaty is a catalogued document in a national archive.

## The trade imbalance that created the trade

Start with the commercial mechanics, because the drug trade was a solution to a problem, and the problem came first.

Britain wanted Chinese tea in enormous quantity. MIT's Visualizing Cultures project records that by 1800 the East India Company was buying 23 million pounds of tea a year "at a cost of 3.6 million pounds of silver". The difficulty was that Britain produced little the Chinese market wanted in exchange, so payment flowed out in bullion. Opium grown in British-controlled India reversed that flow.

The scale of the reversal is visible in the import figures Visualizing Cultures gives: about 4,000 chests in 1790, around 10,000 in the early 1820s, and roughly 40,000 by 1839. By the 1830s the project estimates some 10 million Chinese consumers, of whom around 2 million were addicted. The National Archives summarises the same process plainly: Britain "started growing opium in its Indian colonies and exporting it to China, where it spread through the population".

This is the part that gets softened in older teaching. The trade was not an unfortunate side effect of commerce. It was a deliberate commercial strategy, pursued after prohibition, by merchants and an imperial administration that knew it was illegal in the destination country.

## The Qing prohibition, and the 1839 confrontation

China banned opium in 1796. Enforcement was inconsistent for decades, partly because enforcement is difficult against a sea-borne smuggling network and partly because officials at several levels were implicated in it. Neither fact changes the legal position.

In 1839 the Daoguang emperor appointed Lin Zexu as imperial commissioner with authority to suppress the trade, and Lin arrived at Canton in March of that year. His letter to Queen Victoria, preserved in Fordham University's sourcebook, sets out the Qing case in terms a modern reader has no difficulty following. On Britain's domestic prohibition: "Since then you do not permit it to injure your own country, you ought not to have the injurious drug transferred to another country." On the commercial relationship: "Your honorable nation takes away the products of our central land, and not only do you thereby obtain food and support for yourselves, but moreover, by re-selling these products to other countries you reap a threefold profit."

Lin confiscated and destroyed the opium stocks held at Canton. The National Archives dates the outbreak of the first war to 4 September 1839. Reading Lin's letter before reading any modern summary is instructive, and it is a good practical example of the difference between [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/): the argument in the document is not the argument later historiography put in his mouth.

## The treaties, and why the adjective is accurate

The first war ended in 1842 with the Treaty of Nanking, held at The National Archives under catalogue reference FO 93/23/1b. Its Article 3 granted Hong Kong Island to Britain. Its Article 5 allowed British subjects to trade with any merchants in China, which dismantled the licensed monopoly through which Canton trade had been channelled. China was required to pay damages for the opium its officials had destroyed, and additional ports were opened.

A second war followed from 1856 to 1860, bringing further territorial and commercial concessions including the addition of Kowloon to the Hong Kong colony. In 1898 a convention leased the New Territories for 99 years, a term whose arithmetic set the date of the handover on 1 July 1997.

"Unequal treaties" is a description of structure, not a slogan. The obligations ran one way: indemnities, ceded territory, tariffs fixed by agreement rather than by Chinese law, and legal privileges for foreign nationals on Chinese soil. They were signed after military defeat by a state that had no realistic option to refuse. That is what the phrase names.

## The "century of humiliation", held at arm's length

The period from 1839 onward is described in Chinese public discourse as a century of humiliation, and the phrase does two jobs at once.

It describes something documented: a sustained sequence of military defeats, imposed treaties, territorial losses and foreign privilege inside Chinese territory. It is also an organising frame used in present-day political argument, both domestically and internationally, and it selects and emphasises accordingly.

A careful account states both, attributes both, and refuses two opposite temptations: dismissing the framing because it is politically useful, or adopting it wholesale as though the nineteenth century contained nothing else. The Qing state of this period was simultaneously fighting the enormous internal conflicts covered in our article on [China's deadliest rebellions](/blog/chinas-deadliest-rebellions/), which is part of why its capacity to resist was what it was. Qing officials were neither uniformly helpless nor uniquely corrupt; they were administrators of a very large state under simultaneous internal and external pressure, and some of them, Lin among them, acted decisively on the law as it stood.

Visualizing Cultures notes that conventional Chinese historiography dates the modern period from the end of the first war in 1842. That is itself a periodisation decision with consequences, of the kind our guide to [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) unpacks — and a reminder that where a period is cut determines what looks like a cause. The same problem sits underneath the long argument over [what caused the Bronze Age collapse](/blog/what-caused-the-bronze-age-collapse/), where the chosen start and end dates do a great deal of unacknowledged work.

## What to take away

**The cause was enforcement of a drug ban, and the objective was forced market access.** Keep the two separate: the first is the trigger, the second is what the victory was used for.

**The terms are documented, not inferred.** Articles 3 and 5 of the Treaty of Nanking, in a catalogued archival file, say what the settlement did.

**Name the coercion, and keep the framing attributed.** The trade was deliberate and illegal in China; the "century of humiliation" describes real events and is also a live political frame, and a good account says both.
