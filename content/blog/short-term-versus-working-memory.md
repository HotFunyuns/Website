Short-term memory usually names the temporary holding of information in an accessible state. Working memory usually names holding *plus* doing something to what is held. The second term contains the first, which is why the two get used interchangeably and why that interchange causes trouble. This article separates them, explains why the boundary is genuinely blurry rather than merely badly taught, and gives you a test you can apply to any task in about five seconds.

## Why even the researchers disagree

It would be convenient to report a clean consensus definition. There is not one, and pretending otherwise would be the wrong kind of simplification.

Nelson Cowan's review of the topic states the problem directly: the distinction between short-term memory and working memory "is clouded in a bit of confusion but that is largely the result of different investigators using different definitions." He then lists three senses in which working memory has been used — as short-term memory applied to cognitive tasks, as a multi-component system that holds and manipulates information in short-term memory, and as the use of attention to manage short-term memory.

Those three are not wild variants. They differ in emphasis: task context, architecture, and attentional control. But they are different enough that two competent sources can describe "working memory" and not be describing the same thing. Cowan's own move is to suggest the substantive question is not the label but why some memory tasks correlate with broader cognitive aptitudes and others do not.

The architecture side of the debate is live too. Robert Hughes, writing in the *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*, summarises the classical account — a phonological store described as "a passive short-term store that holds representations of verbal items in phonological form", refreshed by "an active articulatory control process that supports articulatory rehearsal" — and then argues the evidence is better explained by a perceptual-motor account with no discrete store at all. You do not need to take a side to draw the useful lesson: the boxes in the diagram are models, not organs.

## The test that separates them in practice

Skip the taxonomy and ask one question about the task in front of you:

**Could you succeed while doing nothing but keeping the material alive?**

If yes, you are in storage territory. Hearing a sequence and repeating it in the same order is the clean case. Nothing is being transformed; the only job is survival and order.

If no — if the task requires a second operation performed *on* the held material — you are in working memory territory. Repeating a sequence backwards requires a transformation. Adding numbers as they arrive requires an update after every item. Keeping the premises of an argument available while you evaluate the conclusion requires you to hold and judge simultaneously.

That test is not a formal definition, and it will not resolve the literature. It is reliable enough for the decisions a learner actually makes, such as which drill to run and why a task that looks simple keeps collapsing.

## What the duration figures really mean

"Short-term memory lasts about twenty seconds" is one of those statements that is repeated far more often than it is qualified. Different figures come from different paradigms, and the paradigm is doing most of the work.

Berman, Jonides and Lewis, examining whether time itself is the culprit, are worth quoting on the two halves of the design. They note that rehearsal "happens so habitually that it is often not noticed and is difficult to disengage", which is why experiments block it with a distractor task. And in their comparison of intervals they report that "both conditions with a filled interval led to worse memory performance, but the difficulty of the intervening task had no effect." Their broader conclusion is that the mere passage of time does not cause forgetting in short-term memory, with interference playing the dominant role.

So the honest version is: material survives as long as you can keep refreshing it and as long as nothing competes with it. Take away refreshing and add competition, and it goes quickly. That is a mechanism, not a stopwatch reading, and it is treated more fully in [our article on delayed recall and retention intervals](/blog/delayed-recall-explained/).

## The capacity is shared, which is why the second job hurts

The reason working memory tasks feel disproportionately harder is that storage and processing draw on the same limited pool. Cowan's review of capacity argues for a central store limited to roughly **3 to 5 meaningful items in young adults**, counted in chunks rather than raw items. Every operation you perform on held material competes for that pool.

This is the mechanism behind a familiar experience in mental arithmetic. You know how to multiply 37 by 6. You are not confused about the method. But holding 180 while you work out 42 and then combining them is three live items plus an operation, and if anything interrupts you, the partial result is the thing that vanishes. The method survived; the holding did not. Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and its memory catalogue happens to divide along this line unusually cleanly, which makes it a decent demonstration tool.

On the storage side sit Number Recall and Sequence Memory: the digits appear, they disappear, you reproduce them in order. Nothing is transformed. On the working memory side sit Running Total Memory, where numbers arrive one at a time and you hold the running sum rather than the list; Mental Math Chain, which strings operations together; and the Memory Math mode, which shows numbers and then asks you to calculate with them after they are gone.

Running the two families back to back is the clearest way to feel the difference. Most people find their sequence lengths drop sharply the moment an operation is added, and that drop is the point rather than a failure.
