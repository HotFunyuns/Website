Man-to-man defence gives each defender one opponent to guard, wherever that opponent goes. Zone defence gives each defender an area of the floor to guard, and whoever enters it. Both are legal in the NBA and under FIBA's international rules. The NBA's only real limit on zones is its defensive three-second rule, which stops a defender standing in the lane for more than three seconds without guarding anyone.

The interesting question is not which system is better. It is what each one is protecting, what it is willing to give away, and which players a team needs to make the trade worth it.

## Players or areas: the core difference

| | Man-to-man | Zone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Each defender owns | An opponent | An area of the floor |
| Strongest against | Individual creators and shooters, who are always contested | Drives and post play, since the paint is pre-crowded |
| Most exposed by | Screens that force switches or help, and one weak defender being hunted | Ball movement, skip passes, shooting and attackers in the gaps between areas |
| Rebounding | Box-out assignments come with the matchup | Box-outs have to be organised by area, because nobody starts with a fixed opponent |
| Hides a weak defender? | No, the opponent can target them | Often, by placing them where the ball rarely goes |
| Foul exposure | Concentrated on whoever guards the best scorer | Shared more evenly across the zone |

Man-to-man is built on accountability. Every shot has an obvious defender, and any breakdown has a name attached. The price is that screens and drives force defenders into decisions, to switch, help or chase, and each decision opens something else.

A zone is built on positioning. It starts every possession with bodies between the ball and the basket. The price is that the gaps between areas are fixed, so an offence that moves the ball faster than the zone can shift will find them.

## The common zones and their soft spots

Zones are named by their alignment, read from the top of the floor toward the basket.

| Zone | Shape | Built to protect | Gaps attackers look for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2-3 | Two at the top, three along the baseline | The paint and the baseline | The free-throw line area, the short corners, the wings after a quick swing |
| 1-2-2 (or 3-2) | One at the top, two at the elbows or wings, two low | The perimeter, especially the wings | The middle of the paint and the baseline behind the front line |
| 1-3-1 | One top, three across the middle, one low | The middle and the wings, and trapping on the sides | The corners and the short baseline, where one low defender must cover both sides |
| Matchup zone | A zone shape that picks up man-to-man once the ball is in an area | Its starting areas, while still contesting shots | The moment of handover between areas, especially on cuts |
| Box-and-one | Four in a box zone, one following the opponent's best scorer | Denial of one player | The other four attackers, who face a zone with one fewer defender in it |

The pattern across the table is consistent. Each alignment chooses where to put its five bodies, and every place it puts them is a place they are not. Beating a zone is mostly a matter of passing the ball to where they are not before they can get there: into the high post, out to the short corner, or across the floor with a skip pass.

## Is zone defence illegal?

No. The defensive three-second rule in the NBA's 2025-26 rulebook, the latest edition the league had posted by October 2026, states that "any defensive player may play any offensive player" and that "the defenders may double-team any player." The restriction is narrow:

- The count starts when the attacking team has the ball in its frontcourt.
- Any defender in the 16-foot lane, or the area 4 feet beyond it, must be "actively guarding" an opponent within three seconds, which the rule defines as within arm's length and in a guarding position.
- The defender guarding the ball-handler is exempt and may stand in the lane; once the ball is passed, that defender must guard someone or leave.
- The count is suspended during a shot, on a loss of team control, while the defender is actively guarding, once the defender completely clears the lane, or when it is imminent that the defender will become legal.
- The penalty is a technical foul, so one free throw, and the attacking team keeps the ball.

In practice the rule means an NBA zone cannot leave a centre standing in the middle of the lane guarding nobody. The middle defender has to step in and out, or stay attached to a nearby attacker. A traditional 2-3 can still be played; it just has to breathe.

FIBA's rules, in the 2026 edition valid from 1 October 2026, are looser. Its three-second rule applies only to attacking players, so a FIBA defence can keep a centre in the restricted area for the whole possession. The zone that NBA rules make awkward is entirely legal in international basketball.

## When teams switch between them

Nothing obliges a team to play one system for a whole game, and the good reasons for changing are mostly about protecting something:

- **Foul trouble.** A zone can tuck a defender with four fouls into an area where contact is less likely, rather than leaving them on the best scorer.
- **A weak individual defender.** If a player is needed for their scoring, a zone can hide them where a man-to-man scheme would let the opponent hunt them.
- **A driving opponent.** Against a team that scores by attacking the rim and does not shoot well from distance, a zone takes away its preferred shots and dares it to take the ones it dislikes.
- **One dominant scorer.** A box-and-one, or a man-to-man with an early double-team, concentrates effort on the player who matters most.
- **Changing the rhythm.** A different look after a timeout can cost an offence a few possessions while it works out what it is seeing.

The reasons for switching back are the mirror image. A zone that keeps conceding open threes stops paying once they start going in, and a skilled passer at the free-throw line can pick apart a 2-3 from its weakest spot.

## The front-office view: let the roster choose

A defensive scheme is only as good as the players asked to run it, which makes it a team-building question as much as a coaching one.

| If the roster has... | ...it leans toward |
| --- | --- |
| A slow but strong rim protector | Zone looks, or man-to-man with the big staying near the basket |
| Several similar-sized, mobile defenders | Switching man-to-man |
| One poor defender who is needed on offence | Zone, to hide them |
| Long, quick wings | Trapping zones such as the 1-3-1, or aggressive man-to-man |
| Little shot-blocking at all | Man-to-man with early help, because a zone without a rim protector has an empty middle |
