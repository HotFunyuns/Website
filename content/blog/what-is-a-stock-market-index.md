"The market was up 1% today" is usually a statement about a **stock market index**. The SEC's investor bulletin on index funds describes a market index as a measure of the performance of a "basket" of securities, such as stocks or bonds, meant to represent a sector of a stock market or an economy. It also makes a point that is easy to miss: **you cannot invest directly in a market index.** You can buy a fund that tracks one, which [index funds explained](/blog/index-funds-explained/) covers. This article is about the index itself: what is in the basket, and how much each member counts.

## Three ways to weight an index

Two indexes can hold exactly the same stocks and still move differently, because of their **weighting method**. Wikipedia's example is the S&P 500 and the S&P 500 Equal Weight: same companies, different rules for how much each counts.

| Method | Each stock's weight depends on | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Market-cap weighted | Its total market value (price × shares outstanding) | S&P 500 (float-adjusted) |
| Price weighted | Its share price alone | Dow Jones Industrial Average |
| Equal weighted | Nothing: each of n stocks gets 1/n | S&P 500 Equal Weight |

The SEC's bulletin explains that market indexes often use market capitalization to decide each security's weight, so bigger companies account for a bigger share of the index; [market capitalization explained](/blog/market-capitalization-explained/) covers the measure. In a price-weighted index, the price per share decides the weight instead.

## A worked example with three stocks

Take three companies:

| Stock | Share price | Shares outstanding | Market cap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | $300 | 1 million | $300 million |
| B | $50 | 20 million | $1,000 million |
| C | $20 | 10 million | $200 million |

Their weights under each method:

| Stock | Price weighted | Cap weighted | Equal weighted |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 81.1% | 20.0% | 33.3% |
| B | 13.5% | 66.7% | 33.3% |
| C | 5.4% | 13.3% | 33.3% |

Now suppose one stock rises 10% and the others are unchanged:

| Stock that rises 10% | Price-weighted index | Cap-weighted index | Equal-weighted index |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A, the high-priced small company | +8.11% | +2.00% | +3.33% |
| B, the large company | +1.35% | +6.67% | +3.33% |

The same day in the market produces three different headlines. In the price-weighted index, the high-priced but smallest company dominates. Wikipedia notes that equal weighting produces the least concentrated portfolios, but tends to tilt towards smaller companies, which usually means higher volatility than cap weighting.

## The S&P 500

According to Wikipedia, the S&P 500 is maintained by S&P Dow Jones Indices, and its members are chosen by a **committee** using published criteria, not purely by a formula. It took its current 500-company form on 4 March 1957 and has used public float-adjusted market-cap weighting since 2005, meaning only shares available to the public count. Despite the name, Wikipedia lists 503 constituents as of 2026. Under cap weighting, a move in one of its largest members shifts the index far more than the same percentage move in one of its smallest, exactly as stock B did in the example above.

## The Dow Jones Industrial Average

The Dow is older and much smaller. Wikipedia records that it debuted on 26 May 1896 with 12 industrial stocks; today it tracks **30**. It is **price-weighted**: its value is the sum of its members' share prices divided by a factor, the Dow divisor, which was about 0.168 as of July 2026. The divisor is changed whenever a member splits its stock, so a split alone does not move the index; [stock splits explained](/blog/stock-splits-explained/) shows why a split leaves a company's value unchanged.

The divisor explains the Dow's big point moves. Wikipedia gives the divisor in effect since 29 June 2026 as 0.16824816528350, so a $1 change in any one member's share price moves the index by 1 ÷ 0.168248 ≈ **5.94 points**, whatever the company's size.

## Price return and total return

Wikipedia's S&P 500 article tabulates its annual returns two ways: **price return**, which excludes dividends, and **total return**, which includes them. A headline index level reflects price changes, so comparing your own results, including dividends, with a price-return index understates the index. [How dividends work](/blog/how-dividends-work/) covers the dividend side.

## Why indexes matter

Indexes are the yardsticks for funds and for the market as a whole. The SEC's alert on social media fraud suggests comparing any promised investment return with the returns of well-known stock indexes, and the S&P 500 also sets the thresholds for the [market-wide circuit breakers](/blog/market-circuit-breakers-explained/). [How the stock market works](/blog/how-the-stock-market-works/) explains the trading behind each index's moves.
