A regnal year counts time from the moment a particular ruler took power: year one, year two, year twelve of that reign, then the count resets when the next ruler accedes. For most of recorded history this is how documents dated themselves. A modern reader encountering "in the fourteenth year of Emperor Kinmei" is looking at a complete, precise, locally unambiguous date — that happens to be useless without a separate body of work connecting reigns to a continuous year count.

## What regnal dating is actually good at

The system suits the society that uses it. A regnal date is short, it is verifiable by anyone in the administration, it cannot drift, and it carries a political statement — the year is the king's year. Tax records, land grants, legal judgments and chronicle entries can all be filed and cross-checked without anyone needing to agree on an era epoch.

What it cannot do is span dynasties. Two documents from different reigns cannot be placed relative to each other from their dates alone. Someone has to know the order of the reigns and how long each lasted, and that knowledge lives in a separate kind of document.

## King lists: the instrument that makes chronology possible

A king list records rulers in sequence with the length of each reign. Chain the reigns and you get elapsed time; anchor the chain at one end and you get calendar dates.

The Ashmolean Museum holds one of the most important examples from ancient Mesopotamia: a clay prism, each of its four sides inscribed with two columns of cuneiform recording the Sumerian language, listing a succession of cities in Sumer and neighbouring regions, their rulers, and the length of their reigns. Several versions of the Sumerian King List survive; the Ashmolean's is the most extensive and the most complete. It is dated to around 1800 BC and probably came from Larsa.

The museum's own description is admirably direct about what kind of document this is. It calls the list "not history as we would understand it, but a combination of myth, legend and historical information". It begins with the mythological origins of kingship, where individuals have fantastically long reigns, passes through the famous lines about the Flood sweeping over and kingship descending from heaven, and only becomes realistic in its reign lengths as it reaches historical rulers. It ends with King Sin-magir of the Isin dynasty, around 1827–1817 BC.

And it had a purpose. The Ashmolean notes that the list seems to have been composed to imply that dominion over Mesopotamia, determined by the gods, could be exercised by only one city at a time and only for a limited period — which conveniently made the rulers of Isin the rightful successors of earlier powerful kingdoms.

Egypt's equivalent is the Turin King List, or Royal Canon, in the Museo Egizio. It is a papyrus roughly 1.8 metres long, catalogued as Cat. 1874, dated by the museum to 1279–1213 BCE — the reign of Ramesses II, in the Nineteenth Dynasty of the New Kingdom — and acquired in the Drovetti purchase of 1842. It is a list of kings compiled centuries after many of the reigns it records, which is the ordinary situation for this genre.

## Where the chains break

Four failure modes recur, and they are why early absolute dates are so often published as ranges rather than points.

- **Partial years.** A reign rarely starts on New Year's Day. Whether the remainder of the outgoing ruler's year is counted to the old reign or the new one changes every downstream date by one year, and the convention is not always recoverable.
- **Overlaps.** Co-rulers, contested successions and rival dynasties all produce reigns that ran simultaneously. Adding their lengths together inflates the chronology.
- **Gaps and damage.** These documents survive on clay and papyrus, and damage is normal. A missing reign length does not just lose one date; it disconnects everything before it from everything after it.
- **Argument.** As the Sumerian King List shows, these documents were often written to justify a claim. A compiler with a case to make may present simultaneous dynasties as consecutive ones.

Independent evidence is how the chains get checked. Excavated archives can attach a ruler to a place and a material record — UNESCO's inscription for Yin Xu, the late Shang capital near Anyang dated there to 1300–1046 BC, notes that inscriptions on oracle bones found at the site bear invaluable testimony to one of the world's oldest writing systems and to ancient beliefs and social systems. [How historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) covers the wider set of anchoring methods and how confidence varies between them.

## Era names and the long survival of reign-based counting

East Asian era-name systems are a refinement of the same idea: a named period, proclaimed by the court, within which years are numbered.

The National Diet Library of Japan's account of the Japanese calendar shows the convention in use. Japan's first calendar came from China by way of Korea; in the middle of the sixth century the Yamato court invited a priest from Paekche to teach calendar-making, astronomy and geography, and Japan is reported to have organised its first calendar in the twelfth year of Suiko, which the library gives as 604. The first reference to a calendar in any Japanese record is an entry in the Nihon Shoki, Japan's oldest chronicle, dated to the sixth month of the fourteenth year of Emperor Kinmei — 553. Calendar preparation, astronomy and divination were the business of a single government office, and from the end of the tenth century the calendar passed down through the Kamo family and astronomy through the Abe family.

Regnal dating also sits inside the origin of the Christian era itself. The standard reference account of calendars records that Dionysius Exiguus built his year count by continuing an existing Easter table whose years were numbered from the beginning of the reign of the emperor Diocletian — so one regnal era was replaced by grafting a new epoch onto it. The same source notes that even after the Christian era became common in medieval ecclesiastical writing, traditional dating by regnal years continued in civil use. The arithmetic consequences of that epoch are covered in [why the 1400s are the fifteenth century](/blog/how-to-count-centuries-correctly/), and the calendars underneath in [how calendars and dating systems work](/blog/how-calendars-and-dating-systems-work/).
