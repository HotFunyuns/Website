Not all tennis points are worth the same, and the scoring system is the reason. A game, a set and a match are each decided by margin rather than by total points won, so a player can lose more points than an opponent and still win comfortably. What follows from that arithmetic is the whole subject of this article: a small number of scorelines decide sets, and everything else is accounting.

The useful question is therefore not "how do I win more points" but "which points am I allowed to plan for, and what does the plan look like".

## Which points are actually big

A point is big when winning or losing it changes who is favoured to take the set. That definition rules a lot of tennis out.

The scorelines that qualify most of the time:

- **Break point.** The receiver is one point from taking the opponent's service game, which in most sets is the single largest swing available.
- **30-40 and 30-all.** 30-all is the fork in the road; 30-40 is the consequence of losing it.
- **Deuce and advantage.** A standard game keeps going until someone leads by two, so a deuce game can pile several decisive points on top of each other.
- **The first point after a break.** Consolidating a break is a different psychological job from earning one.
- **Mini-breaks inside a tie-break.** The ITF Rules of Tennis require a tie-break game to be won by a margin of two points, so the middle of a tie-break is a sequence of near-decisive points with no slack in it.

If you are new to the notation behind all of that, [tennis scoring for beginners](/blog/tennis-scoring-for-beginners/) sets out how games, sets and tie-breaks fit together before any of the tactics make sense.

## The second serve is the structural pressure point

The rules create one moment of forced risk in every service game, and it is worth reading the actual wording rather than the folklore.

Under the ITF Rules of Tennis, if the first service is a fault the server "shall serve again without delay from behind the same half of the court from which that fault was served". There is no third attempt. That is the entire mechanism: one retry, then the point is gone.

This makes the second serve a genuine trade rather than a technical exercise. Serve it too safely and a competent returner steps in and takes control of the rally before it starts. Serve it as hard as a first serve and you hand over points for nothing. On a break point the cost of the second option becomes very visible, because a double fault does not lose a rally, it loses a game.

Serve placement is also not a constant. A 2025 study in the *Journal of Sports Sciences* modelled men's singles serve strategy as a Nash-equilibrium problem across surfaces and reported that the highest-value placements differed between hard, clay and grass courts for both first and second serves. The takeaway for a player, or for someone building a career in a simulation, is that a big-point serve plan is surface-specific. What is right at a hard-court event is not automatically right three weeks later on clay.

## What the research says about deciding under pressure

Two strands of published work are relevant here, and both are more modest than the usual "clutch" commentary.

The first looks directly at serving behaviour at break points. Meffert and colleagues, publishing in the *European Journal of Sport Science* in 2018, analysed 28,843 points from the 2016 Wimbledon men's draw, separating 2,035 break points from 26,808 regular points in order to examine whether serve behaviour changes in the situation that carries the most presumed mental stress. The value of the study for a reader is less any single number than the framing: break points are treated in the literature as a distinguishable category of point, analysed separately, because players do not behave on them the way they behave elsewhere.

The second strand is about the decision rather than the stroke. A 2025 study in *Frontiers in Psychology* ran two experiments with 120 tennis players each, split into expert, skilled and novice groups, testing how the framing of a choice, the competitive state (leading or trailing) and time pressure affected risk-taking. The authors report that novices were the most susceptible to framing effects, that skilled players shifted toward very conservative choices under high time pressure, and that experts made the most stable decisions — keying their risk to whether they were ahead or behind, conservative when leading and aggressive when trailing, with comparatively little influence from framing or from the clock.

That is a usable model. The expert pattern is not "always attack the big point" and it is not "always play safe". It is: know the state of the match, and let the state set the risk.

## Turning that into a plan you can actually run

Three habits follow from the above and none of them require a better forehand.

**Decide before the point, not during it.** The framing research is essentially a study of what happens when a decision is made under time pressure by someone who had not already made it. A pre-committed plan — first serve wide, second serve to the body, first ball to the backhand — is a way of moving the decision to a moment when you are not standing at 30-40.

**Make the risk match the score.** Defending a break point on your own serve and chasing one on the opponent's serve are different situations with different downsides, and treating them identically is the mistake the expert group in that study does not make.

**Let your style decide the menu.** A counterpuncher and a serve-and-volley player have genuinely different best options on the same second serve. [Tennis playing styles](/blog/tennis-playing-styles/) walks through six of them and where each gets exposed, and [serve and volley strategy](/blog/serve-and-volley-strategy/) covers the one that most obviously changes what a second serve is for.
