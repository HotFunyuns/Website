Two fighters walk to the centre and their lead feet end up on the same side of the ring instead of opposite sides. Nothing has happened yet, and the fight is already a different fight.

An **orthodox** fighter puts the left foot forward, which places the left side nearer the opponent and keeps the stronger right side in the rear, where rotation and distance can accelerate it. A **southpaw** is the mirror image: right hand and right foot forward, leading with right jabs and following with a left cross or right hook. Orthodox is the normal stance for right-handers and by some distance the more common of the two; southpaw is the normal stance for left-handers.

When the two meet, the matchup is called an open stance. When two fighters share a stance, it is closed. Almost everything people say about southpaws is really a statement about open-stance matchups.

## What physically changes in an open stance

Three things, and they cascade.

**The lead feet collide.** In a closed-stance fight the lead feet sit diagonally opposite and each fighter circles away from the opponent's power hand more or less instinctively. In an open stance the lead feet are on the same line and compete for the outside position. Winning that foot position is worth more than any single punch in the exchange that follows, because it decides whose rear hand has a clear path.

**The rear hands line up.** Both fighters' power hands are now aimed down the same open channel. An orthodox fighter's right cross and a southpaw's left cross are travelling toward each other rather than past each other. That is why open-stance fights produce simultaneous landings and abrupt finishes more often than closed-stance ones.

**The lead-hand jab loses its usual job.** Jabs meet each other on the outside rather than finding a clean line to the centre. Much of the technical work in an open stance is about getting the lead hand to do something other than trade jabs — pawing, slipping outside, or clearing the opponent's lead hand to create a path.

## The advantage is smaller than the folklore

The standard claim is that southpaws have an edge. The honest version is more qualified.

Many advantages have been theorised for left-handedness in sport. Many studies have found no impact at the professional level. What survives that is a narrower and more believable claim: **the advantage is a familiarity effect, and familiarity effects shrink with exposure.**

Because orthodox is the most common stance, a fighter's training hours are mostly spent against orthodox opponents, and the habits built there are what a southpaw disrupts. But that cuts both ways. A southpaw has spent their entire career fighting orthodox opponents, so the open-stance matchup is the one they know best — which is exactly why southpaw-versus-southpaw fights are so often scrappy: neither fighter has much experience of the closed version.

Orthodox has two structural advantages worth stating against the folklore. Its ubiquity means high-quality coaching in the stance is far more widely available. And with the left side forward, the lead hand and foot sit close to the opponent's right side, where the liver is a highly vulnerable target.

## How the open-stance problem gets solved

There is a reasonably settled technical answer, and it is mostly about feet.

1. **Take the outside foot position.** Step so your lead foot is outside theirs. From there your rear hand has a clear line and theirs does not.
2. **Circle toward their lead hand, away from their power hand.** Against a southpaw, an orthodox fighter generally circles to their own left. The instinct built over years of closed-stance sparring says the opposite, which is precisely the habit that gets punished.
3. **Use the lead hand to clear, not to score.** Controlling or displacing the opponent's lead hand opens the channel for the rear hand.
4. **Attack the lead leg.** In mixed martial arts, the calf and lead leg are exposed in an open stance in a way they are not in a closed one, and a compromised lead leg takes away the foot position the whole matchup rests on.

None of this is exotic knowledge. It is simply difficult to execute against someone who has practised the same exchange from the other side several thousand more times than you have.

## Stance as a matchup, not an attribute

When we built the fighter model for [40-0 MMA & Boxing Fight Draft](/apps/mma-boxing-fight-draft/), the first version treated stance as an attribute — a property of a fighter, sitting on the card alongside power, defence, cardio, reach, grappling and fight IQ.

It produced an obviously wrong result. Because southpaws are rarer, "southpaw" behaved like a bonus, and the optimal draft strategy became: take southpaws. The game had accidentally asserted that left-handed fighters are better, which is not a claim the evidence supports and not a claim we wanted the model making.

What shipped treats stance as a **matchup modifier**. Stance contributes nothing to a fighter's own quality. It is evaluated when two fighters are paired: same stance, no modifier; opposite stance, a modifier that depends on how much open-stance exposure each fighter's profile implies. A southpaw facing an orthodox opponent gains something from unfamiliarity. The same southpaw facing another southpaw does not — and may be slightly worse off.

The practical consequence at the draft board is one that catches people out: **you cannot stockpile stance.** Drafting five southpaws does not give you a stable of five advantaged fighters. It gives you five fighters whose modifier depends entirely on who the matchmaker puts in front of them, which is a different thing to plan around. [MMA fantasy draft strategy](/blog/mma-fantasy-draft-strategy/) covers how that changes a board.

**What the model does not represent:** switch-hitting. A fighter in the game has one stance for the duration of a bout. Real fighters who change stance mid-fight are doing something the simulator has no vocabulary for, and modelling it credibly would mean modelling the moment of transition — the brief window where footwork and defence are both unsettled — which our round-level resolution is simply too coarse to express. It is a known limitation, not a simplification we are pleased with.
