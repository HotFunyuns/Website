Annotating is the PDF version of a pen in the margin: highlight what matters, write a note, circle a mistake, stamp "approved". It is how documents get reviewed, studied and corrected. This guide covers the main annotation tools, how annotations differ from the page itself, and the one mistake that turns markup into a data leak.

## Annotations sit on top of the page

Wikipedia's PDF article reflects a separation between a page and the marks made on it in two places: the permissions an author can restrict list **adding or modifying text notes** separately from modifying the document, and the XFDF format can carry a document's **forms and annotations** as data on their own. That separation is the key idea: an annotation is a layer **on top of** the page, not a change to it. You can add a highlight, delete it and the text underneath is exactly as it was.

## The main tools

| Tool | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| Highlight | Marking passages that matter |
| Note or comment | Explaining something without changing the page |
| Text | Adding words on the page, such as a label or a correction |
| Drawing or pen | Circling, underlining or sketching freehand |
| Marks and stamps | Ticks, crosses or a standard stamp such as "approved" |

## Annotating for study

For reading and revision, annotations work best with a simple system:

1. **One colour for key points**, another for things you do not understand.
2. **Notes for summaries**: a one-line note at the top of each section saves rereading.
3. **Drawings for connections**: an arrow linking a definition to the example that uses it.

Fewer, deliberate marks beat a page highlighted from top to bottom.

## Annotating for review

When marking up someone else's document, the aim is that they can act on every mark without asking you what it means:

- **Point and explain**: highlight the text and add a note saying what is wrong and what you suggest.
- **Be specific**: "total on page 4 does not match the table on page 6" beats "check numbers".
- **State your colours** at the top, for example yellow for questions and red for errors.
- **Send the annotated copy and the clean original**, named so they cannot be confused.

## Annotating on a small screen

A phone makes markup fiddly, so a few habits help:

- **Zoom before you mark**: highlights land more precisely on enlarged text.
- **Use a stylus for drawings**, or turn the phone sideways for a wider page.
- **Use undo freely** rather than erasing mark by mark.
- **Write notes, not essays**: long comments are easier to read as a short summary note at the start of the document.
- **Save regularly**, so an interruption does not lose a review session.

## A worked example: one contract, three passes

You receive a 12-page agreement to check. Pass one: highlight every date, amount and deadline in one colour, which gives you a map of the obligations. Pass two: add a note to each highlight that does not match what you agreed. Pass three: stamp or mark the pages you accept. The result is a document the other party can work through note by note, and your clean copy is untouched for signing later.

## A black box is not a redaction

The layer that makes annotations convenient also makes them dangerous for hiding information. Wikipedia's article on redaction explains that where text in a PDF or word-processing file is covered by **overlaying graphical elements, usually black rectangles**, the **original text remains in the file** and can be uncovered by simply deleting the overlay. It adds that effective redaction of an electronic document requires the **removal of all relevant text and image data** from the file. Hidden data is not only under black boxes: the Federation of American Scientists' summary of the US National Security Agency's December 2005 guidance, *Redacting with Confidence*, quotes its warning that merely converting a Word document to PDF does not remove all sensitive metadata automatically.

So never use a drawing, highlight or stamp to hide an account number or a name.
