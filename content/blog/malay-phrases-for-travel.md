If you have one evening before the flight, learn six words: **boleh** (can), **ada** (there is, do you have), **tolong** (please, help), **berapa** (how much, how many), **di mana** (where) and **terima kasih** (thank you). Bolt any noun onto those and you have a functioning sentence, because Malay verbs do not change for person, tense or number. *Ada teh?* Do you have tea. *Boleh?* Can you. *Berapa?* How much. That is most of a trip.

Everything below is the expanded version, grouped the way you will actually need it.

## Arriving, and the ten words that open doors

**Selamat pagi** — good morning. **Selamat tengah hari** — good afternoon, around midday. **Selamat petang** — good evening, late afternoon. **Selamat malam** — good night.

**Terima kasih** is thank you; **sama-sama** is the reply. **Maaf** covers both sorry and excuse me. **Ya** and **tidak** are yes and no, and in speech *tidak* is almost always shortened to **tak**.

**Encik** (sir), **puan** (madam) and **cik** (miss) are the polite forms of address. Using one before a question costs you nothing and changes the register of the exchange completely.

And **tak apa** — never mind, it is fine — is the phrase you will hear most and use most. It smooths over everything.

A pronunciation note that saves a lot of confusion: in Peninsular Malaysia the final *a* of a word is usually not the *a* of "father". *Apa* comes out closer to "ah-puh", *saya* closer to "sah-yuh". The spelling is not lying to you; the vowel just relaxes at the end of a word. The [Malay pronunciation guide](/blog/malay-pronunciation-guide/) works through that and the other spelling-to-sound patterns, which are unusually reliable once you know them.

## Money, and the exchange you will repeat most

**Berapa harga ini?** — how much is this? Shortened in practice to **Berapa ini?** or just **Berapa?** with a gesture.

You need to hear the answer, so learn the numbers properly: *satu, dua, tiga, empat, lima, enam, tujuh, lapan, sembilan, sepuluh.* Then *belas* for the teens (*sebelas*, *dua belas*), *puluh* for tens (*dua puluh*), *ratus* for hundreds (*tiga ratus*). Prices come in **ringgit** and **sen**.

**Mahal** is expensive. **Murah** is cheap. **Boleh kurang sikit?** — can you make it a little less? — is the polite opening of a negotiation where one is appropriate, which in a fixed-price shop it is not.

**Ada?** on its own, pointing at a thing, means "do you have this". It is the single highest-leverage word in the list.

## Eating

**Saya nak ...** — I want ... — is the everyday ordering form. **Saya hendak ...** is the fuller standard version and nobody will mind either.

- **Sedap** — delicious. Say it and mean it.
- **Pedas** — spicy. **Kurang pedas** — less spicy. **Tidak pedas** — not spicy.
- **Air** — water, pronounced roughly "ah-yer", not like the English word. **Air kosong** is plain water; **air suam** is warm water.
- **Bungkus** — wrapped up to take away. Essential at a hawker stall.
- **Kira** — literally "count it up", the normal way to ask for the bill at a casual place. **Boleh saya minta bil?** is the full polite version.
- **Halal** and **tidak halal** you will see on signage rather than need to say.

On allergies, a piece of advice that is worth more than fluency: **use the blunt construction.** *Saya tidak boleh makan kacang* — I cannot eat peanuts — is unambiguous to any listener. The more idiomatic *saya alah kepada kacang* is correct Malay, but if you mispronounce it under pressure you may be understood as expressing a preference. For anything that matters medically, choose the sentence that cannot be softened by accident, and carry it written down as well.

## Getting around

- **Di mana ...?** — where is ...? **Di mana tandas?** — where is the toilet? (*Tandas* on signage; *bilik air* also appears.)
- **Saya nak pergi ke ...** — I want to go to ...
- **Berhenti di sini** — stop here.
- **Kiri** left, **kanan** right, **lurus** straight on, **dekat** near, **jauh** far.
- **Stesen** station, **bas** bus, **keretapi** train, **lapangan terbang** airport.
- **Jam berapa?** — what time?

Notice how many of those are recognisable if you read them aloud: *stesen*, *bas*, *teksi*, *tiket*, *hotel*, *polis*, *doktor*, *hospital*. Malaysian Malay took a large layer of its modern vocabulary from English, which is why a traveller's reading vocabulary is bigger than their speaking one from day one. Indonesian took the equivalent layer largely from Dutch, which is the main reason a phrase list learned for Malaysia is not quite a phrase list for Indonesia — our [Malay and Indonesian comparison](/blog/malay-vs-indonesian-differences/) sets out where that divergence sits.

## Trouble

- **Tolong!** — help.
- **Panggil polis** — call the police.
- **Saya perlukan doktor** — I need a doctor.
- **Saya sesat** — I am lost.
- **Saya tidak faham** — I do not understand.
- **Boleh cakap perlahan-lahan?** — can you speak slowly?
- **Boleh cakap Bahasa Inggeris?** — can you speak English?

Learn *saya tidak faham* early and use it without embarrassment. The alternative — nodding through an explanation you did not follow — is how travellers end up on the wrong bus.

## Why the phrases hold their shape

One structural fact does more for a traveller than any vocabulary list: **Malay verbs are not inflected for person, tense or number, and there is no grammatical gender.** *Saya nak* and *dia nak* use the same *nak*. Yesterday, today and tomorrow use the same verb, with a time word doing the work instead.

This is why a Malay phrase list behaves better than a French or Russian one. What you memorise is what you say. The complexity in Malay is elsewhere — in the affix system that builds words like *pembelian* and *dilarang* out of roots, which is a reading problem rather than a speaking one, and which the [affixes guide](/blog/malay-affixes-guide/) takes apart.

## Where English is already enough, and where Malay changes things

Straightforwardly: in central Kuala Lumpur, in Penang, in Johor Bahru and in Singapore, you can complete nearly every transaction you will attempt in English. If your question is whether you *need* Malay to travel comfortably, the answer is no, and an article that pretended otherwise would be selling you something.

What changes is everything that is not a transaction. The stall holder who has been answering tourists in English all day and gets a *terima kasih, encik*. The taxi driver on a long ride out of the city. The small-town shop where English is genuinely a second language rather than a default. In those moments a handful of phrases does not improve the outcome — you were going to get the food either way — it improves the exchange, and that is a reasonable thing to spend an evening on.
