**Every player drafted into the NFL signs a four-year contract, an undrafted rookie signs for three years, and a first-round pick's deal also carries a club option for a fifth year.** How much a drafted rookie earns is mostly settled before his name is called. The 2020 collective bargaining agreement caps what all clubs together may spend on each rookie class and divides that money by draft position, so the slot a player is taken in largely sets his pay.

The clearest way to see how the rules fit together is in the order a player meets them: draft night, signing, three locked seasons, and then a fork that depends on the round he was picked in.

## Draft night: the slot sets the price

Article 7 of the agreement builds rookie pay from the top down.

- **A league-wide limit.** The Total Rookie Compensation Pool is "the League-wide limit on the total amount of Rookie Salary" all clubs may commit to a class over the whole length of the contracts. A separate Year-One pool limits first-season pay, and it grows each year in line with the percentage rise in the salary cap, subject to a guaranteed minimum increase.
- **A club allocation.** Each club's share of the pool is "calculated based upon the number, round and position of the Club's selection choices in the Draft". Trading up or down changes the money a club has for rookies as well as the players available to it.
- **A figure for every pick.** Each selection carries a Year-One Formula Allotment, a fraction of the pool set by round and position, agreed by the league and the players' union and shared with clubs, rookies and agents. Each pick also has a minimum below which the player cannot be signed.

Clubs may pay a rookie more than his formula figure, but only if they have room left in their allocation, and no club may sign rookies whose combined pay exceeds it. The agreement calls the arrangement "an absolute maximum limit" on what each draft class can earn. That is what "slotting" means: the position of the pick, not a negotiation over the player's merits, sets most of the price.

Two practical consequences follow. A drafted player counts against his club's cap at the minimum salary from draft day until he signs. And the round affects more than money: the order in which picks are made, and how compensatory picks are added, is covered in [how draft order works](/blog/how-football-draft-order-works/).

## Signing: what a rookie contract may contain

The agreement fixes the length — "four years for Rookies selected in the first round of the Draft, with a Club option for a fifth year", four years for rounds two to seven including compensatory picks, and three years for undrafted rookies. It then limits what can go in the contract.

| Allowed | Not allowed |
| --- | --- |
| Signing bonus, base salary, roster and reporting bonuses, permitted playtime incentives, salary guarantees | Option bonuses and option fees |
| Minimum offseason workout per diem from year two | Most salary advances |
| The fifth-year option (round one) or the Proven Performance Escalator (rounds two to seven) | Voidable years, and buybacks of voidable years |
| | A "contract within the contract" |

Two further rules shape every rookie deal.

**The 25% Increase Rule.** Rookie salary cannot rise by more than 25 percent of the player's first-year rookie salary in any year, unless the contract pays only the minimum each season. The agreement's own example: a second-round pick with a $400,000 signing bonus and a $500,000 first-year salary has a first-year rookie salary of $600,000 — the $100,000 slice of bonus plus the salary — so his rookie salary may rise by no more than $150,000 a year.

**Four-year proration.** A rookie's signing bonus is prorated in equal amounts over the contract, with a maximum of four years. For first-round picks the fifth, option year is left out of that split.

## Seasons one to three: locked in

A drafted rookie's contract "may not be renegotiated, amended or altered in any way until after the final regular season game of the player's third contract year". Undrafted rookies wait until after their second season. Until then, the only things that move the money are those written in at signing.

Incentives are one of them, and they are narrower than in veteran deals. The league's contract guide explains that rookie incentives are based only on playing time and must name a fixed percentage of plays. First- and second-round picks cannot be rewarded below 35 percent of plays in year one or 45 percent later; for third-round picks onward and undrafted players the floors are 15 and 30 percent. Under the cap rules, any incentive in the first year of a rookie contract counts as "likely to be earned".

## After season three: two different paths

### Rounds two to seven: the Proven Performance Escalator

The escalator is written into every contract for picks in rounds two to seven; first-round picks and undrafted players cannot earn it. It raises the player's fourth-year base salary if he meets one of three qualifiers, and if he meets more than one he receives only the highest escalator. For a second-round pick, whose own-round tender already carries second-round compensation, Level Two (that tender plus $250,000) pays more than Level Three.

| Level | Qualifier in the first three regular seasons | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One | Round 2: at least 60% of his side's plays in any two seasons, or a 60% cumulative average. Rounds 3–7: the same at 35% | Tops up the year-four salary to the restricted-free-agent tender tied to his original draft round |
| Two | At least 55% of plays in each of the three seasons | Tops it up to that same tender plus $250,000 |
| Three | Selected to a Pro Bowl on the original ballot | Tops it up to the restricted-free-agent tender that carries a second-round pick as compensation |

The design rewards exactly what a later pick has to earn — a regular place in the line-up — and it does so a year before he can renegotiate.

### Round one: the fifth-year option

For a first-round pick, the club holds "the unilateral right to extend from four years to five years the term" of the contract. It must give notice after the final regular-season game of the player's third season and before May 3 of the following league year, according to the agreement; the league's own contract guide gives the deadline as before May 1, so check the current year's notice before relying on either date.

The option salary is fixed by formula, not negotiated. For players drafted in 2018 or later, it depends on Pro Bowl selections on the original ballot and playing time over his first three seasons, measured at the position where he took part in the most plays in his third season.

| Player's first three seasons | Fifth-year salary |
| --- | --- |
| No Pro Bowl, and below the playing-time thresholds | Transition-tag method, averaging the 3rd to 25th highest salaries at his position |
| No Pro Bowl, but 75% of plays in two seasons, a 75% cumulative average, or 50% in every season | The same method using the 3rd to 20th highest salaries |
| One Pro Bowl | The transition tag for his position |
| Two or more Pro Bowls | The franchise tag for his position |

Once exercised, the option-year salary becomes fully guaranteed for skill, injury and cap purposes, and any part of the fourth-year salary that was not already guaranteed becomes guaranteed too. That is what makes the decision real: a club exercising the option in the spring of year four is committing to a guaranteed fifth-year salary at a price it can calculate in advance.

## What the timeline means for each side

For a club, a first-round pick who becomes a starter is a cost-controlled player for up to five seasons, with the most expensive year known before it has to decide. A pick who has not become a regular presents a different calculation, because exercising the option guarantees money for a season that may not be worth it. Neither outcome is predictable on draft night, which is why [the draft simulator guide](/blog/football-draft-gm-guide/) treats every pick as a decision under uncertainty.

For a mid-round player, the snap counts in the escalator table are the clearest route to more money before he can renegotiate. For an undrafted player, the shorter three-year contract and the earlier renegotiation date are the trade-off for starting outside the pool.
