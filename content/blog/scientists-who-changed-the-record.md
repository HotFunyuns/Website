Lists of the most influential scientists in history are really lists of who got the credit, and those are different things. Discovery is usually distributed across several people and often several laboratories; credit is awarded to one, two or at most three. This article works through documented cases where the gap between those two facts is visible in the archives, because the mechanism is more instructive than any ranking.

## The rules that shape the record

Start with the constraints, because a surprising amount of what looks like injustice is partly arithmetic.

The Nobel Foundation's statutes say that if a rewarded work was produced by two or three people, the prize is awarded to them jointly, and that in no case may a prize amount be divided between more than three persons. That cap has no relationship to how many people actually did the work; modern experimental papers routinely carry dozens of authors.

The posthumous rule is more interesting than it is usually reported. From 1974 the statutes bar a posthumous award unless the death occurred after the announcement. Before 1974, posthumous prizes were possible and had been given twice — to Erik Axel Karlfeldt in literature in 1931 and Dag Hammarskjöld for peace in 1961. So the sentence you often see, that a scientist who died before the award "could not" have received it, is an anachronism when applied to anything before 1974.

Those two rules — a hard cap of three, and a posthumous bar that did not formally exist until 1974 — do a lot of work in the cases below.

## DNA: three papers, one story

The April 1953 issue of *Nature* carried three related papers back to back. James Watson and Francis Crick proposed the double-helical structure. Maurice Wilkins, Alexander Stokes and Herbert Wilson published on the molecular structure of deoxypentose nucleic acids. Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling published on molecular configuration in sodium thymonucleate. The model and the X-ray evidence appeared together, from two different institutions.

The 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded jointly to Crick, Watson and Wilkins for their discoveries concerning the molecular structure of nucleic acids and its significance for information transfer in living material. Three names, which is the maximum. Franklin had died in 1958, at thirty-seven.

The popular account that grew up afterwards is that Watson was shown Franklin's X-ray image without her knowledge, understood at a glance what she had failed to understand for months, and stole the discovery. In 2023, Matthew Cobb and Nathaniel Comfort argued in *Nature* that this version is wrong. Working in Franklin's archive at Churchill College, Cambridge, they found a previously unstudied 1953 draft article written in consultation with Franklin for *Time* magazine, and an overlooked letter from one of her colleagues to Crick. Their reading is that Franklin understood what her data showed and that the two groups were working in parallel with some awareness of each other's progress, rather than one of them being robbed by stealth.

This is the most useful thing in the whole subject. The correction to a bad story turned out to be a second, different story — and it came from unpublished papers in an archive, not from rethinking the published record. That is the general point made in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/): what changes a settled account is usually new evidence rather than new opinion. [How we know what historical figures actually did](/blog/how-we-know-what-historical-figures-did/) sets out the same evidence ladder for claims about individuals.

## Pulsars: what a citation says and does not say

In 1967 a research student at Cambridge, Jocelyn Bell Burnell, noticed an anomalous repeating signal in chart recordings from a radio telescope she had helped build, and pursued it until it was identified as a new class of object.

The 1974 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded jointly to Martin Ryle and Antony Hewish for pioneering research in radio astrophysics — Ryle for aperture synthesis, and Hewish, in the official wording, "for his decisive role in the discovery of pulsars". Bell Burnell is not among the laureates.

The value of this case is that no archival detective work is needed. The prize citation is published, it names two people, and the phrase "decisive role" is doing visible work. Whatever view you take of the committee's decision, the document records a choice about whose role was decisive, and that choice is the thing worth examining.

## Fission: a division of labour, an undivided prize

The Nobel Prize in Chemistry for 1944 was awarded to Otto Hahn, alone, for his discovery of the fission of heavy nuclei. The chemical identification of barium among the products of neutron bombardment was Hahn's work with Fritz Strassmann. The physical interpretation — that the nucleus had split, and what that implied energetically — came from Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch, working from exile after Meitner had fled Nazi Germany.

An experiment that produces an unexpected result and a theory that explains it are two halves of one discovery. The prize went to one half. The Nobel Foundation's own record notes a further wrinkle: the 1944 chemistry prize was not awarded in its own year and was given to Hahn in 1945 under the statute allowing a reserved prize.

## The name for the pattern

In 1993 the historian of science Margaret Rossiter published "The Matthew Matilda Effect in Science" in *Social Studies of Science*. She took Robert Merton's "Matthew effect" — the observation that recognition accrues disproportionately to those who already have it — and identified a gendered version of it, naming it for Matilda Joslyn Gage, a nineteenth-century writer who had described the phenomenon long before anyone measured it.

What makes Rossiter's paper more than a complaint is that it treats misattribution as a structural process with identifiable mechanisms: who is listed as an author, who presents at conferences, who is cited, who writes the textbooks, and who is available to be interviewed when a journalist needs a face for a discovery. Each of those is a place where credit is assigned, and each can be examined.

## Why individual attribution is usually wrong anyway

Two more things are worth saying about the whole framework.

The first is multiple discovery. Independent simultaneous discovery is common enough in the history of science to be unremarkable: calculus, natural selection, the telephone, oxygen. When a field's tools and questions make a result reachable, several people usually reach it. A prize for one of them is a statement about timing and visibility as much as insight.

The second is that a scientist is not a lone mind. The Royal Society's motto, adopted in its charter in 1662, is *Nullius in verba* — take nobody's word for it. The whole point of the institutional apparatus that grew up around that motto, journals and referees and repeated experiments, is that a claim only becomes knowledge once other people have worked on it. A system deliberately built to make knowledge collective is then asked to hand out medals to individuals, and the friction between those two things is exactly where credit disputes live.

## One defensible superlative

Very few "greatest scientist" claims survive contact with evidence. One narrow claim does: Marie Skłodowska Curie remains the only person to have won Nobel Prizes in two different sciences, in physics in 1903 and in chemistry in 1911. The Nobel Prize's own biographical record describes her as the first person ever to be awarded two Nobel Prizes.

State it exactly that way and it is true. Stretch it — to "the only person to win two Nobel Prizes", which is false — and it joins the enormous body of science history that is repeated confidently and checked rarely. Checking is the whole job, and it is a habit worth carrying into every claim in this field, including the ones in this article.
