The bands on an IQ score chart are lines a publisher drew across a continuous curve. They are not discovered categories, there is no natural break anywhere along the scale, and the same number can carry different words depending on which chart you are reading. Understanding how the lines get placed is more useful than memorising any particular set of them.

Underneath every such chart is one distribution and two parameters, and once you can see those, the labels stop being mysterious.

## The curve the bands are drawn on

Scores from the major individually administered batteries are expressed on a standard-score metric with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15 — a convention stated explicitly in the psychometric literature, including a 2026 meta-analysis of Wechsler profiles that standardised its effect sizes against exactly that scale.

The shape is the normal distribution. NIST's statistical handbook gives the proportions that shape forces, and they are the real content of any score chart:

- 68.27 per cent of the distribution lies within one standard deviation of the mean
- 95.45 per cent lies within two
- 99.73 per cent lies within three

Translate those onto a scale centred on 100 with a standard deviation of 15 and the familiar landmarks appear without anyone having to look anything up. About 68 per cent of the norm sample sits between 85 and 115. About 95 per cent sits between 70 and 130. About 99.7 per cent sits between 55 and 145.

Those percentages are properties of the curve, not facts about people. They would hold for any variable forced onto a normal metric.

## Why a band is the width it is

Band widths are chosen for convenience. Ten points is a common choice; so is fifteen. On this scale, fifteen points is exactly one standard deviation and ten points is two-thirds of one. Nothing in the measurement requires either.

This has a consequence that charts rarely mention: bands near the middle of the scale contain enormously more people than bands of identical width near the ends. The stretch from 90 to 110 — two bands at ten-point spacing — covers close to half the comparison group. The stretch from 130 to 150, the same twenty points wide, covers a little over two per cent. Equal width, wildly unequal population. The relationship between a score's position and its percentile rank is worked through in [percentile, standard score and raw score](/blog/percentiles-and-standard-scores/).

## Who decides the labels

The descriptive words — the adjectives attached to each range — are editorial decisions by the publisher of a particular instrument. They differ between publishers, and they have been revised within the same instrument family across editions, generally away from older clinical terminology and towards neutral descriptions of position.

Three practical consequences follow.

**A label is not portable.** Two instruments can attach different words to the same number, so quoting a band without naming the test is close to meaningless.

**A label is not a diagnosis.** Descriptive classifications summarise where a score sits. They are not clinical conclusions, and no responsible report treats them as one.

**A label is not a threshold effect.** Nothing changes at 119 that does not change at 120. The boundary is a line on a chart.

## The one boundary that does carry weight

There is a real exception, and it is worth stating carefully because it shows both how a cut point can matter and how fragile it is.

Criteria for intellectual disability have traditionally been anchored to performance approximately two standard deviations below the mean — in standard-score terms, roughly 70 on an individually administered measure. A study of Wechsler-based classification in *Developmental Medicine & Child Neurology* describes that convention directly, and then immediately complicates it: because of the standard error of measurement, the authors note that people scoring in the low 70s may in some cases be better conceptualised within the impaired range, and they treat a score around 75 as the upper end of the margin of error around the criterion.

That is the clearest available illustration of why a band edge should never be read as a wall. A cut point that carries clinical and legal consequences is still surrounded by measurement error wide enough to move an individual case across it.

## The error bar the chart leaves out

ETS describes reliability as the extent to which scores are consistent across occasions of testing, across editions of a test, and across raters scoring the same responses. What is left over is measurement error, summarised as the standard error of measurement.

A score chart never shows it. That is the chart's central omission: it renders a number as a location with hard edges when the number is really an estimate with a range around it. A competent report gives that range, and a reader who only ever sees the point estimate is missing the part of the result that governs how much confidence it deserves.

## Reading a chart without being misled by it

Ask which instrument the chart belongs to, what the standard deviation is, and what confidence interval the report attaches. Treat the label as shorthand for a position, not as a category anyone belongs to. And remember that the most striking thing about the curve is how much of it is crowded into the middle.
