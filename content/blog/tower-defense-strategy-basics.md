The strongest tower you can afford, placed badly, does less work than a cheap one placed well. That is the whole of tower defence strategy compressed into a sentence, and it is the thing most players discover about twenty levels later than they need to. What decides a wave is not a tower's damage per second — it is damage per second multiplied by the number of seconds an enemy spends inside that tower's range. The first number is printed on the upgrade screen. The second one is hidden in the shape of the path.

## Time on target is the currency you are actually spending

Picture two identical towers with a four-second reload and enough damage to kill a goblin in three shots.

Tower A sits beside a straight stretch. An enemy enters its range, walks through, and is gone in three seconds. One shot lands. The goblin survives.

Tower B sits inside a hairpin. The same enemy enters its range, walks the outbound leg, turns, and comes back along the inbound leg — eleven seconds inside the circle. Three shots land. The goblin dies, and so do the two behind it.

Same tower, same money, same statistics. One of them is roughly three times as useful as the other, and nothing on the tower's stat card tells you which is which. This is why "what is the best tower" is the wrong opening question and "where does the path spend the most time" is the right one.

Tower defence is generally classified as a subgenre of strategy, descended from real-time strategy, and the genre's own definition puts the emphasis exactly there: the strategic choice is the positioning of defensive elements along the attackers' route. Everything else is downstream of that.

## Reading a map before you spend a coin

Before the first wave starts, trace the path with your eye and mark four things.

**The bends.** Every change of direction is a place where a circle of range covers two legs of path instead of one. Two bends close together are worth more than either alone, because a single tower can cover both.

**The switchbacks.** A path that doubles back parallel to itself is the single most valuable terrain in the genre. A tower dropped in the middle of the fold covers both lanes for their entire length.

**The junctions.** Where two routes converge, one tower does the work of two. Where they split, you need two of everything, so spend before the split rather than after it.

**The dead ground.** The long straight with nothing interesting near it. You will be tempted to build there because it is empty and it looks unguarded. Resist it until the profitable positions are full.

In [Regal Tower Defense: TD Game](/apps/regal-tower-defense/) this reading step is the design. The campaign runs fifty levels across ten worlds, from Royal Meadow through Frozen Crossing and Crystal Caverns to the Chaos Realm, and each world changes the path shapes rather than simply raising the numbers. We build the maps around **bend density** — how many direction changes sit within one tower's range — rather than around total path length, because path length by itself just means the wave takes longer to arrive.

We learned that the blunt way. One early level had a genuinely long route, which on paper should have made it forgiving. It was instead the most boring level we had built, because a single enormous straight made every tower type interchangeable: with that much dwell time, anything killed anything, and the choice between Frost and Lightning stopped meaning anything. We rebuilt it with two switchbacks and a junction, and it became a level where the same budget produces wildly different results depending on where it goes.

## The order you buy things in

Once the path is read, spending follows a fairly stable order.

1. **One tower on the best bend you can reach with the opening budget.** Not the entrance. The bend.
2. **A second tower covering the same corner from the other side**, so the overlap doubles rather than the coverage spreads.
3. **Upgrade that cluster** before opening a new one. Upgrades compound with geometry you have already bought.
4. **Only then extend**, and extend to the next-best bend rather than to the nearest empty tile.
5. **Keep a reserve.** A wave you can see coming is cheaper to answer before it arrives than after it leaks.

Step three is the one people skip. Upgrading is unglamorous next to building, and a new tower feels like progress in a way that a tier-two arrow tower does not. But an upgrade on a corner position buys damage on top of dwell time you already own, while a new tower on dead ground starts its accounting from zero.

The mirror-image mistake is upgrading on autopilot. If a tower is in a poor position, pouring money into it makes it a more expensive tower in a poor position. Move on.

## What kind of loss are you having?

When a level stops working, the useful question is not "how many got through" but "where did they get through".

**Leaks at the tail of a wave, near the exit.** Your coverage is fine and your throughput is not. The enemies that die are dying too late, which means everything behind them arrives at a defence that is still reloading. Upgrade the cluster you already have, or add a second tower into the same zone rather than a new zone.

**Leaks from the front of a wave, early on the path.** Something walked through a section untouched. That is a coverage gap, and upgrading at the far end cannot fix it — the enemy has already gone past. Find the unguarded stretch and cover it, even cheaply.

**Leaks only on certain waves.** That is a type problem, not a placement problem: armour, flight or an elite modifier is beating the damage you have chosen rather than the amount of it. That is a different diagnosis entirely, and [countering armoured, flying and elite enemies](/blog/countering-armoured-and-flying-enemies/) works through it.

Which of the seven attacking towers you are placing matters too, and it matters in a different way from how much you place — [what each tower type is actually for](/blog/tower-types-and-what-they-counter/) covers the roles. Once a cluster exists, support towers that buff their neighbours change the value of the whole layout again, which is its own subject in [aura towers and support stacking](/blog/aura-towers-and-support-stacking/).

## The thing that gets harder is not the enemy health bar

Games in this genre scale difficulty in two very different ways, and it is worth knowing which one you are up against. The crude way is to raise enemy health and count on your upgrades to keep pace, which produces a level that feels identical and takes longer. The better way is to change the *shape* of the problem: tighter maps, mixed wave composition, path layouts that punish a single cluster.

Balancing those knobs is the branch of game design concerned with adjusting challenge and reward to produce an intended experience, and the honest admission is that it is iterative rather than calculated — you build a level, you watch it get solved in a way you did not intend, and you change the map rather than the numbers. Our fifty levels introduce new hazards and path layouts world by world for exactly that reason. ## A short method you can apply to any level

- Look at the path before you look at the shop.
- Find the bend with the most path inside one range circle. Build there.
- Double up on that corner before you spread out.
- Upgrade good positions; relocate bad ones instead of feeding them.
- Read your leaks by location, not by count.
- Assume the wave that just beat you wants a different *type* of answer, not just a bigger one.
