Farming did not begin once and spread from there. It began separately in several parts of the world, with different crops, on different timetables, for reasons that do not appear to reduce to a single global cause. The definite article in "the agricultural revolution" is the most misleading word in the phrase, and dropping it makes the whole subject easier to understand.

## Not one revolution, and not one place

A 2025 analysis in *Scientific Reports* modelled the global transition to food production and worked with independent centres spread across four continents. In the Americas: southwestern Mesoamerica, where maize was domesticated; northwestern South America, associated with squash; and the Llanos de Moxos in Amazonian Bolivia, associated with manioc and squash. In Africa: a western centre producing pearl millet and African rice, and an eastern one producing sorghum, finger millet and teff. In Eurasia: the Fertile Crescent with wheat and barley, the Indian subcontinent with millets and beans, the Yellow River basin with millets, and the Yangtze basin with rice. In Oceania: the New Guinea highlands, associated with bananas.

Two things follow from that list. First, the staples differ, so the agricultural systems built on them differ — a rice economy and a maize economy impose different labour calendars, different settlement patterns and different vulnerabilities. Second, the idea of a single Neolithic "package" that travelled outward from Southwest Asia describes one regional story reasonably well and the others badly.

The same study concluded that no universal driver explains the global pattern. Region-specific bioclimatic factors proved critical instead: temperature patterns dominated in the Americas, while interactions between precipitation and temperature mattered more in Southwest Asia and Europe. Diffusion did real work once farming existed — spatial proximity accounted for a measurable share of the variance — but it did not create the centres.

## What "domestication" actually means, and why scholars argue about it

It is tempting to treat domestication as a self-evident category. It is not. A 2025 review in *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* opens by noting continuing confusion about "what domestication is, how it occurs or even what organisms are domesticated."

The disagreements are substantive. Did humans deliberately breed these species, or did the changes accumulate unconsciously as a by-product of harvesting and replanting? Did domestication happen quickly or over many centuries? Does landscape management — burning, weeding, transplanting — count? The review also notes that the argument has picked up ideological freight, with domestication read variously as evidence of human mastery over nature or as a template for domination, and it points out that even grammar takes sides: "they domesticated wheat" makes humans the agent in a way that a description of co-evolution does not. The authors propose a neutral definition anchored in heritable phenotypic change resulting from human interaction, independent of intent or tempo.

This matters for readers, not just specialists. When a popular account says farming "began" on a particular date, it is quietly choosing one of these definitions.

## What the evidence actually consists of

The physical record is less about fields than about tools, seeds and bones. Stone sickle blades used to harvest plants sit in the Smithsonian's Human Origins collection alongside cut-marked animal bone, projectile points and wooden spears — the evidence that, as that programme puts it, earlier people "had to hunt, scavenge, and gather food every day." Plant remains carbonised in hearths, phytoliths, starch grains on grinding stones, and the shifting shape of seeds and animal skeletons over time are what actually establish a transition.

Every one of those lines of evidence has to be dated, and dating is where most of the published disagreement lives. Our guide to [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) covers the methods and their error ranges; in this field those ranges are frequently wider than the differences being argued about.

## Why anyone would switch, given the costs

Farming supports more people per unit of land. It does not obviously improve life for the people doing it. It demands more labour per calorie, ties a community to one place, and concentrates risk into a small number of crops that can all fail in the same season.

Research published in *Nature* in 2024 gives this argument an empirical shape. Analysing 154 population decline periods across 16 global regions and 30,000 years of radiocarbon evidence, the authors found that agricultural and pastoral societies experienced downturns *more* frequently than hunter-gatherer populations — while also showing that populations exposed to frequent disturbance developed greater capacity to resist and recover from the next one. Farming was simultaneously more crisis-prone and more resilient after the fact.

That is a better answer than "farming was an improvement." It suggests a ratchet rather than a choice: once a population's density depends on cultivation, reverting is not available, and the system adapts to the shocks it keeps producing.

## Çatalhöyük, and what one site can and cannot show

Çatalhöyük in central Anatolia is the settlement most often used to illustrate the Neolithic, and it is worth understanding what kind of evidence it is. The Çatalhöyük Research Project, a twenty-five-year programme directed by Ian Hodder that concluded its field phase in 2018, generated an archive now held in Stanford's special collections, with excavation continuing under Ali Ozan of Pamukkale University.

A site excavated that intensively can support fine-grained claims about houses, diet, burial and symbolism. What it cannot do is stand for the Neolithic in general — it is one large settlement in one region, and the other independent centres produced nothing like it. Using it as the type-case for a global process is exactly the substitution this article is arguing against.
