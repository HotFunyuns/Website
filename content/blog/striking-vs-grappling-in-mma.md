The question is usually posed as "which is better", and the answer is that it is the wrong question. Mixed martial arts permits striking and grappling, standing and on the ground, drawing on techniques from a wide range of combat sports. In a ruleset that allows both, the discipline that matters most is the one that decides **where the fight happens** — and that is neither striking nor grappling exactly. It is the ability to impose a phase.

## Two disciplines, two different kinds of control

**Striking** controls distance and time. A striker wants to be at a range where their weapons reach and the opponent's do not, and wants exchanges to be short and repeated. Damage is the currency, and it can accumulate from the first second of a round.

**Grappling** controls position and space. Grappling is fighting that involves gripping the opponent — clinching, throwing, taking down, holding position, submitting — without striking being the primary tool. A grappler wants to remove the distance a striker depends on, and once the distance is gone, replace a contest of reflexes with a contest of leverage.

Those are not simply two ways of hurting someone. They are two different theories of what a fight is. And crucially, **only one of them can be true at a time.** A fight is either happening at striking range or it is not.

## Phase control is the actual skill

This is the reframing that makes the whole debate tractable.

Imagine two fighters: one is a substantially better striker, the other a substantially better grappler. If every second of the fight happens standing at range, the striker wins comfortably. If every second happens on the ground, the grappler does. The result is therefore decided almost entirely by what proportion of the fight occurs in each phase — and that proportion is decided by takedown ability and takedown defence.

This is the real reason wrestling is spoken about as the strongest base in the sport. Not because wrestling itself finishes fights, but because a wrestler with good takedowns and good takedown defence effectively **chooses the ruleset**. Everyone else has to accept it.

It also explains the two most common ways these matchups are actually won:

- **A striker beats a grappler by staying standing.** Sprawls, underhooks, hip position, fence awareness, and footwork that keeps them off the cage. Almost none of that is striking.
- **A grappler beats a striker by getting inside.** Level changes, pressure, entries from the clinch, using the fence. Almost none of that is submission skill.

The decisive skills in a striker-versus-grappler fight are usually defensive skills belonging to the other discipline.

## Where each one is fragile

**The striker's fragility is compounding.** Every failed takedown defence costs energy, and energy is what makes the next sprawl work. A striker who defends four takedowns and fails the fifth is often in far worse shape than the raw count suggests, because the fifth one lands against a tired body.

**The grappler's fragility is front-loaded.** Entries happen at striking range, which means a grappler has to travel through the striker's best real estate to reach their own. A shot taken from too far out is a shot taken with the head low and the chin exposed.

Neither fragility is a flaw to be coached away. They are the costs each approach pays for the control it seeks.

## What the judges do with it

The scoring layer matters here, because a lot of fights in this matchup go to a decision.

Judging guidance treats effective striking and effective grappling together as the primary criterion, with legal strikes that inflict damage counted alongside successful takedowns, reversals and submission attempts. Judges are also asked to take each round on its own merits rather than weigh the cumulative impact of strikes across a fight.

The practical reading is that grappling scores when it is *offensive*. A takedown followed by sustained work scores; a takedown followed by stalling in a neutral position is a much weaker case, and it is the single most common source of arguments about a scorecard. [How MMA judging criteria work](/blog/how-mma-judging-criteria-work/) goes through the hierarchy in detail.

## Deciding where the fight happens first

The biggest realism fix we ever made to [40-0 MMA & Boxing Fight Draft](/apps/mma-boxing-fight-draft/) was not an attribute change. It was reordering the resolution.

The original model resolved an exchange by comparing relevant attributes and producing an outcome. It weighed striking, wrestling, takedowns, submissions and ground control, all in one pass. It looked reasonable and it was badly wrong in one specific way: **it systematically overrated strikers.** A fighter with excellent striking numbers and poor takedown defence would beat a wrestler more often than they should, because their striking numbers were contributing to every exchange regardless of whether the exchange was one they should have been allowed to have.

What shipped resolves in two stages. **First, the model decides the phase** — does this exchange happen standing, in the clinch, or on the ground — using takedown ability against takedown defence, with fatigue applied. **Only then** does it resolve the exchange, using the attributes that are actually relevant to that phase. A striker's striking is worth nothing in an exchange that is happening from bottom position.

The change in behaviour was immediate and, for a while, unpopular with testers who had built stables of high-rated strikers. It is also the closest the model has come to reproducing something the sport genuinely does, which is why it stayed.

**A limitation worth naming.** The phase decision is made per exchange, not continuously, so the simulator represents a fight as a sequence of discrete situations rather than as a flowing contest. Scrambles — the transitional moments where neither fighter has position and the fight is genuinely in between phases — are the thing this model cannot express. They are resolved into whichever phase came next, which loses a real and important part of the sport.
