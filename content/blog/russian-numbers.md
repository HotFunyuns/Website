Russian numbers have two layers. The first is the words themselves, which are regular once you see how they are built. The second is the grammar: in Russian the number decides the **case and number of the noun after it**, so "one rouble", "two roubles" and "five roubles" use three different forms of the noun. This reference covers both, using Wikipedia's articles on Russian grammar and Russian declension. Romanisations are a simplified BGN/PCGN style, with ' for the soft sign, and are only a guide; stress is shown with an accent on the Cyrillic.

## Zero to ten

| Number | Russian | Romanised |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | ноль / нуль | nol' / nul' |
| 1 | оди́н, одна́, одно́ | odin, odna, odno |
| 2 | два, две | dva, dve |
| 3 | три | tri |
| 4 | четы́ре | chetyre |
| 5 | пять | pyat' |
| 6 | шесть | shest' |
| 7 | семь | sem' |
| 8 | во́семь | vosem' |
| 9 | де́вять | devyat' |
| 10 | де́сять | desyat' |

Two of these change form. **One** agrees with its noun in gender: *оди́н* (masculine), *одна́* (feminine), *одно́* (neuter), and even has a plural, *одни́*. **Two** is *два* with masculine and neuter nouns and *две* with feminine ones. Wikipedia adds that *раз* is used for "one" when counting.

## Eleven to a million

The teens all end in *-надцать*: *оди́ннадцать* (11), *двена́дцать* (12), *трина́дцать* (13), *четы́рнадцать* (14), *пятна́дцать* (15), *шестна́дцать* (16), *семна́дцать* (17), *восемна́дцать* (18), *девятна́дцать* (19).

| Tens | | Hundreds | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 20 | два́дцать | 100 | сто |
| 30 | три́дцать | 200 | две́сти |
| 40 | со́рок | 300 | три́ста |
| 50 | пятьдеся́т | 400 | четы́реста |
| 60 | шестьдеся́т | 500 | пятьсо́т |
| 70 | се́мьдесят | 600 | шестьсо́т |
| 80 | во́семьдесят | 700 | семьсо́т |
| 90 | девяно́сто | 800 | восемьсо́т |
| | | 900 | девятьсо́т |

As the table shows, forty (*со́рок*) and ninety (*девяно́сто*) do not end in *-дцать* or *-десят* like their neighbours, so they need learning on their own. Compound numbers are simply listed, with no "and": Wikipedia's example is *сто пятьдеся́т три ры́бы*, "153 fish".

Above that come *ты́сяча* (1,000), *миллио́н* (1,000,000) and *миллиа́рд* (1,000,000,000). Wikipedia notes these behave as nouns, which is why "two thousand" is *две ты́сячи* and "five thousand" is *пять ты́сяч*, following the rule in the next section. The leading "one" can be dropped: *ты́сяча и одна́ ночь*, "a thousand and one nights".

## The noun after the number

This is the rule to learn first. Wikipedia's Russian declension article states it for a number in the nominative or accusative: **the last word of the number decides** the form of the noun. Wikipedia words each line as applying to "most" numbers of that kind, and the special cases follow below.

| Number ends in | Noun form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | nominative singular | *два́дцать одна́ маши́на*, 21 cars |
| 2, 3, 4 | genitive singular | *три соба́ки*, 3 dogs; *со́рок два окна́*, 42 windows |
| 5–9, 0 | genitive plural | *пять я́блок*, 5 apples; *де́сять рубле́й*, 10 roubles |
| 11–14 | genitive plural | *сто оди́ннадцать ме́тров*, 111 metres |

The last row is the exception to watch: 21 ends in *оди́н*, but 111 ends in *оди́ннадцать*, so it takes the genitive plural. Wikipedia also notes that the plain nominative plural is used **only without numerals**: *э́ти дома́*, "these houses", but *три до́ма*, "three houses".

A few nouns have their own counted forms. Wikipedia gives *четы́ре го́да* but *пять лет* for "years", and *три челове́ка* for "three people". In other cases, such as instrumental "with five cars", the number and noun both take that case.

If the grammar of cases is new, [Russian cases explained](/blog/russian-cases-explained-for-beginners/) introduces the nominative and genitive this rule depends on.

## Working out the noun for six prices

Apply the last-word rule to *рубль*, "rouble". Wikipedia's own examples give *оди́н рубль* and *де́сять рубле́й*; the genitive singular *рубля́* fills the middle row.

1. **1** → ends in 1 → *оди́н рубль*.
2. **3** → ends in 3 → *три рубля́*.
3. **5** → genitive plural → *пять рубле́й*.
4. **21** → ends in *оди́н* → *два́дцать оди́н рубль*.
5. **111** → ends in *оди́ннадцать* → *сто оди́ннадцать рубле́й*.
6. **2,024** → ends in *четы́ре* → *две ты́сячи два́дцать четы́ре рубля́*.

A quick check: take the number modulo 100. If the result is 11 to 14, use the genitive plural. Otherwise look at the number modulo 10: 1 gives nominative singular, 2 to 4 give genitive singular, anything else gives genitive plural. Running 1, 3, 5, 21, 111 and 2024 through that test gives the six answers above.

## Ordinals, halves and money

Ordinals are adjectives in Russian: *пе́рвый* (first), *второ́й* (second), *тре́тий* (third), *четвёртый* (fourth), *пя́тый* (fifth). Wikipedia notes they decline exactly like adjectives, and in a long ordinal only the last word changes: *сто пятьдеся́т три ты́сячи пе́рвый*, "153,001st".

For one and a half there is a special word, *полтора́* (feminine *полторы́*), which takes the genitive singular: *полторы́ ты́сячи*, 1,500. Decimals use a comma, as in 3,14.

The rouble, Wikipedia says, is technically divided into 100 kopecks, though kopeck coins are now rarely used because of inflation.
