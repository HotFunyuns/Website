Khmer is not tonal. That single fact changes what a travel phrase list is worth in Cambodia compared with Thailand, Laos or Vietnam, because approximate pronunciation gets you understood rather than getting you a different word. You can be sloppy and still be followed.

So a short list genuinely works here. Here is one, ordered by how often you will reach for it.

## Greetings, and the register choice

**ជំរាបសួរ** — *chum reap suor* — hello, formal. **សួស្ដី** — *suosdei* — hello, informal.

As a visitor, default to the formal one. You will usually be addressing someone whose age and standing relative to your own you cannot read, and formal is the safe error. [Khmer greetings and honorifics](/blog/khmer-greetings-and-honorifics/) sets out how the register system works and why the choice matters more than it does in English.

**អរគុណ** — *arkun* — thank you. **លាសិនហើយ** — *léa sen haeuy* — goodbye. **ជួបគ្នាថ្ងៃក្រោយ** — see you another day.

Pair these with the polite acknowledgement particle appropriate to your own gender; used constantly, it is what stops correct Khmer sounding curt.

## The repair phrases

These outrank most vocabulary, because they keep a conversation going rather than ending it:

- **មិនយល់ទេ** — *min yol te* — I don't understand.
- **សូមនិយាយយឺតៗ** — *som niyeay yeut yeut* — please speak more slowly.
- **សូម...** — *som* — please, as a request opener.
- A request to repeat, and a request to write something down. Place names in particular survive being written far better than being spoken.

## Meeting people

- **អ្នកឈ្មោះអី?** — *ânâk chhmoh ei* — what's your name?
- **ខ្ញុំឈ្មោះ ...** — *khnhom chhmoh ...* — my name is ...
- **អ្នកមកពីប្រទេសណា?** — *ânâk mok pi bratés na* — where are you from?
- **ខ្ញុំមកពី ...** — I'm from ...

Note **ខ្ញុំ** (*khnhom*) doing the first-person work. It is the neutral form and the one a visitor should use. Khmer speakers frequently avoid pronouns altogether in favour of kinship terms and names, and drop the subject entirely when context supplies it — so short sentences are normal, not lazy.

## Money and the market

Cambodia runs on riel and US dollars simultaneously, which means numbers and currency vocabulary do more work here than in most destinations. You will be quoted in one and given change in the other.

*How much* is the essential question. Understanding the answer requires numbers, and Khmer numbers have a quinary structure that makes them briefly strange and then very regular — [learning Khmer for beginners](/blog/learn-khmer-beginners-guide/) covers how counting in fives works.

Bargaining is normal in markets and is conducted politely rather than aggressively. Being able to name a number is most of what you need.

## Food

Enough to state what you do not eat, and enough to recognise staples on a menu. **អញ្ជើញពិសាឲ្យបានឆ្ងាញ់** is the Khmer equivalent of *bon appétit*.

The genuinely important group is allergies and restrictions. It is dull to learn and it is the vocabulary people most regret not having, and it needs to be automatic rather than merely recognised, because it is needed in a hurry.

## Transport, temples and getting around

Tuk-tuks, taxis, place names, "where is". Confirm a price before you get in — which is a numbers problem again.

Temple etiquette is worth reading before Angkor rather than after. Dress, footwear and behaviour around monks are all governed by conventions that visitors routinely breach without knowing, and the relevant vocabulary is a small set.

## Health and emergencies

Pharmacy, hospital, doctor, help, pain. Nobody enjoys this group and everyone who has needed it remembers whether they had it.

## A note on the spellings

Every romanisation above should be read as one system's version of the word. Khmer transliteration is not standardised — the Geographic Department system, the UNGEGN system and the ALA-LC tables all exist and all make different choices — so the same Khmer word will appear differently spelled in two guidebooks without either being wrong.

This is the strongest practical argument for learning at least part of the script. A transliteration tells you what one author thought the word sounded like; the script tells you what the word is. [Khmer script explained](/blog/khmer-script-explained/) covers what the writing system actually asks of you.

## How we grouped this, and the one place we broke our own rule

The Cambodia travel material in [Learn Cambodian, Speak Khmer](/apps/learn-cambodian-khmer/) is built around situations rather than grammar: airport arrivals, hotels, tuk-tuks and taxis, market bargaining, ordering food, temple etiquette, currency, pharmacies and emergencies, with location-specific content for Phnom Penh, Siem Reap, Angkor Wat, Battambang, Kampot, Kep and Sihanoukville.

Situation grouping is deliberate — retrieval under pressure is cued by context, and you are standing in a market, not in a grammar chapter.

The decision worth explaining is about transliteration, because it is the one place the course is deliberately inconsistent with itself.

Across the main course, Khmer script is primary. Romanised spelling appears alongside it, and the lessons push steadily toward reading, because the script is the only stable representation of the language and transliteration systems disagree.

**In the travel unit, transliteration sits beside the Khmer script with equal weight, and it is the only unit where we do that.**

The reason is who opens that unit and when. Someone revising the tuk-tuk phrases is frequently doing so in the week before a flight, or in the back of a vehicle, and they have not worked through the alphabet lessons. For that person, script-primary presentation means a phrase they cannot use.

We resisted this for a while, because an inconsistent policy is a worse policy in the abstract and because transliteration-dependence is a real trap — a learner who leans on it never moves to the script, and their reading plateaus.

What settled it was noticing that the travel unit's users and the reading track's users are substantially different people with different goals. A traveller needs a phrase for a fortnight. A learner needs a writing system for years. Forcing the traveller through the learner's path does not convert them; it just means they arrive in Siem Reap with nothing.

So the inconsistency stands, and it is confined to that one unit, and the lessons say why.
