**Most plateaus are not plateaus. They are the ordinary shape of a practice curve, which improves fast at the start and then flattens hard.** A score that leapt in week one and has barely moved in week five is behaving exactly as the research on practice curves predicts, and the useful question is not "why have I stopped improving" but "which of four different things is my record actually showing".

This article covers the shape first, because it reframes the problem, then the four readings and the specific change each one calls for.

## Practice curves decelerate, and faster than you think

The standard textbook claim is the "power law of practice": performance improves as a power function of the number of trials, with each additional repetition buying less than the last.

That claim has been challenged in an interesting way. Heathcote, Brown and Mewhort's paper [*The Power Law repealed: The case for an Exponential Law of Practice*](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~paritosh/papers/KIP/power-law-repealed.pdf), published in *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, argues that "the evidence for a Power Law is flawed because it is based on averaged data". The authors fitted power and exponential functions to "40 sets of data representing 7910 learning series from 475 subjects in 24 experiments" and reported that "the exponential function fit better than the power in all the unaveraged data sets", adding that "averaging produced a bias in favour of the power function".

You do not need to care which function wins to take the useful consequence. Both describe a curve that rises steeply and then flattens, and the exponential flattens *sooner*. More importantly, the paper's central methodological point applies directly to a personal practice record: curves built by averaging many learners look smoother and more gradual than any individual's curve does. Your own series is bumpier and flattens earlier than the tidy diagram in a productivity article, and that is not a defect in your practice.

So the baseline expectation for any repeated task is: large gains quickly, small gains slowly, then a long stretch where nothing headline-level moves. Reaching that stretch is a sign that the early work succeeded.

## Four readings of a flat score

A score is a composite. To diagnose a plateau you need the components: accuracy, median answer time, and, if your record has one, a consistency figure. Hold the mode and the difficulty constant while you read them — comparing across modes is the single most common way to invent a plateau that is not there, and [how to track practice progress without fooling yourself](/blog/how-to-track-practice-progress/) covers the rest of those traps.

**Reading one: accuracy is at the top and answer time is flat.** The task is finished. You are getting almost everything right, as fast as the method allows, and there is nothing left in this rung to learn. This is the clean case, and the fix is to change what is being asked.

**Reading two: answer time is falling but accuracy is falling with it.** You are not improving; you are moving along the speed-accuracy trade-off, buying pace with errors. The score may even rise for a while because speed bonuses reward it. The fix is the opposite of raising difficulty: go back to untimed rounds until accuracy is stable again, as set out in [accuracy before speed](/blog/accuracy-before-speed/).

**Reading three: the headline number is flat but consistency is rising.** You are still learning, and looking at the wrong column. The first thing practice usually removes is the occasional long stall rather than the ordinary answer, which shows up as a shrinking tail rather than a lower typical time. Response time distributions are "invariably positively skewed", as Lo and Andrews put it in [*To transform or not to transform*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01171/full), so the mean and the median tell different stories — [reading your response time numbers](/blog/reading-response-time-numbers/) separates them.

**Reading four: everything is flat and has been for two or three weeks at a difficulty you are comfortable with.** This is the genuine article. The session has become maintenance. That is a perfectly reasonable thing for a session to be, but if you want it to be something else you have to change the demand.

## Performance is not the same as learning

Before changing anything, one caveat that cuts both ways.

Soderstrom and Bjork's review [*Learning Versus Performance*](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/11/soderstorm_ra_learningvsperformance.pdf) is blunt about how weak a guide in-session performance is: what we observe during training "is performance, which is often an unreliable index of whether the relatively long-term changes that constitute learning have taken place". The review also notes that "improvements in performance can fail to yield significant learning", and that people "often mistakenly interpret their performance during acquisition as a reliable guide to long-term learning".

Applied here, that means two things. A flat score does not prove you have stopped learning — the stable part might be retention you would have lost without the practice. And a rising score does not prove you have learned anything durable, especially when it rises within a single session on the back of warm-up.

The check worth building in is a cold run: same mode, same difficulty, no warm-up, a couple of days after your last session. What survives a gap is a better estimate of what you have than what you can produce at the end of a hot session.

## What to change, and in what order

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app — Reign Creative publishes it — and the ladders below are the shipped ones, which makes them a concrete illustration of the general point: a plateau is fixed by changing a specific variable, not by adding effort.

1. **Step up one difficulty label.** The arithmetic modes change what is actually generated as you climb — single digit, then wider ranges, then two-digit by two-digit, then mixed chains — and the memory modes have seven levels that change sequence length, grid size and how long items stay on screen. One rung at a time. If your accuracy at the new rung sits somewhere well short of perfect but not close to guessing, you have found the right one.
2. **Switch from a single-operation sprint to the mixed-operation mode.** This adds the step of working out what is being asked, which is a different skill from executing it. Expect the score to fall; that is the task being harder, not you getting worse. [Interleaved versus blocked practice](/blog/interleaving-versus-blocked-practice/) explains why the mixed version feels worse while working better.
3. **Change the format rather than the content.** Moving from an untimed accuracy round to a timed sprint, or to a survival format that ends on a mistake, changes the pressure without changing the arithmetic. This is the lightest of the three interventions and the easiest to reverse.
4. **Change the content entirely.** If arithmetic has genuinely stopped moving, a memory mode asks something different of the same session slot. The [IES practice guide on organising instruction and study](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1) recommends spacing learning over time and using quizzing to re-expose learners to key content, both of which favour rotation over grinding.

The rule that matters more than the list: **change one variable and give it several sessions.** If you raise difficulty, switch to mixed and add a timer in the same week, the resulting mess of numbers cannot tell you which change did anything.

## What a plateau does not mean

It does not mean the practice was pointless, and it does not mean the next tier of effort will unlock something. Nothing about a flat arithmetic score says anything about general ability, and no amount of pushing through one will — the cornerstone article [do brain training apps work](/blog/do-brain-training-apps-work/) sets out why claims of that kind do not hold up, and the whole [brain training games hub](/blog/topics/brain-training-games/) stays inside the narrower and better-supported claim that you get better at what you practise.

Within that frame, a plateau is a useful signal. It tells you the current demand has been met, and that improvement from here is a matter of asking for something else.
