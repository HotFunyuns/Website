**Rugby union now has two kinds of red card, and since 1 July 2026 the difference has been full law rather than a trial. In elite rugby, a player sent off for anything other than intentional and highly dangerous foul play can be replaced by a team-mate once 20 minutes of playing time have passed; for intentional and highly dangerous actions, and at every level below elite, the player still cannot be replaced and the team plays short for the rest of the match.** Either way, the player shown red never returns. A yellow card is different again: ten minutes in the sin bin, then back on.

That is the whole system in brief. Because the 20-minute replacement spent a year as a trial before becoming law, descriptions of the trial are still easy to find; everything here is taken from the Laws of the Game as published on World Rugby's laws site in October 2026, with dates for each change.

## The three cards at a glance

| Card | The player | The team | Where it applies |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Yellow | Off for ten minutes of playing time (two in sevens), then returns | Plays a player short for that time | Every level |
| 20-minute red | Off for the rest of the match | Plays short for 20 minutes of playing time, then any available replacement may come on | Elite rugby only |
| Permanent red | Off for the rest of the match | Plays short for the rest of the match | Elite rugby for intentional and highly dangerous actions; every red card below elite level |

The key law is 9.30. Its general rule is that a player sent off takes no further part and "may not be replaced". It then adds an elite-only exception: for intentional and highly dangerous actions the player cannot be replaced, and for other actions the player sent off may be replaced after 20 minutes of playing time. Law 3.33 makes the same exception in the replacement rules.

## What counts as elite rugby

The laws define elite rugby as "a professional level of rugby, determined by individual unions/competitions". World Rugby's implementation guideline repeats that unions decide what level counts as elite in their countries. There is no single global line: the same law book applies everywhere, but whether the 20-minute replacement is available depends on how the union or competition running the match has classified it.

For a club or school match outside that elite tier, the red-card rule has not changed. A red card means fourteen players, or fewer, until the final whistle.

## How the 20-minute red card became law

| Date | Step | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| November 2024 | Council defers a decision on the 20-minute red card to 2025, while approving other trials | Global Law Trials page, January 2025 |
| 21 May 2025 | Council approves a global law trial in elite competitions, to be used at the 2025 Women's Rugby World Cup and the U20 Championship | World Rugby news release |
| 1 July 2025 | Trial wording enters World Rugby's laws: Laws 9.30 and 3.33, and a new definition of elite rugby | Global law trial page |
| August 2025 | A Law Application Guideline sets out how competitions can run the off-field review | Implementation guideline |
| 17 June 2026 | Council moves four older trials into full law: the brake foot, elite water-carrier restrictions, the 20-minute red card replacement and the role of the TMO | 2026-07 Law updates |
| 1 July 2026 | Full law, for new competitions and events starting after that date; competitions already in progress check with their union | 2026-07 Law updates |

One discrepancy in World Rugby's own pages is worth flagging. The August 2025 guideline says the Council approved the trial in November 2024, but the January 2025 trials page records the decision as deferred that month, and both the May 2025 release and the trial page date the approval to 21 May 2025. The table follows the latter.

Two other details catch people out. The wording now in Law 9.30 is the same wording that was trialled from July 2025, so accurate descriptions of the trial still hold. And the start date is tied to competitions rather than a calendar day: a league that began before 1 July 2026 may have continued on its existing basis, which is why World Rugby told mid-season competitions to check with their union.

## The yellow card and the sin bin

A yellow card is a caution plus a temporary suspension. Under Law 9.29 the player is suspended for ten minutes; the definitions describe it as "usually for 10 minutes playing time", spent in the sin bin, a designated place outside the playing area. Because it is playing time, stoppages do not count. Sevens, played under World Rugby's variations to the laws, cuts the suspension to two minutes.

Yellow cards arrive in a few predictable ways:

- **Foul play** that meets the yellow threshold. Law 9 opens with the principle that a player who commits foul play must be cautioned, shown a yellow card or sent off.
- **Repeated offences by a team.** When different players keep committing the same offence, the referee gives the team a general warning, and the next player to repeat it is carded (Law 9.10).
- **A penalty try.** When foul play prevents a probable try, the referee awards seven points and the offender must be shown a yellow or red card (Law 8.3).

## When a yellow becomes red: the off-field review

The guideline published in August 2025 created a two-stage process for elite matches that have the technology and officials to run it.

1. **The on-field decision.** The referee, assistant referees and TMO watch the replays. If an action is clearly intentional and highly dangerous, the referee can show a permanent red card straight away.
2. **The referral.** If the officials agree the yellow-card threshold is met but want a fuller look, the referee shows a yellow card and crosses their arms above their head. The player goes to the sin bin as normal.
3. **The review.** A foul play review officer, or the TMO if no such officer is appointed, has up to eight minutes to examine all available footage.
4. **The outcome.** The card either stays yellow, and the player returns after ten minutes, or is upgraded to a 20-minute red. The reviewing official cannot recommend a permanent red card. If upgraded, the referee shows red with the crossed-arms signal, and the 20 minutes are counted from the original incident.

Competitions choose how to run this according to the officials they appoint. With only a referee and assistant referees, or with a TMO but no off-field review capacity, every decision is made on the field at the time: a red card with arms crossed is a 20-minute red, and a red card without that signal is permanent.

## Two yellow cards

Law 9.29 is simple: a player who commits a second yellow-card offence is sent off. In elite rugby, the guideline treats that sending-off as a 20-minute red card, so a replacement can come on after 20 minutes, unless the second offence itself meets the intentional and highly dangerous threshold, in which case it is a permanent red. Below elite level, two yellows still leave the team a player short for the rest of the match.

## The bench rule most people miss

When the 20 minutes are up, the replacement does not have to be an unused player. Law 3.33 lets a tactically replaced player return in place of a team-mate who received a qualifying red card, and the guideline confirms that any available member of the replacements bench, "unused or otherwise", may come on. A coach can therefore bring a starter who was taken off earlier back into the match.

## A worked example: what each card costs

Assume an 80-minute elite match with no extra time, and count only playing time.

| Card shown at | Yellow | 20-minute red | Permanent red |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 30:00 | Short until 40:00 | Short until 50:00, then back to 15 | Short for the remaining 50 minutes |
| 55:00 | Short until 65:00 | Short until 75:00, then back to 15 | Short for the remaining 25 minutes |
| 65:00 | Short until 75:00 | Short for the remaining 15 minutes | Short for the remaining 15 minutes |

The last row is the useful one. From the 60th minute of playing time onwards, the 20 minutes cannot run out before the 80th, so a 20-minute red card costs a team exactly as much playing time as a permanent one in that match. The difference between the two cards is largest early in a match and disappears late on.

Two more things sit outside the table. Whichever red it is, the player sent off is gone for the match, and the player who restores the team to 15 has to come from the bench. And whichever red it is, the referee must send the match organiser a written report on the foul play as soon as possible (Law 6.18).

## Cards and the front row

A card for a prop or hooker has a knock-on effect at the scrum. If the team cannot keep contesting scrums with the players left on the field, it nominates another player to leave so that a front-row replacement can come on; after a yellow card, that nominated player stays off until the suspension ends (Laws 3.19 and 3.20). If no suitable front-rower is available at all, scrums become uncontested, and in elite rugby the team responsible can lose an extra player. World Rugby's uncontested-scrum guideline works through a case where a single yellow card leaves a team with 13 players for ten minutes.
