**The difference is one touch.** In soccer (association football), a goal can be scored straight from a **direct** free kick, while an **indirect** free kick has to touch another player — of either team — before a goal counts. The referee shows which is which: an arm raised above the head means indirect. Which one a team gets depends on the offence, and [Law 13](https://www.theifab.com/laws/latest/free-kicks/) of the IFAB Laws of the Game, read with Law 12, sets that out.

It reflects the 2026/27 Laws of the Game, effective from 1 July 2026.

## Which offence gives which restart

| Offence | Restart | Where it sits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Charging, jumping at, kicking, pushing, striking, tackling or tripping an opponent carelessly, recklessly or with excessive force | Direct | Law 12.1 |
| Holding an opponent, or impeding one **with** contact | Direct | Law 12.1 |
| A handball offence (except a goalkeeper inside their own penalty area) | Direct | Law 12.1 |
| Biting or spitting at someone on the team lists or a match official | Direct | Law 12.1 |
| Throwing an object at the ball, an opponent or a match official, or playing the ball with a held object | Direct | Law 12.1 |
| A physical offence against a team-mate, substitute, team official or match official | Direct | Law 12.5 |
| Any direct free kick offence inside the offender's own penalty area | Penalty kick | Law 14 |
| Playing in a dangerous manner | Indirect | Law 12.2 |
| Impeding an opponent **without** contact | Indirect | Law 12.2 |
| Dissent, offensive or abusive language, or any other verbal offence | Indirect | Laws 12.2 and 12.5 |
| Preventing the goalkeeper from releasing the ball, or kicking at it as they release it | Indirect | Law 12.2 |
| The deliberate trick of passing to the goalkeeper with the head, chest or knee to get round the handling rule | Indirect | Law 12.2 |
| Goalkeeper, inside their own area, handles after releasing the ball, or from a team-mate's deliberate kick or throw-in | Indirect | Law 12.2 |
| An offside offence | Indirect, where the offence occurred | Law 11 |
| The kicker touches the ball a second time at a restart before anyone else does | Indirect (direct if by handball) | Law 13.3 |
| An attacker within 1 m (1 yd) of a wall of three or more defenders | Indirect | Law 13.3 |
| Any other offence, not named in the Laws, for which play is stopped to caution or send off a player | Indirect | Law 12.2 |
| Goalkeeper controls the ball with the hands for more than eight seconds | **Corner kick**, not a free kick | Law 12.3 |

The pattern is easy to remember. Contact with an opponent, holding, handball and violence are direct; non-contact, technical and verbal offences are indirect. The last row is the odd one: since 2025/26 a goalkeeper who holds the ball too long concedes a corner kick, replacing the old indirect free kick after six seconds, as the [IFAB's 2025 announcement](https://www.theifab.com/news/the-ifab-tackles-goalkeeper-time-wasting/) explains. The current [Law 12](https://www.theifab.com/laws/latest/fouls-and-misconduct/) files it under its own heading, 12.3.

## Reading the referee's signal

The referee indicates an indirect free kick by raising an arm above the head and keeps it raised until the kick has been taken and the ball touches another player, goes out of play, or clearly cannot be scored directly. A direct free kick gets no raised arm.

The signal matters enough that the Law protects against it being forgotten: if the referee fails to signal an indirect free kick and the ball is kicked straight into the goal, the kick is retaken.

## What happens if the kick goes straight in

| Free kick | Straight into the opponents' goal | Straight into the kicker's own goal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct | Goal | Corner kick to the opponents |
| Indirect | Goal kick to the opponents | Corner kick to the opponents |

A defender's deflection counts as a touch, so an indirect free kick that clips the wall on its way in is a goal.

## Where the kick is taken from

Free kicks are taken where the offence happened, with four exceptions in Law 13:

- an **indirect free kick to the attackers inside the opponents' goal area** is moved to the nearest point on the goal-area line that runs parallel to the goal line;
- a **free kick to the defending team inside its own goal area** may be taken from anywhere in that area;
- an offence involving a player **entering, re-entering or leaving the field without permission** restarts from the position of the ball when play stopped, while an offence committed **off the field** restarts on the nearest boundary line — or with a penalty kick, for a direct free kick offence, if that point is within the offender's own penalty area;
- anywhere else the Laws designate a position, as Laws 3, 11 and 12 do.

## Distances, walls and quick kicks

Until the ball is in play — kicked and clearly moving — every opponent must be at least **9.15 m (10 yds)** from it, with one exception: defenders may stand on their own goal line between the posts. That exception is why an indirect free kick a few metres from goal produces the familiar scene of a whole team lined up on the goal line. For a free kick to the defending team inside its own penalty area, opponents must also stay outside the area; if some are still inside because they had no time to leave, play goes on, but the kick is retaken if one of them touches or challenges for the ball before it is in play.

When **three or more** defenders form a wall, every attacker must stay at least **1 m (1 yd)** from it until the ball is in play. An attacker who breaks that rule concedes an indirect free kick.

A team may take a free kick **quickly**. If an opponent closer than 9.15 m intercepts a quick kick, play continues — the cost of going early falls on the kicking team. An opponent who deliberately prevents a free kick being taken quickly must be cautioned. Otherwise, an opponent who encroaches has the kick retaken, unless the referee can play advantage. The Law also allows the ball to be lifted with one foot or both feet together, and permits a feint before the kick.

## Penalty kicks are direct free kicks in the box

A direct free kick offence committed by a player inside their own penalty area becomes a [penalty kick](https://www.theifab.com/laws/latest/the-penalty-kick/), taken from the penalty mark with every player other than the kicker and the defending goalkeeper outside the area, behind the mark and at least 9.15 m away. An indirect free kick offence inside the same area — a goalkeeper picking up a deliberate back-pass, say — stays an indirect free kick. That is the whole reason for the goal-line scramble: the attackers get a free kick very close to goal, but they still need a second touch.

## Why the distinction matters on the pitch

For attackers, a direct free kick within range is a shooting chance; an indirect one needs a rehearsed touch first, which gives a defending wall a moving target. For defenders, the table explains why referees separate obstruction with contact from obstruction without it: impeding an opponent **with** contact is direct, **without** contact is indirect — the difference between a possible shot and a free kick that needs a second touch.
