Hold one number, not a list. The single change that makes long mental addition possible is refusing to store the numbers you have not used yet: you carry a running total, replace it after each step, and let the page, the receipt or the speaker keep the rest. Everything else — grouping to round numbers, rounding then correcting — is an optimisation on top of that.

## Why a list is a memory problem first

Adding two two-digit numbers is an arithmetic task. Adding nine of them is a storage task with some arithmetic attached, and it fails for a different reason: the intermediate result has to be held and overwritten, repeatedly, while new information arrives.

Research on mental arithmetic points at that specific mechanism. A study of memory updating and mental arithmetic found that performance on tasks requiring people to hold and update numerical information predicted success on difficult multi-digit multiplication, and that the prediction held only for the harder problems — precisely the ones where intermediate results have to be maintained. A separate study of multi-step mental addition found that children with faster single-step addition were faster at multi-step addition, and that verbal working memory capacity was associated with quicker multi-step performance.

The practical reading of that is not "train your working memory". It is: *design the procedure so that less has to be held*. A running total holds one number regardless of list length. Any method that asks you to remember three partial sums until the end is strictly more expensive.

If the underlying capacity question interests you, [what working memory is, and whether training changes it](/blog/working-memory-and-training/) treats the evidence directly and is careful about what has and has not been shown.

## The running total, worked

Take 34, 17, 46, 23, 9.

- 34
- 34 + 17 = 51
- 51 + 46 = 97
- 97 + 23 = 120
- 120 + 9 = **129**

At no point are two numbers in mind at once. Each new number is consumed immediately and the total is overwritten.

The failure mode to watch for is the pause. If a step is hard — 97 + 23 crosses a hundred — the temptation is to hold the next number ready while you work it out, and that is the moment the total gets corrupted. Finish the step, say the new total to yourself, and only then look at the next number.

## Grouping to round numbers, when you can see the list

If the whole list is in front of you, you are allowed to reorder it. Addition is commutative and associative, so the total does not care.

Take 12, 38, 45, 55, 27, 23. Pair them deliberately: 12 + 38 = 50, 45 + 55 = 100, 27 + 23 = 50. The total is 50 + 100 + 50 = **200**.

The running total on the same list gives 12, 50, 95, 150, 177, **200** — the same answer with two more awkward steps.

Another: 7, 13, 25, 8, 12, 15. Pair 7 + 13 = 20, 8 + 12 = 20, 25 + 15 = 40. Total **80**.

Scanning for complements is a skill in itself, and it is the same skill that makes single-digit addition fluent: spotting the pairs that make ten, a hundred, or a clean multiple. NRICH's work on flexible calculation makes the general point that a set of calculations sharing an operation does not imply they should share a method — the numbers decide.

Two cautions. First, grouping costs you the constant memory load: you now hold three subtotals instead of one. Keep the groups few. Second, it is easy to use a number twice or skip one, so strike each number off as you consume it if the list is written.

## Numbers clustered near a benchmark

When the entries are all close to the same round figure, neither method above is the shortest route. Multiply the benchmark by the count, then add the deviations with their signs.

Take 198 + 203 + 197 + 202. All four sit near 200, so start at 4 × 200 = 800. The deviations are −2, +3, −3 and +2, which sum to 0. The total is **800**.

The direct check: 198 + 203 = 401, 197 + 202 = 399, and 401 + 399 = 800.

This is the same manoeuvre as averaging by deviations, and it collapses a four-term addition into one multiplication and one small signed sum. It is at its best with measurement data, repeated prices, or any list generated by the same process.

## Money, where the rounding comes first

Prices are the everyday case, and the right order is estimate then correct.

Take 4.99, 12.49, 2.25, 7.80 and 1.99. Round to 5.00, 12.50, 2.25, 7.80 and 2.00. Those total 29.55, and you can get there by grouping: 5.00 + 2.00 = 7.00, 12.50 + 7.80 = 20.30, plus 2.25 gives **29.55**.

Now correct. You rounded three items up by one penny each, so take three pence back: **29.52**.

The estimate is the part that actually matters at a till. It is right to within a few pence, it arrives in about two seconds, and it is enough to catch a scanning error. [Estimation techniques that make mental math useful](/blog/estimation-techniques-explained/) makes the wider argument for treating the approximate answer as the primary product rather than a consolation prize.

## Checking a total you cannot see twice

Re-adding the same list in the same order mostly reproduces the same mistake. Re-add it backwards, or in different groups, and a matching answer becomes real evidence.

Two cheap partial checks are worth knowing. The last digit of the total is fixed by the last digits of the entries, so 34 + 17 + 46 + 23 + 9 must end in the last digit of 4 + 7 + 6 + 3 + 9 = 29, which is 9 — and 129 ends in 9. And the rough size is fixed before you start: five numbers averaging roughly 26 must land near 130.
