Ancient Egypt's history is conventionally divided into three "kingdoms" separated by "intermediate periods", with an early phase before them and a late phase after, all built from about thirty numbered dynasties of rulers. The order is settled. The dates are not: Wikipedia's article on Egyptian chronology notes that respected chronologies differ by about 300 years for the Early Dynastic Period, up to 30 years for the New Kingdom and only a few years in the Late Period. This timeline gives the sequence, shows how much the numbers move, and explains why.

## The sequence, period by period

The table below follows the grouping in Wikipedia's Egyptian chronology table, with Ian Shaw's dates (2000), one of the scholarly schemes it compares.

| Period | Dynasties | Shaw (2000), BC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early Dynastic | 1st–2nd | 3000–2686 |
| Old Kingdom | 3rd–6th | 2686–2181 |
| First Intermediate | 7th(?)–10th | 2181–2125 |
| Middle Kingdom | 11th–12th | 2125–1773 |
| Second Intermediate | 13th–17th | 1773–1550 |
| New Kingdom | 18th–20th | 1550–1069 |
| Third Intermediate | 21st–25th | 1069–664 |
| Late Period | 26th onward | from 664 |

Groupings vary even within Wikipedia. Its History of ancient Egypt article puts the 11th Dynasty in the First Intermediate Period and the 13th in the Middle Kingdom, and its Middle Kingdom article says that period ended in 1782 "or 1700 BC (depending on the definition)". The Late Period, it says, ran from 664 BC to 332 BC and included Achaemenid Persian rule after Cambyses II conquered Egypt in 525 BC. Ptolemaic Egypt followed, from 305 to 30 BC.

The labels themselves are modern. Wikipedia's Middle and New Kingdom articles credit the idea of three "golden ages" to the German scholar Christian Charles Josias von Bunsen in 1845, and the term "First Intermediate Period" was coined in 1926 by Georg Steindorff and Henri Frankfort. The Egyptologist Ludwig David Morenz, arguing it was not a dark age, proposed calling it the "Period of Regions" instead.

## Manetho and the numbered dynasties

The dynasties come from Manetho, an Egyptian priest of the Ptolemaic Kingdom active around 290–260 BCE, who wrote a history of Egypt in Greek, the *Aegyptiaca*. Wikipedia notes that none of his original text survives; it is known only from fragments transmitted by later authors, including Eusebius and Sextus Julius Africanus, and the reign length given for the same pharaoh "often varies substantially" depending on which intermediary you read.

Native king lists help but have their own gaps. Wikipedia describes the Turin King List as comprehensive but damaged, and the Abydos King List and Palermo Stone as complete as texts but missing rulers. The general method of counting by reigns is covered in [regnal years and dynastic dating](/blog/regnal-years-and-dynastic-dating/).

## Three chronologies side by side

Wikipedia's chronology table compares James Breasted (1906), Ian Shaw (2000) and Erik Hornung (2006). A few rows show the drift:

| Dynasty | Breasted (1906) | Shaw (2000) | Hornung (2006) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | 3400–2980 (with 2nd) | 3000–2890 | 2900–2730 (± 25) |
| 3rd (start of Old Kingdom) | 2980–2900 | 2686–2613 | 2592–2544 (± 25) |
| 18th (start of New Kingdom) | 1580–1350 | 1550–1295 | 1539–1292 |
| 26th | 663–525 | 664–525 | 664–525 |

The gap narrows as you move forward in time: by the 26th Dynasty the three schemes differ by a single year at most. An EBSCO Research Starter adds another variant: it dates the Old Kingdom to roughly 2687–2125 BCE and counts it as the Third to Eighth Dynasties, while its New Kingdom entry starts that period about 1570 BCE. Wikipedia's New Kingdom article says radiocarbon dating places the start between 1570 and 1544 BC.

A worked example shows what that means for a single figure. Old Kingdom length in Shaw's scheme: 2686 − 2181 = 505 years. In Hornung's, from the 3rd Dynasty's start to the 6th Dynasty's end: 2592 − 2118 = 474 years. The New Kingdom comes out at 1550 − 1069 = 481 years for Shaw and 1539 − 1077 = 462 for Hornung. Same pharaohs, same order, a difference of a generation. When you meet two figures like this, [what to do when two sources disagree on a date](/blog/when-two-sources-disagree-on-a-date/) sets out how to handle it.

## Why the early dates moved down

Wikipedia gives three reasons the numbers have changed over a century.

**Overlapping dynasties.** Following Manetho, Breasted assumed all the dynasties were sequential. It is now known that several ruled at the same time in different regions. Revisions like this lowered the conventional dates by up to 400 years at the beginning of the 1st Dynasty (a statement Wikipedia currently marks as needing a citation).

**Inferring reign lengths.** Where a king's total years are unknown, Egyptologists use proxies. For the Old Kingdom, inscriptions mention a periodic cattle census. Early scholars assumed it was held every second year, so 24 recorded censuses gave Sneferu a reign of 24 × 2 = 48 years. Later research showed the censuses were sometimes held in consecutive years and sometimes at longer intervals, so the multiplier itself is uncertain.

**Changing anchors.** The Sothic cycle, an astronomical calculation once used to fix dates, has lost favour: Wikipedia says experts have moved away from relying on it. Radiocarbon and tree-ring work now provide independent checks, such as a coffin dated 2073 ± 9 BC.

## The end of pharaonic Egypt

Wikipedia dates the pharaonic period from the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt in the 32nd century BC to Macedonian conquest in 332 BC, when [Alexander the Great](/blog/alexander-the-great-explained/) took Egypt from the Persians. The Ptolemaic Kingdom, founded in 305 BC by his general Ptolemy I Soter, lasted until the death of Cleopatra VII in 30 BC; Wikipedia calls the Ptolemies the longest and final dynasty of ancient Egypt. Her story is in [Cleopatra beyond the legend](/blog/cleopatra-beyond-the-legend/), and the 25th Dynasty of rulers from Kush, who governed Egypt late in the Third Intermediate Period, is covered in [Kush and the Nubian pharaohs](/blog/kush-and-the-nubian-pharaohs/).
