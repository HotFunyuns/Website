North America before 1492 is dated by three main methods working together: dendrochronology, which can give a calendar year; stratigraphy and associated artefacts, which give sequence; and radiocarbon, which gives a statistical range. The continent is unusual in having an exceptionally strong tree-ring record, so some construction dates in the Southwest are known to the year. It is also the place where a recent radiocarbon programme moved an entire region's chronology by roughly two generations, which is a useful reminder that precision and accuracy are different things.

## Tree rings: the method that produces a year, not a range

In climates with a strong seasonal cycle, a tree adds one growth ring a year, and the sequence of wide and narrow rings is shared across a region because it records shared weather. Overlapping samples — living trees, historic buildings, archaeological beams — can be chained into a continuous master chronology, and a new sample matched against that chronology is placed in specific calendar years.

The discipline has an institutional home in the American Southwest. The Laboratory of Tree-Ring Research was created at the University of Arizona in Tucson in 1937, by which point its founder, Andrew Ellicott Douglass, had already been working with wood for more than thirty years. The laboratory's own account notes that it helped establish many of the dendrochronology labs that now operate worldwide.

The limitation is precise and worth stating plainly: the date belongs to the timber. A beam may be cut years before use, taken from an older building, or left standing through generations of remodelling. Turning a cutting date into an occupation date is an argument, not a reading.

## Chaco and Mesa Verde: dated wood inside a contested story

Chaco Canyon shows both the power and the limits of the method. The National Park Service describes the cultural flowering of the Chacoan people as beginning in the mid-800s and lasting more than three centuries, with the great houses of Pueblo Bonito, Una Vida and Peñasco Blanco constructed during the middle and late 800s and Hungo Pavi, Chetro Ketl and Pueblo Alto following. By 1050, on the same account, Chaco had become the ceremonial, administrative and economic centre of the San Juan Basin, with dozens of great houses in the canyon connected by roads to more than 150 great houses across the region. New construction slowed in the 1100s and 1200s.

Those dates are firm. What they mean is not. The Park Service is explicit that the great houses may not have been ordinary farming villages with large resident populations, and may instead have been public architecture used periodically during ceremony and trade. It also records what Pueblo descendants say — that Chaco was a gathering place where many peoples and clans converged to share ceremonies, traditions and knowledge — and notes that Chaco is central to the origins of several Navajo clans and ceremonies. These are living nations describing a place that remains theirs, not commentary appended to an archaeological report.

Mesa Verde, where the Park Service records nearly 5,000 ancestral sites and describes Cliff Palace as the largest cliff dwelling, shows the same pattern: excellent chronological control over structures, continuing debate about what the structures were for.

## Mounds and stratigraphy: the Cahokia figures

Where timber is scarce, dating rests on stratigraphy, associated artefacts and radiocarbon. Cahokia, on the Mississippi floodplain across from present-day St Louis, is recorded by UNESCO as the largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico, occupied primarily during the Mississippian period between 800 and 1400 CE, covering nearly 1,600 hectares and containing some 120 mounds. Its population may have reached 10,000 to 20,000 at a peak between 1050 and 1150. Monks Mound, described as the largest prehistoric earthwork in the Americas, covers over five hectares and stands 30 metres high.

Read those numbers as what they are. The area and the mound dimensions are measurements. The population figure is an estimate with a stated range, derived from house counts and occupation density, and UNESCO frames it as a possibility rather than a finding. A timeline that prints "20,000" without the range has quietly turned a model into a fact. This distinction between a measured quantity and a reconstructed one is the same one that runs through [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/).

## When radiocarbon moved a whole region

For much of the twentieth century, the chronology of Northern Iroquoian sites in the lower Great Lakes was built on the presence or absence of European trade goods, with the relative sequence of villages read from artefact styles. That works only if you know how fast trade goods spread.

A 2018 study in *Science Advances* tested the assumption with 86 radiocarbon measurements. Warminster, often associated with the town of Cahiagué that Samuel de Champlain visited in 1615 and 1616, returned a radiocarbon age compatible with the conventional date. But the well-known Draper–Spang–Mantle site sequence in southern Ontario, usually placed around 1450 to 1550, returned dates of roughly 1530 to 1615 — some eighty years later. The authors describe the consequence bluntly: the revised time frame substantially rewrites sixteenth-century contact-era history for the region.

Nothing in that study was a correction of sloppy work. The earlier chronology was a reasonable inference from the evidence then available. It was wrong because one of its assumptions was wrong, and it took an independent method to show it.

## The founding of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy

This is the clearest case in North American chronology where an honest article has to decline to give a number. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy — described on its own website as made up of the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca nations — states that the Confederacy was founded by the Peacemaker with the help of Aionwatha, known more widely as Hiawatha, and that the exact date of the joining of the nations is unknown and is described as time immemorial. The same account sets out the structure that followed: the Onondaga as Keepers of the Fire, the Mohawk, Seneca and Onondaga as Elder Brothers and the Cayuga and Oneida as Younger Brothers within Grand Council, which still meets on Onondaga territory.

Various dates have been argued in the scholarly literature on documentary, archaeological and astronomical grounds. Those arguments rest in part on the regional archaeological chronology that radiocarbon has now revised, so they are not a fixed baseline against which the Haudenosaunee account can be measured. The responsible presentation gives the Confederacy's own position, notes that scholarly proposals differ and why, and does not adjudicate. Treating one dated estimate as the answer and the Haudenosaunee account as decoration gets both the evidence and the courtesy wrong.

## What to ask of any pre-Columbian date

Which method produced it? Is it a cutting date, a radiocarbon range, a stratigraphic inference or a population model? Has the underlying regional chronology been revised recently? And is there a descendant community with a position on the question, stated in its own words? A date that survives those four questions is worth writing down.
