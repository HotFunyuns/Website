"Mutually unintelligible" is not a polite way of saying "quite different". It is a claim about whether two speakers can understand each other without having studied, and for Shanghainese and Mandarin the answer is no. Wu varieties, Shanghainese among them, do not give a Mandarin speaker access.

What confuses the picture in practice is that Shanghai is a Mandarin-speaking city as well as a Shanghainese-speaking one, and inserting Mandarin into Shanghainese conversation is common, especially among younger speakers. A Mandarin speaker standing on a Shanghai street will catch words. That is code-switching, not comprehension.

## Sound: three initials against two

The difference a Mandarin speaker notices last and struggles with most is the initial series.

Mandarin contrasts two kinds of stop: unaspirated voiceless and aspirated voiceless. Shanghainese has three, adding a genuinely voiced series — a feature rare among Chinese varieties outside Wu and Xiang.

A Mandarin ear has two boxes and will put Shanghainese's three sounds into them, which means a whole dimension of contrast is invisible until it is trained. It is also the single best diagnostic for whether a Shanghainese course was built from Wu material or adapted from Mandarin material, because an adaptation tends to quietly lose the distinction.

Shanghainese is also vowel-rich, with roughly twenty distinct vowel qualities of which twelve are phonemic — considerably more crowded than Mandarin's vowel space.

## Tone: a different kind of system, not a different number

The tempting summary is "Mandarin has four tones, Shanghainese has five". It is true on a narrow reading and it misses the point.

In Mandarin, tone is a property of the syllable. You learn a syllable with its tone and you produce it, with predictable and limited modification when syllables meet.

In Shanghainese, tone has largely migrated to the word. Word-level sandhi is left-prominent: the first syllable sets the contour for the entire domain and the underlying tones of the following syllables have essentially no effect. There is a phrasal sandhi layer on top of that.

The consequences run in both directions. It is *harder*, because you cannot build words out of separately learned syllables. It is also, oddly, *easier* in one respect: you are not maintaining an independent tone on every syllable of a long word, which is where Mandarin learners leak errors.

The tone count itself is contested. Five tones are distinguishable in isolation, but because the yin-yang split is conditioned by the voicing of the initial and the checked tones by the presence of a final glottal stop, one analysis reduces Shanghainese to a two-way phonemic contrast. [Shanghainese tones and tone sandhi](/blog/shanghainese-tones-and-tone-sandhi/) works through why sources disagree.

## Vocabulary: different words, not shifted ones

Some vocabulary differences between Chinese varieties are regular sound correspondences — the same word, aged differently. Others are simply different words. Shanghainese has plenty of the second kind, and they cluster inconveniently in the highest-frequency slots.

The pronouns are the clearest case. Everyday second person in Shanghainese is 侬 (*nóng*), which is not the Mandarin second-person pronoun at all. The greeting is 侬好, not 你好. First person appears as 吾 / 我 with a Wu reading, not Mandarin's.

The yes-no question particle is 伐 (*va*) rather than Mandarin's 吗. Word order in ordinary questions can differ too: "have you eaten?" surfaces as 饭吃过伐? — object first — alongside forms closer to the Mandarin arrangement.

None of these is exotic vocabulary. They are the words you need in your first hour.

## The comparison in one view

| | Shanghainese | Mandarin |
|---|---|---|
| Family | Wu, Northern Wu, Shanghai subranch | Mandarin group |
| Mutual intelligibility with the other | None without study | None without study |
| Stop initials | Three-way: voiceless unaspirated, aspirated, voiced | Two-way: voiceless unaspirated, aspirated |
| Tone domain | Largely the word, reshaped by left-prominent sandhi | Largely the syllable |
| Tones counted in isolation | Five | Four, plus a neutral tone |
| Standard romanisation | None; four broad competing schools | Hanyu Pinyin, officially standardised |
| Written standard | No standard orthography | Standardised written Chinese |
| Everyday second-person pronoun | 侬 | 你 |
| Yes-no question particle | 伐 | 吗 |

## What we decided about the audio, and what it cost

This comparison is not academic for us. It determined how [Learn Shanghainese Wu Chinese](/apps/learn-shanghainese/) was built.

The fast way to produce a Shanghainese course is to take an existing Mandarin course, keep its structure and its vocabulary lists, and re-record the audio with a Shanghai accent. It is fast because the hard part — deciding what to teach and in what order — is already done.

We did not do that. The audio was recorded from Wu speakers, working from Shanghainese source material rather than from translated Mandarin lists.

That decision had a price, and it was not the recording cost. **It constrained the vocabulary we could cover.** A Mandarin word list translated into Shanghainese will always produce a Shanghainese equivalent for every item, because translation always produces something. Working from Wu material means you sometimes find that the natural Shanghainese way of handling a situation does not map onto the Mandarin lesson you were expecting to build, and the choice is to teach the natural version and lose the parallel, or force the parallel and teach something nobody says.

We chose the first, repeatedly, and the result is a course with somewhat narrower topical coverage than a translated one would have had. There are concepts a Mandarin course of the same size covers that ours does not.

The benefit is the part you can hear. The voiced initial series is present because the speakers have it. Sandhi is correct because it was produced rather than assembled. And the pronouns and particles are the Shanghainese ones rather than Mandarin ones wearing a different accent.
