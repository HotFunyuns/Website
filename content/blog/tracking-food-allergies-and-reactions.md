Most food diaries fail for the same reason: they record *what* and not *when*.

"Tuesday — pasta — felt bad" is an anecdote. "Tuesday, ate at 19:10, roughly 300 g, tomato sauce with garlic and a hard cheese, noticed something at 19:40" is a record someone can actually reason about. The difference is about fifteen extra seconds of typing, and it is the difference between a diary that is worth handing to a clinician and one that is not.

This page is about what to capture, and about the firm limit on what any log — ours included — can tell you.

## The five fields that carry the information

**What was eaten.** As specifically as you can manage. Brand and product name if it was packaged, because formulations differ between products with the same generic name.

**How much.** A rough quantity is enough. "Two slices" beats "some".

**When it was eaten.** An exact clock time, not a meal name. "Lunch" covers three hours.

**When something was noticed.** Also an exact time. This and the field above are the two people skip.

**What else was in the meal.** A reaction attributed to one food in a meal of six is a hypothesis about one of six things.

The interval between the third and fourth fields is the single most informative number in the whole record, because it is objective and because it constrains what is plausible. The NHS notes that symptoms of a food allergy may appear straight after eating the food or days later, and separately that food intolerance — a different thing with a different mechanism — usually produces symptoms such as bloating and stomach pain a few hours after eating. A log with only a date in it has thrown that interval away.

## Why a log is evidence and not an answer

Here is the limit, stated plainly, because everything else in this article depends on it.

**A log records what happened. It does not identify what caused it.**

Identifying a food allergy is a clinical process. It involves history, examination and testing, carried out by people qualified to interpret the results, and a diary is one input among several. NIAID — the lead institute at the NIH conducting food allergy research — describes food allergy as a condition affecting approximately 8 per cent of children and nearly 11 per cent of adults in the United States, and its research programme spans immunology, epidemiology and clinical trials. That is the scale of the effort that goes into understanding this properly, which is a reasonable indication of how far a phone app is from answering it.

What a good log *does* do is make an appointment more productive. Arriving with three weeks of timestamped entries is materially more useful than arriving with a recollection, and it is the one part of the process you control entirely.

There is also a category of situation where nothing in this article applies. The NHS lists signs of a serious allergic reaction — breathing very fast or struggling to breathe, wheezing, a feeling of choking or gasping, skin, tongue or lips turning blue, grey or pale, a child becoming limp or floppy or unresponsive — and describes these as needing immediate treatment in hospital, with an adrenaline auto-injector or nasal spray used immediately if one is available. That is an emergency, not a logging opportunity.

## Labels are better input than memory

If you are recording what was in something, the ingredient declaration is a far more reliable source than recollection.

The FDA enforces regulations requiring companies to list ingredients on packaged foods and beverages, and for certain substances the declaration has to be explicit. The **Food Allergen Labeling and Consumer Protection Act** identified eight major food allergens — milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat and soybeans — and the **FASTER Act**, signed into law on 23 April 2021, declared sesame the ninth. At the time FALCPA passed, those eight accounted for around 90 per cent of food allergies and serious allergic reactions in the US.

Two practical implications for a log. First, the label is the authoritative record of what was in a packaged product at the time you ate it — worth photographing or noting rather than reconstructing later. Second, "the same product" is not a stable category over time, because manufacturers reformulate; a log entry that names a brand and a date is more precise than one that names a food. How to read the rest of a label, including where allergen information sits relative to everything else, is covered in [reading nutrition labels](/blog/reading-nutrition-labels/).

## What our allergy log does, and what the flag means

[Easy Recipes & Meal Planner](/apps/easy-recipes-meal-planner/) lets you record allergies and previous reactions with notes and timestamps, and flags a recipe when one of its ingredients matches something you recorded.

The design question we spent the longest on was not how to detect the match. It was how to present it.

There were two options. The flag could read as a **verdict** — a red mark, a blocked recipe, an implication that the app has checked this dish for you. Or it could read as a **prompt** — a note that says an ingredient in this recipe matches something in your log, and that you should go and look.

We built the prompt, and the wording is deliberate: recipes are flagged when an ingredient **may** match something you recorded, and the app tells you to confirm ingredients and food labels for your own needs.

The reason is that a verdict would be a claim the software cannot support. An ingredient name in a recipe is a loose label: "cheese", "stock", "sauce" and "flour" each cover products with materially different compositions. The app does not know which brand you will buy, what it currently contains, how the dish will be prepared, or what it will be prepared alongside. A green tick on a recipe would be asserting all four.

There is a second, less comfortable reason. A flag that reads as clearance trains people to stop checking. The dangerous failure is not the flag that fires unnecessarily — that is an annoyance. It is the recipe that does *not* fire a flag, because the ingredient was named differently, and which the interface has implicitly presented as checked. Keeping the flag firmly in the "go and look" category is the only way to avoid building that habit.

So: the flag is a prompt to check an ingredient list. It is not an assurance about safety, and it is not a substitute for reading the label on the thing you actually bought.

## Keeping a log you will actually keep

The best log is the one that survives three weeks, which usually means the least elaborate one.

- **Record at the table, not at bedtime.** Reconstructed timestamps are the main source of bad data.
- **Use a fixed shape** — the five fields above, in the same order, every time. Free-form notes are quick to write and slow to read back.
- **Note the ordinary meals too.** A log containing only bad days cannot show that the same food was fine four other times, which is often the most informative comparison available.
- **Photograph unfamiliar labels.** Faster than typing, and it captures the version you actually ate.
- **Keep it long enough to repeat.** Weeks rather than days, because one episode has too many variables.
- **Take it to a professional.** That is the purpose of the exercise.
