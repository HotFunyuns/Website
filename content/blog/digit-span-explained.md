A digit span is the longest run of digits you can take in once and repeat back in the right order. It is not a number you either have or lack, and it is not read off a single attempt: it is the output of a stepped procedure that lengthens the sequence until you fail. Mental Math & Memory Games, which we build at Reign Creative, stores a best span from its own drills, and that figure is a personal best in a game rather than a test score. The app administers no cognitive assessment of any kind, and this article explains what the real procedure does so you can see the difference.

## What a span task is actually sampling

The task asks you to hold an arbitrary, meaningless sequence in an accessible state for a few seconds and then output it in order. Two things have to go right. You have to keep the items available, and you have to keep their order intact, which is a separate problem — people who recall every digit in the wrong sequence have failed the task just as completely as people who lose two of them.

Because the digits are arbitrary, the task deliberately strips away the thing that normally rescues memory: meaning. You cannot lean on knowledge you already have, which is exactly why the procedure is used to probe a capacity rather than a body of learning. Our [guide to working memory and whether training changes it](/blog/working-memory-and-training/) covers the capacity itself in detail; this article is about the measuring procedure that sits on top of it, which the cornerstone does not describe.

## How the procedure produces one number

The clinical form is unglamorous and precise. In their normative study of an educationally diverse elderly sample, Choi and colleagues describe using a standardised clinical digit span procedure, presenting digits at a rate of one per second. Forward recall begins at a length of three, backward recall at a length of two. **Two trials are presented at each length**, and the test is halted when the participant fails both trials at the same digit length.

Three features of that description carry all the weight:

- **A fixed presentation rate.** One digit per second is slow enough to be heard and fast enough to block elaborate encoding. Change the rate and you change the score.
- **Two trials per length.** A single lucky or unlucky run does not decide anything. Redundancy at each rung is what converts a noisy performance into a stable estimate.
- **A stopping rule agreed in advance.** The examiner does not decide when you have had enough. The rule does.

None of those three exists in a casual self-administered version. That is the practical gap between a procedure and a game.

## Why the span number overstates your storage

Here the honest answer is uncomfortable for anyone who likes the figure seven. George Miller's 1956 paper observed that decimal digits are worth about 3.3 bits apiece and that we can recall about seven of them. That is where the popular number comes from, and Miller was explicit that the span is a fixed number of *chunks* rather than of raw items — "we can increase the number of bits of information that it contains simply by building larger and larger chunks."

Nelson Cowan's later review argues that beneath the task surface there is a smaller and more constant central store, **limited to roughly 3 to 5 meaningful items in young adults**. Standard span tasks come out higher than that because you are allowed to rehearse: Cowan notes that one can retain about two seconds' worth of speech through silent rehearsal, and that in running-span procedures, where rehearsal is disrupted, only the last 3 to 5 digits can be recalled.

So a span of seven is not seven units sitting in a store. It is a smaller core plus rehearsal plus whatever grouping you managed in the moment. That is also why "average digit span" is a slippery phrase: it depends on age, education, the length of the digit words in the language being used, and how much rehearsal the procedure permits. Choi and colleagues published norms for one specific population using one specific instrument, which is the only way a norm can honestly exist.

## What practice on a span task does and does not change

This is the question most readers actually arrived with, and the evidence points one way. Norris, Hall and Gathercole, in *Memory & Cognition*, tested extensive digit span training directly and concluded that **digit span training does not substantially improve the capacity of verbal short-term memory**, finding little evidence for fundamental capacity changes with extensive practice. Transfer was thin: training on digit span produced no substantial benefit for spoken digits or letter span.

What does improve is your performance on the drilled task, through better grouping, better ordering habits and less flailing under time pressure. That is a real gain and it is worth having. It is not evidence that your memory in general got bigger, and we do not claim otherwise. If you want the full argument about near and far transfer, [our evidence review of brain training apps](/blog/do-brain-training-apps-work/) lays out where competent researchers still disagree.

There is a second, quieter reason to be cautious about comparing span numbers across settings. A 2026 study in *iScience* by Türüdü and colleagues, which followed the same two-trials-per-length structure, reported that a gamified online version produced significantly higher scores than both onsite and standard online versions — and that the boost was identical for forward and backward tasks. A friendlier interface can lift the number without anything about the person changing.
