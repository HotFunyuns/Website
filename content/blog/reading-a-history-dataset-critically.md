You can tell a good deal about a history app in about five minutes, using five questions: does its content cite anything, does it separate speculation from the documented record, does it label uncertainty honestly, does it disclose who made it, and does it say plainly what it is not for. An app that answers those well is a useful study tool. One that sounds authoritative and answers none of them is a liability, however polished it looks.

World History Timeline Sim is our own app, built by Reign Creative. Rather than describe the checklist in the abstract and then recommend ourselves, this article runs the checklist on our own product and reports what it finds, including where the answer is unflattering. That is the only version of this article worth publishing.

## Question one: does it cite anything?

This is the question most educational apps fail, and the one to ask first.

Open a detail screen — an event, a person, a civilisation — and look for a citation, a bibliography, a named author, a reference list, or even a general "sources" page. If none exists, every factual claim in the product is unattributed. That does not make it wrong. It does mean that nothing in it can function as evidence, and that you have no way to tell a well-researched entry from a confidently phrased guess.

**Our app fails this one.** Its content banks carry no citations, no bibliography and no external scholarly review. The historical text was written in-house. We have no basis for calling it sourced, fact-checked, peer-reviewed or historian-approved, and we do not.

By contrast, look at what a sourced product looks like. The Library of Congress's [research guides](https://guides.loc.gov/) are compiled by named staff and point at catalogued collections; The Programming Historian publishes [peer-reviewed, open-access lessons](https://programminghistorian.org/en/lessons/) under a CC-BY licence with an ISSN, so you can see who wrote each one and how it was reviewed. That is the standard against which an unsourced app should be measured, and the gap is real.

## Question two: does it separate speculation from the record?

Many history apps include counterfactual or "what if" content, which is genuinely useful for thinking about causation and genuinely dangerous if the invented branch is remembered as fact.

The test is whether the separation is structural or decorative. A disclaimer buried in a settings screen does not count. What counts is whether, at the moment a speculative outcome appears, the app shows you what is actually documented in a visibly different place.

**Our app passes this one, consistently.** Alternate-history outcomes are labelled as speculative wherever they appear, carry a footer noting that speculative outcomes are educational rather than factual, and are accompanied by a separate card giving what actually happened. The decision-based modes that produce a score also carry a standing note that the score is a simple educational model based on your choices, not a historical measurement. That framing is correct, and it is applied throughout rather than once.

## Question three: does it label uncertainty, and label it accurately?

There are two failure modes here. The first is labelling nothing, so every claim arrives with the same flat confidence. The second is labelling things in a way that sounds precise but is not.

**Our app is mixed.** Its everyday-life profiles do this well: each carries an explicit confidence grade and a short note naming the kind of evidence behind it, and a meaningful number of profiles are openly marked low confidence rather than quietly upgraded. Telling a learner that a picture of ordinary life in a given place and century rests on thin evidence is exactly right, and comparatively rare in consumer learning content.

Elsewhere it is weaker. Some content carries an uncertainty flag that is attached to the *subject* rather than to the *statement* — a topic with a contested legacy gets marked as debated even when the specific sentence is not in dispute. If you are using an app to find out whether historians actually disagree about something, that flag will mislead you. Check what a label is attached to before you treat it as historiography, and go to real historiographical writing if the disagreement matters.

## Question four: does it disclose who made it?

An app that will not say who wrote its content is asking for trust it has not offered to earn. Look for a developer name, a contact route, a privacy policy, and some statement about how content is produced.

**Our app passes, and this page is part of that.** The developer is named in the store listing and in the app itself, and we have stated above that the content is written in-house without external review. Professional bodies treat this kind of transparency as basic: the American Historical Association's [standards and guidelines for the discipline](https://www.historians.org/community-careers/professional-career-resources/standards-and-guidelines-for-the-discipline/) set expectations for evidencing and attributing historical claims, and the [Royal Historical Society](https://royalhistsoc.org/) publishes position papers on good practice in historical research and teaching. Those apply to scholarly work rather than to consumer software, but they are a reasonable yardstick for anyone claiming to teach history.

## Question five: does it say what it is not for?

The most useful sentence in any learning product is the one that names its limits.

**Our app passes this one clearly.** Its disclaimer screen states that it is "not a substitute for textbooks, peer-reviewed scholarship, or primary sources", and that "historians disagree about many topics covered, and interpretations change over time". Its world map carries a footer noting that coordinates "are approximate and intended for learning, not navigation". Those are honest statements, and they are the right ones.

Hold any app to that standard. A product that positions itself as a replacement for a course, a library or an archive has told you something important about how carefully it was built.

## Where our app's content is weakest, stated plainly

Three structural problems are worth naming, because a reader running this checklist would find them.

**Duplicate records.** The content was assembled in waves and some subjects ended up with more than one entry. A handful of those duplicates disagree with each other on dates, which means the app can show two different end years for the same conflict with no citation for either. Treat any date in it as a prompt to check rather than an answer, as [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) explains in more detail.

**Classification errors.** A small number of events are filed under an era band centuries away from their actual date, so they appear under the wrong era filter.

**Feature descriptions that do not match behaviour.** Two of the games are misleadingly named. The crossword validates each typed answer against a stored answer string rather than reading an interlocking solution grid, so in practice it is a set of independent clue boxes rather than a true crossword. The map challenge is a text-only multiple-choice quiz with no map interaction at all. Neither is a content-accuracy problem, but both are the kind of gap this checklist is meant to surface, and neither should be described as anything other than what it is.

## What to do with any history app, ours included

Use it for the job it can actually do. An unsourced app is good for orientation — finding out what existed, roughly when, and what connects to what — and good for retrieval practice on material you have already verified. Our [app](/apps/world-history-timeline-sim/) is built for that: a large browsable corpus with era, region and country filters, and decision-based modes that make causation concrete.

It is not built to be cited, and it is not a substitute for teachers, scholarly books, archives or primary sources. When a claim matters, follow it to a museum catalogue, a national archive, a university department or a peer-reviewed article. UNESCO's framework for [open educational resources](https://www.unesco.org/en/open-educational-resources) is a useful reminder of what openly licensed, quality-assured teaching material is supposed to look like, and how much of what circulates as "educational content" does not meet it.
