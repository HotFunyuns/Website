Nine is ten minus one. So nine lots of a number are ten lots with one lot taken away, and **47 × 9 = 470 − 47 = 423**. That single observation covers 9, 99, 999 and every other number sitting one below a power of ten, and it replaces a multiplication most people find awkward with a shift and a subtraction.

## The move at nine

Shift the number one place left, then subtract the original once.

- **63 × 9** = 630 − 63 = **567**
- **28 × 9** = 280 − 28 = **252**
- **154 × 9** = 1,540 − 154 = **1,386**

The subtraction is the only real work, and it is the kind that [counting up as a subtraction strategy](/blog/counting-up-subtraction/) handles cleanly if the borrowing bothers you: from 154 to 1,540 is 46 to reach 200, then 1,340 to reach 1,540, giving 1,386.

The error worth naming is subtracting 1 instead of one *lot*. Writing 470 − 1 = 469 for 47 × 9 is a slip that a rough estimate kills instantly: nine lots of 47 must be a little under 470, not one below it.

## The same move at 99, 999 and beyond

The identity generalises: *n* × (10ᵏ − 1) = *n* × 10ᵏ − *n*. Shift by however many places the power of ten requires, then subtract the original once.

- **38 × 99** = 3,800 − 38 = **3,762**
- **84 × 99** = 8,400 − 84 = **8,316**
- **26 × 999** = 26,000 − 26 = **25,974**
- **125 × 9,999** = 1,250,000 − 125 = **1,249,875**

This is where the method genuinely beats the alternatives. Nobody wants to multiply 125 by 9,999 any other way, and the two steps here are a shift you cannot get wrong and a subtraction of a small number from a round one.

The related family runs the other direction — numbers just *above* a power of ten. For 101 you shift and add rather than subtract: 47 × 101 = 4,700 + 47 = **4,747**. Same identity, opposite sign.

## Numbers near a power of ten, generally

Nothing about the method requires the gap to be one. Any number close to a round figure can be handled as the round figure minus the difference.

- **47 × 8** = 470 − 94 = **376**, since 8 = 10 − 2 and two lots of 47 is 94.
- **45 × 98** = 4,500 − 90 = **4,410**, since 98 = 100 − 2.
- **72 × 97** = 7,200 − 216 = **6,984**, since 97 = 100 − 3 and three lots of 72 is 216.

The gap has to stay small enough that multiplying by it is trivial. At 10 − 4 you are multiplying by four to save multiplying by six, which is no saving at all. In practice the method pays for gaps of one, two and three, and the further shortcuts for numbers sitting near 100 on either side are covered in [multiplication shortcuts and why they work](/blog/multiplication-shortcuts-explained/).

## Why every multiple of nine has digits that add to nine

The pattern in the nine times table — 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, and so on, each with digits summing to 9 — is not a coincidence of small numbers. It follows from place value.

Ten is nine plus one. A hundred is 99 plus one. A thousand is 999 plus one. Every power of ten is one more than a multiple of nine. So a number like 3,762 can be rewritten as

3 × (999 + 1) + 7 × (99 + 1) + 6 × (9 + 1) + 2

which separates into a pile of multiples of nine — 3 × 999, 7 × 99, 6 × 9 — plus 3 + 7 + 6 + 2, the digit sum. The multiples of nine are divisible by nine no matter what the digits are, so the whole number is divisible by nine exactly when its digit sum is.

NRICH's article on divisibility tests, by Tim Rowland at the University of Cambridge, sets out this argument directly, observing that ten is nine plus one and that every power of ten is therefore one more than a multiple of nine, which is what makes the digit-sum tests for three and nine work. A companion NRICH article on digital roots notes that the pattern is usually first met by children spotting it in the nine times table, and that repeatedly adding digits until a single digit remains is the same operation as working modulo nine. The NRICH problem *Adding All Nine* makes the connection concrete from the other side: the digits 1 to 9 total 45, so any number built from all of them once is divisible by nine.

## Using the digit sum as a check

Any product with 9, 99, 999 or 9,999 as a factor is a multiple of nine, because 99 = 9 × 11, 999 = 9 × 111 and 9,999 = 9 × 1,111. So the answer's digits must reduce to nine. Check the results above:

- 423 → 4 + 2 + 3 = 9
- 3,762 → 3 + 7 + 6 + 2 = 18 → 1 + 8 = 9
- 25,974 → 2 + 5 + 9 + 7 + 4 = 27 → 2 + 7 = 9
- 1,249,875 → 1 + 2 + 4 + 9 + 8 + 7 + 5 = 36 → 3 + 6 = 9

The check costs about a second and it catches the most common failure, which is losing or mis-shifting a digit.

It is not a proof. Swapping two digits leaves the digit sum untouched, so 3,762 written as 3,726 passes the test and is still wrong. Treat the digit sum as a filter that removes most errors, not as a verification, and keep a rough estimate alongside it.

## The finger trick, and what it is not

Hold up both hands and fold down the *n*-th finger from the left. For 7 × 9, fold the seventh: six fingers stand to its left and three to its right, giving 63.

It is correct, and its scope is exactly 1 to 10 times 9. The reason is that the fingers left standing count *n* − 1 tens, and those to the right count 10 − *n* units, which sums to 10(*n* − 1) + (10 − *n*) = 9*n*. The construction needs ten fingers, so it cannot reach 11 × 9 or 12 × 9, and it says nothing about 99.

That makes it a lookup device for one row of the times table rather than a method. Worth showing a child; not worth carrying as an adult, because the shift-and-subtract move covers the same row and everything beyond it.
