There are two words for hello in Khmer and they are not a long version and a short version of the same thing. **ជំរាបសួរ** (*chum reap suor*) is formal. **សួស្ដី** (*suosdei*) is informal. Which one you use is a statement about the person in front of you, and a phrasebook that gives you only the second one has handed you a greeting that is wrong for a large share of the people you will meet.

That is the shape of Khmer politeness in miniature: it lives in word choice, and the choice is made before you open your mouth.

## Two greetings, two registers

Use **chum reap suor** with elders, with strangers, with anyone of evidently higher standing, and in any situation where you are not sure. The matching parting phrase is **លាសិនហើយ** (*léa sen haeuy*).

Use **suosdei** with friends, with peers, with people of roughly your own age in an informal setting.

For a visitor the practical rule is short: **default to the formal one.** Being slightly too careful with a peer costs nothing. Being too familiar with an elder is a real misstep, and it is the one a tourist is most likely to make, because informal greetings are what phrasebooks print.

Time-specific greetings exist and are built on the *suosdei* element: **អរុណសួស្ដី** (*arun suosdei*) for the morning, **ទិវាសួស្ដី** (*tivea suosdei*) for the afternoon, **សាយ័ណ្ហសួស្ដី** (*sayon suosdei*) for the evening, and **រាត្រីសួស្តី** (*reatri suosdei*) for good night. They are somewhat formal in feel, and the two general greetings will cover you.

## Register is vocabulary, and it goes deeper than greetings

Khmer employs a system of registers in which a speaker has to remain conscious of the social standing of the person being addressed. There are registers for common speech, polite speech, speaking to or about monks, and speaking to or about royalty, and they differ in their verbs, their words for body parts, and their pronouns.

The standard illustration is the verb *to eat*, which has five forms:

| Context | Form |
|---|---|
| Between intimates, or of animals | *sii* |
| Polite reference to commoners | *nyam* |
| Of those of higher social status | *pisa* or *totuel tien* |
| Of monks | *chan* |
| Of royalty | *saoy* |

One everyday verb, five words, selected by who is eating and who you are talking to.

A beginner needs the first two rows. The point of seeing all five is to understand that Khmer politeness is not a layer you add to a sentence — it is built into which sentence you choose.

## Pronoun avoidance, and why "you" is awkward

The pronominal system carries the same honorific load, and Khmer sidesteps it in a way that surprises English speakers.

**Khmer exhibits pronoun avoidance.** Among people who know each other, kinship terms, nicknames and proper names are frequently used instead of pronouns — including for the first person. Someone may refer to themselves by name or by a kinship term rather than saying *I*. Subject pronouns are also dropped altogether in ordinary conversation.

The pronoun forms that do exist are stratified. **ខ្ញុំ** (*khnhom*) is the neutral first person and is the one to learn. There is an intimate set below it used with close friends or subordinates, and honorific forms above it. Getting this wrong in the downward direction is the error to avoid.

For a beginner the workable strategy is: use *khnhom* for yourself, use **អ្នក** (*ânâk*) or a kinship term for the person you are addressing, and drop the pronoun entirely when the context makes it obvious. That last option is not a shortcut — it is what Khmer speakers do.

Alongside the pronouns sit the polite acknowledgement particles, which differ by the speaker's gender and are used constantly. [Learning Khmer for beginners](/blog/learn-khmer-beginners-guide/) covers those and the wider politeness layer in detail.

## The sampeah

The gesture accompanying a greeting is the **សំពះ** (*sampeah*), made with the palms pressed together.

It is based on the Indian *Añjali Mudrā*, the same ancestor as the *namaste* gesture, and it reached Cambodia through the spread of Hinduism and Buddhism into Southeast Asia. The Thai *wai* and the Lao *nop* belong to the same family, which is why they look similar and why they are not the same thing.

The height at which the hands are held varies with who is being greeted, and it carries real meaning. As a visitor, the safe behaviour is to return a sampeah offered to you rather than to initiate one.

## Why the first unit teaches both registers

Most beginner courses teach one greeting. Ours teaches two, in the first unit, with the register difference as the content of the lesson rather than as a footnote.

That was a change, and it came from a failure mode we could see clearly once it was described to us.

The original unit taught **suosdei** — the informal greeting — because it is shorter, easier to pronounce, and the one most commonly printed in phrasebooks. Perfectly defensible on its own terms.

The result was learners arriving in Cambodia with exactly one greeting, and that greeting being the wrong register for a significant share of the people they met. And the failure was invisible to them. Nobody corrects a foreign visitor's register. Cambodians are gracious about it and the exchange proceeds, so a learner can use an over-familiar greeting with an elderly person for two weeks and come home believing it went well.

Teaching one greeting is not teaching a subset of the system. It is teaching a version of the system in which register does not exist — and register is not a refinement in Khmer, it is the primary mechanism by which respect is expressed.

So both go in the first unit, together, with the selection rule attached: formal by default, informal with peers.

The cost is a denser first lesson. A learner who wants "how do I say hello in Khmer" gets two answers and a rule, which is more than they asked for, and some of them will remember only the first one they read. We would rather that than teach, in the most prominent slot in the course, that Khmer greeting is a single word.
