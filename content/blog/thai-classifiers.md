In English you can usually put a number straight before a noun: two dogs, three books. Thai cannot. A number needs a **classifier**, a counting word chosen to suit the noun, and the order is different too. This guide covers the pattern, the classifiers a beginner needs first, and the other places classifiers turn up.

## The pattern: noun, number, classifier

Wikipedia's article on Thai explains that nouns do not change for plural and that a count is made as **noun + number + classifier**. Its example:

| Thai | ครู ห้า คน |
| --- | --- |
| RTGS | khru ha khon |
| Word by word | teacher five person |
| Meaning | five teachers |

Here *khon* is the classifier for people. Wikipedia compares English, where classifiers are absent ("four chairs") or optional ("two bottles of beer" or "two beers"), while in Thai **a classifier is almost always used**: "chair four item", "beer two bottle".

## The classifiers to learn first

Each noun takes a particular classifier. Wiktionary lists these common ones:

| Classifier | RTGS | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| คน | khon | most people |
| ตัว | tua | animals; also digits, letters and certain objects |
| อัน | an | small things: the general classifier for small objects |
| เล่ม | lem | books, candles and sharp-edged objects such as knives |
| คัน | khan | vehicles, and long objects such as umbrellas, spoons and forks |
| หลัง | lang | houses and tents |
| ลูก | luk | balls, round objects and fruit |
| ใบ | bai | hollow or flat things: plates, glasses, pages |
| แผ่น | phaen | paper, CDs and other thin, flat objects |

Some containers work as classifiers too, as in Wikipedia's "beer two bottle", with ขวด, *khuat*, "bottle". Wikipedia's article on Thai numerals adds that **there is no hierarchy** among classifiers, and that variations on the pattern do occur.

**One warning.** Wiktionary marks *tua* as **derogatory and offensive if applied to humans**. People take *khon*.

## A worked example: three counts

1. **Two books**: หนังสือ (*nangsue*, book) + สอง (*song*, two) + เล่ม (*lem*) = **หนังสือสองเล่ม**.
2. **Three cars**: รถ (*rot*, vehicle) + สาม (*sam*) + คัน (*khan*) = **รถสามคัน**.
3. **Two women**: Wikipedia's own example, ผู้หญิงสองคน, *phuying song khon*.

The noun always comes first, and the classifier always follows the number.

## One glass or a glass

*Nueng*, "one", can sit on either side of the classifier, with a difference in meaning. Wikipedia contrasts **แก้วหนึ่งใบ**, *kaeo nueng bai*, "one glass", which counts, with **แก้วใบหนึ่ง**, *kaeo bai nueng*, "a glass", which works like the English indefinite article.

## Every, some and none

Classifiers also follow some quantifiers. Wikipedia's examples use *khon*: **นักเรียนทุกคน**, "every student", and **ครูบางคน**, "some teachers". For "none" there is no classifier: the pattern is **ไม่มี** (*mai mi*, "there is no") + noun.

## This one and which one

With "this" and "that", the order is **noun + classifier + demonstrative**. Wikipedia's example is **หมาตัวนี้**, *ma tua ni*, "this dog". Questions work the same way with ไหน, *nai*: **แมวตัวไหน**, *maeo tua nai*, "which cat?".

## Asking how many

Use **กี่**, *kii*, before the classifier. Wiktionary notes that *kii* is followed by a classifier or a common noun, and gives two set phrases:

- **กี่บาท**, *kii baht*: "how much?", literally "how many baht";
- **กี่โมง**, *kii mong*: "what time?".

So "how many people?" is **กี่คน**, *kii khon*. [Thai numbers](/blog/thai-numbers/) covers the numbers themselves.

## Four classifier slips

- **Dropping the classifier**: Wikipedia notes a classifier is almost always used in Thai, so "two book" with no *lem* breaks the pattern.
- **Putting the number first**: the noun leads.
- **Using tua for people**, which Wiktionary marks as derogatory and offensive.
- **Forgetting the fallback**: Wiktionary lists *an* as the general classifier for small inanimate things.

## Classifiers and measure words

Linguists separate two ideas that English blurs. Wikipedia's article on classifiers explains that a **classifier** goes with the natural unit of a countable noun, like *khon* for a person, while a **measure word** names a quantity, like a cupful or a bottle, and can go with mass nouns such as water. English has a few classifier-like words, such as *head* in "five head of cattle", but the article notes that classifiers are absent or marginal in European languages. In Thai, both kinds sit in the same slot after the number, which is why *khuat*, "bottle", fits the pattern as easily as *khon*.

## Classifiers across the region

Wikipedia notes that numeral classifiers play an important role in East and Southeast Asian languages, including Chinese, Korean, Japanese and Vietnamese.
