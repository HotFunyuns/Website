A cricket scorecard is a grid of numbers, and five of them do most of the work in judging players: batting average, batting strike rate, bowling average, economy rate and bowling strike rate. Each answers a different question. This guide defines them as Wikipedia's statistics articles do and calculates each from one invented season.

## The five core statistics

| Statistic | Formula | Measures | Better is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Batting average | Runs ÷ times out | Runs per dismissal | Higher |
| Batting strike rate | Runs ÷ balls faced × 100 | Scoring speed | Higher |
| Bowling average | Runs conceded ÷ wickets | Cost of each wicket | Lower |
| Economy rate | Runs conceded ÷ overs bowled | Runs given away per over | Lower |
| Bowling strike rate | Balls bowled ÷ wickets | How often wickets come | Lower |

## Batting average: dismissals, not innings

Wikipedia defines a batting average as total runs divided by the number of times the batter has been out, usually given to two decimal places. Dividing by dismissals rather than innings is the point that confuses newcomers.

**Worked example.** A batter scores 612 runs in 18 innings and is not out in three of them.

| Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Times out: 18 − 3 | 15 |
| Batting average: 612 ÷ 15 | **40.80** |
| Runs per innings, for comparison: 612 ÷ 18 | 34.00 |

The not-outs lift the average above runs per innings, because an unfinished innings adds runs without adding a dismissal. Wikipedia notes averages can be strongly affected by not-outs: Phil Tufnell's ODI average was 15, despite a highest score of 5 not out, because he scored 15 runs in 10 innings and was out only once. A career with very few innings can also produce an extreme figure. Wikipedia notes, for example, that Kurtis Patterson averaged 144 in Test cricket from just two innings, falling short of the 20-innings qualification usually used for records.

The record among qualified players belongs to Australia's Sir Donald Bradman, at **99.94**. Wikipedia adds context: an average over 50 is exceptional, and only four other players average over 60.

## Batting strike rate: speed

Batting strike rate is runs per 100 balls faced. Our batter faced 480 balls: 612 ÷ 480 × 100 = **127.50**. Strike rate matters most where balls are limited. Wikipedia's strike-rate article notes that because each team faces a limited number of balls in limited-overs cricket, the faster a batter scores, the more runs the team can accumulate, and that strike rates over 150 are becoming common in Twenty20 cricket.

## Bowling average, economy and strike rate

Wikipedia defines bowling average as runs conceded per wicket, economy rate as runs conceded per over and bowling strike rate as balls bowled per wicket.

**Worked example.** A bowler sends down 62.4 overs, conceding 318 runs and taking 14 wickets.

First convert the overs. A ball is a sixth of an over, so 62.4 overs is 62 overs and 4 balls: 376 balls, or 62.667 overs. Wikipedia's economy-rate article makes the same conversion in its own example, dividing 51 runs by 16.1667 overs for a spell written as 16.1.

| Statistic | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bowling average | 318 ÷ 14 | **22.71** runs per wicket |
| Economy rate | 318 ÷ 62.667 | **5.07** runs per over |
| Bowling strike rate | 376 ÷ 14 | **26.86** balls per wicket |

Wikipedia's economy-rate article notes that byes and leg byes are not charged to the bowler, while wides and no balls are; [how cricket scoring works](/blog/how-cricket-scoring-works/) explains why.

## Reading the numbers together

- **Average versus strike rate.** A batter averaging 45 at a strike rate of 70 and one averaging 30 at 150 score in very different ways; Wikipedia's strike-rate article says strike rate is of considerably more importance in limited-overs cricket.
- **Economy versus wickets.** Wikipedia's economy-rate article says economy is typically more important in limited-overs cricket, where bowlers must restrict runs before the overs run out, than in Tests, where an innings usually ends when ten wickets fall.
- **Sample size.** Wikipedia's bowling-average article warns that with only a few wickets, an average can be artificially high or low and unstable; the Kurtis Patterson example shows the same for batting.
