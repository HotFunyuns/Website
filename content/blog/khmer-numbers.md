Khmer counting has one surprise near the start and then settles into a pattern that anyone who has met Thai or Lao numbers will recognise. Learn five words, the trick that builds six to nine, and a set of place-value words, and you can read a price tag in Phnom Penh. Romanisations below follow UNGEGN, one of the systems Wikipedia tabulates alongside IPA; romanisation hides vowel detail, so check each word with audio.

## One to five, and the five that builds six to nine

| Number | Khmer digit | Khmer word | UNGEGN |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | ០ | សូន្យ | sony |
| 1 | ១ | មួយ | muŏy |
| 2 | ២ | ពីរ | pir |
| 3 | ៣ | បី | bei |
| 4 | ៤ | បួន | buŏn |
| 5 | ៥ | ប្រាំ | brăm |
| 6 | ៦ | ប្រាំមួយ | brămmuŏy |
| 7 | ៧ | ប្រាំពីរ | brămpir |
| 8 | ៨ | ប្រាំបី | brămbei |
| 9 | ៩ | ប្រាំបួន | brămbuŏn |
| 10 | ១០ | ដប់ | dáb |

Wikipedia describes the spoken numbers as **biquinary**: base 5 and base 10 both appear. Six is literally "five-one", seven "five-two", and so on to nine. It also traces one to five to proto-Austroasiatic, and zero to Sanskrit *śūnya*. Two small notes from the same source: before a classifier, *muŏy* is reduced to something like *mə* in ordinary speech, and *brăm* in six to nine may be pronounced closer to *pəm*.

## Ten, twenty and the tens borrowed through Thai

From ten the system turns decimal. Eleven is *dáb muŏy*, ten plus one; fifteen is *dáb brăm*. Wikipedia adds a colloquial alternative for the teens, using *dândáb* after the unit: fifteen can be *brăm dândáb*.

Twenty is its own word, **mphey** (ម្ភៃ), which Wikipedia explains as a contraction of "one" plus an older word for twenty. The tens after that look very different:

| Number | Khmer word | UNGEGN |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30 | សាមសិប | samsĕb |
| 40 | សែសិប | sêsĕb |
| 50 | ហាសិប | hasĕb |
| 60 | ហុកសិប | hŏksĕb |
| 70 | ចិតសិប | chĕtsĕb |
| 80 | ប៉ែតសិប | pêtsĕb |
| 90 | កៅសិប | kausĕb |

These, Wikipedia says, were borrowed from a southern Middle Chinese variety by way of a Tai language, most likely Thai, which is why *sam sip*, *ha sip* and the rest sound so close to [Thai numbers](/blog/thai-numbers/). The pieces cannot be split in Khmer: *sê* does not mean four on its own and *sĕb* does not mean ten. In informal speech the *sĕb* can be dropped, so 81 can be *pêt muŏy* instead of *pêtsĕb muŏy*.

## Hundreds, ten-thousands and the word kaôdĕ

| Number | Khmer word | UNGEGN |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | មួយរយ | muŏy rôy |
| 1,000 | មួយពាន់ | muŏy poăn |
| 10,000 | មួយម៉ឺន | muŏy mœn |
| 100,000 | មួយសែន | muŏy sên |
| 1,000,000 | មួយលាន | muŏy léan |
| 10,000,000 | មួយកោដិ | muŏy kaôdĕ |

Like Thai, Khmer has single words for **10,000** (*mœn*) and **100,000** (*sên*). Wikipedia lists the first five as borrowings from Thai and *kaôdĕ* from Sanskrit and Pali *koṭi*. It also flags an ambiguity: *kaôdĕ* usually means ten million, but in some areas it is used colloquially for a billion, so speakers sometimes say *dáb léan*, "ten million", to be clear. Dialects also vary above a thousand; *dáb poăn*, literally "ten thousand", is listed as an alternative to *muŏy mœn*.

## A worked example: four numbers you will hear

1. **27**: twenty, then five-two, so *mphey brămpir*.
2. **36**: thirty, then five-one, so *samsĕb brămmuŏy*.
3. **4,100**: four thousands and one hundred, so *buŏn poăn muŏy rôy*.
4. **15,000**: one ten-thousand and five thousands, so *muŏy mœn brăm poăn*.

Ordinals put **ti** (ទី) first: *ti muŏy*, first; *ti pir*, second, the same pattern Wikipedia compares with Thai *thi* and Vietnamese *thứ*.

## The Khmer digits ០ to ៩

Khmer has its own digits, ០ ១ ២ ៣ ៤ ៥ ៦ ៧ ៨ ៩, used in the same positional way as Arabic numerals: ២៧ is 27. Wikipedia notes that they have been in use since at least the early 7th century and that a Khmer inscription gives the earliest extant material evidence of zero as a numerical figure. Thai and Lao digits descend from the same source and still look alike. If the digits render as boxes on your phone, [Khmer fonts on Android](/blog/khmer-fonts-on-android/) explains why, and [the Khmer script explained](/blog/khmer-script-explained/) covers the rest of the writing system.

## Riel, dollars and asking the price

The **riel** (រៀល, sign ៛) is Cambodia's currency. Wikipedia lists banknotes from 100៛ to 50,000៛ in frequent use, while coins of 50 to 500 riels are rarely seen. It also notes that the US dollar works as a de facto currency: the riel remains in common use in the provinces, cities and tourist areas use the dollar heavily, and a rate of **4,000 riels to the dollar** is widely used in retail, with change for fractions of a dollar given in riel. At that rate, $2.50 is 10,000 riels, *muŏy mœn riĕl*.

To ask the price, Wiktionary lists **ប៉ុន្មាន** as interrogative "how much?, how many?". Its example sentence uses the same word to ask the time, so it is worth learning early.
