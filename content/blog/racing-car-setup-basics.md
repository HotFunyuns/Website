**Setup** is the collection of adjustments, wing angles, suspension stiffness, wheel angles and more, that decide how a car uses its grip. This guide explains the core trade-offs, drawing on Wikipedia's articles on downforce, anti-roll bars and camber, and the rules that freeze setup in Formula 1.

## Downforce versus drag

Wikipedia defines **downforce** as a downward lift force created by a vehicle's aerodynamic features, whose purpose is to let the car go faster by increasing the vertical force on the tyres and so creating more grip. The catch is **drag**: the same wings slow the car on the straights. Wikipedia's summary is the first rule of setup: the optimum is almost always a compromise between the two. It also notes that the aerodynamic setup can vary considerably between tracks, depending on the length of the straights and the types of corners.

| Choice | Gains | Costs | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| More wing, high downforce | Corner speed | Top speed on straights | Twisty circuits with few long straights |
| Less wing, low downforce | Top speed | Corner speed | Circuits dominated by long straights |

Downforce scales with speed. Wikipedia notes that, for a constant coefficient, downforce is proportional to the square of airspeed, so a car at 200 km/h makes four times the downforce it makes at 100 km/h. Wikipedia adds that downforce needs a certain minimum speed to have a significant effect, and distinguishes this aerodynamic grip from mechanical grip, which comes from the car's mass, tyres and suspension. It also notes that some cars have had aerodynamics unstable enough that a minor change in angle of attack or ride height caused large changes in downforce.

## Balance: understeer and oversteer

Grip is shared between front and rear tyres, and the balance between them shapes how the car feels. Wikipedia's article on understeer and oversteer describes the limit cases in racing drivers' terms:

- **Understeer, or "push":** the front tyres become saturated and slip first, so the car does not turn enough.
- **Oversteer, or "loose":** the rear tyres let go first, so the car turns more sharply than intended and can spin.

A setup aims for a balance the driver can control at the limit. [The racing line explained](/blog/racing-line-explained/) shows why using all the available grip matters, and how trail braking helps a car turn in.

## Anti-roll bars: tuning the balance

An **anti-roll bar** links the wheels on one axle and resists body roll in corners. Wikipedia notes its other function is tuning handling balance: understeer or oversteer can be changed by adjusting the proportion of total roll stiffness at the front and rear.

| Change | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Stiffer front bar, or softer rear | More of the roll stiffness at the front: more understeer |
| Stiffer rear bar, or softer front | More at the rear: less understeer, more oversteer |

## Camber: angling the wheels

**Camber** is the angle of the wheel seen from the front. When the tops of the wheels lean inwards, it is **negative camber**. Wikipedia explains that negative camber improves grip in corners, because as the car rolls it turns the tyre's contact surface outwards to match, maximising the contact patch. The cost: any camber affects the contact patch when driving straight, and excessive camber can increase tyre wear and impair handling. So camber is another compromise, between cornering grip, straight-line traction and tyre life; [racing tyre strategy](/blog/racing-tire-strategy/) explains why tyre life matters.

## Parc fermé: setup is a commitment

In Formula 1, setup choices are locked in early. Under Article B3.5 of the FIA's 2026 Formula 1 Sporting Regulations (Section B, Issue 09), each car is in **parc fermé** from the moment it first leaves the pit lane during qualifying until the start of the race. In that period no part of the car may be replaced or modified and no setup change made, other than the work listed in Appendix B2 or approved by the Technical Delegate; a breach means starting the race from the pit lane. Wikipedia's article on parc fermé gives the same principle: teams cannot make major set-up changes between qualifying and race day.

Appendix B2 lists the permitted work, such as bleeding the brakes, de-glazing brake friction material, cleaning the car and adjusting the front wing profiles' position within the rules, while no parts may be added, removed or replaced on the wing. That is why teams must choose a setup that works for both a single qualifying lap and a full race distance; [how racing qualifying works](/blog/how-racing-qualifying-works/) explains the qualifying side.
