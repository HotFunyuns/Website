Italian restaurant phrases are a small, high-return set: a greeting, a way to ask for a table, one polite verb for ordering, and the word for the bill. Around them sits the menu itself, which is organised into courses with Italian names you will see on a printed *menù*. This reference gives the phrases first, then the courses, then the charges that appear on the bill, with a worked example at the end.

## The phrases, from the door to the bill

| Situation | Italian | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| arriving | *Buonasera.* | Good evening. |
| asking for a table | *Un tavolo per due, per favore.* | A table for two, please. |
| mentioning a booking | *Ho una prenotazione.* | I have a reservation. |
| ordering | *Vorrei gli spaghetti.* | I'd like the spaghetti. |
| water | *Acqua frizzante, per favore.* | Sparkling water, please. |
| calling the waiter | *Scusi!* | Excuse me! |
| paying | *Il conto, per favore.* | The bill, please. |

A few of these words are worth knowing on their own. Wiktionary lists **vorrei** as the first-person conditional of *volere*, "to want", and notes it is **used to make polite requests**, "I would like…" or "please give me…". It gives **per favore** as "please", with *per piacere* as a synonym; **prenotazione** as "booking, reservation"; **tavolo** as "table"; **frizzante** as "fizzy, sparkling (of wine or water)"; and **cameriere** as "waiter", with the feminine *cameriera*.

**Conto** is the bill. Wiktionary lists it as a bill "for a meal, hotel, etc.", as well as a calculation and a bank account.

Wiktionary marks **scusi** as the formal second-person "excuse me", the form to use with someone you address as *Lei*; the entry cross-refers to *scusa*. Which greeting to use at the door, and when to switch to *Lei*, is covered in [Italian greetings](/blog/italian-greetings-formal-informal/).

## Reading the courses on a menu

Wikipedia's article on Italian cuisine sets out the traditional stages of a meal.

| Course | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| aperitivo | an aperitif before a large meal, such as Campari, Prosecco or a Spritz |
| antipasto | "before the meal": cheese, prosciutto, sliced sausage, marinated vegetables or fish, bruschetta |
| primo | first course: a hot dish such as pasta, risotto, gnocchi or soup |
| secondo | second course, the main dish: usually fish or meat |
| contorno | side dish: a salad or cooked vegetables |
| formaggio e frutta | cheese and fruit |
| dolce | dessert: cakes such as tiramisu, biscuits or ice cream |
| caffè | coffee |
| digestivo | a digestive such as grappa, amaro or limoncello |

You do not have to work through the whole list. Wikipedia notes that **full-course meals are now mainly reserved for special events** such as weddings, and that everyday meals include **a first or second course, sometimes both, a side dish and coffee**. It also says the *primo* is the most frequent course, with the others often seen as somewhat optional.

Wikipedia also notes that a traditional menu features salad along with the main course.

## Trattoria, osteria or ristorante

The sign over the door tells you something about what is inside. Wikipedia's table of food establishments describes:

- **Trattoria**: often family-run, with inexpensive prices and an informal atmosphere.
- **Osteria**: focused on simple regional food, often with **no written menu**, so be ready to listen to the dishes of the day. Wikipedia adds that the name has also become fashionable for upscale rustic restaurants.
- **Ristorante**: often offers upscale cuisine and printed menus.
- **Pizzeria**: specialises in pizza, often from wood-fired ovens.
- **Tavola calda**: literally "hot table", with pre-made regional dishes.

## Coperto and the bill

The charge that most often needs explaining is **coperto**. Wiktionary lists its meanings as "cover", "cover charge" and "place (at dining table)", and Wikipedia's article on cover charges says **pane e coperto**, a "bread and cover" charge, is **nearly ubiquitous in Italy**. It notes that the Lazio region, which includes Rome, began requiring restaurants to remove it in 1995, that the European Union ruled the regional law invalid in 1998, and that the region has continued to try to abolish the practice.

Prices use a decimal comma, so *2,50 €* is two euros fifty. [Italian numbers](/blog/italian-numbers/) covers how to read and say them.

## A worked bill for two

Take two diners and invented prices for illustration: two *primi* at 11 € each, one *secondo* at 16 €, one *contorno* at 5 €, a bottle of water at 3 €, and a *coperto* of 2,50 € per person.

1. Primi: 2 × 11 = 22 €
2. Secondo: 16 €
3. Contorno: 5 €
4. Water: 3 €
5. Coperto: 2 × 2,50 = 5 €

Total: 22 + 16 + 5 + 3 + 5 = **51 €**, said *cinquantuno euro*. Notice the coperto adds 5 € to a 46 € order, which is why it is worth checking the menu for it before you sit down.
