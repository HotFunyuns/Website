The first phrase is 侬好.

A Mandarin reader will look at that and see two familiar characters, and will pronounce neither of them the way a Shanghai speaker does. 侬 is a character Mandarin has largely retired — archaic, literary, not something you say — and in Shanghainese it is the ordinary word for *you*, used every day, in the greeting everyone uses.

We open the Shanghainese course with it deliberately, and the reason is in the next section.

## Greetings

**侬好** — hello. The everyday greeting. In one common romanisation it comes out as *nóng hō*, but see the note on spellings further down.

**大家好** — hello everybody, for addressing a group.

**侬早** — good morning, literally "you early". **夜到好** for good evening, **晚安** for good night.

**再会** — goodbye. A parting formula rather than a literal "again meet", though that is what it is made of.

## The greeting that is not about food

**饭吃过伐?** — literally "rice eaten yet?", functionally "how are you" or "hello".

This is a genuine greeting across much of the Chinese-speaking world and it is not an enquiry about your lunch. The beginner error — answering with a detailed account of what you ate — is common enough to be a running joke. The expected reply is the equivalent of "yes, thanks": **吃过了**.

There are longer forms of the same question, including ones where the pronoun appears first, which is worth noticing because it shows you the question particle in more than one arrangement.

## How are you, and the particle that makes it a question

**侬好伐?** — how are you.

That final 伐, romanised *va*, is the Shanghainese yes-no question particle, and it does the job Mandarin gives to 吗. Attaching it to a statement turns the statement into a question, which makes it one of the highest-value single characters you can learn.

Replies run from **蛮好** ("pretty good") through the equivalent of "so-so" to "not great". Adding "and you?" is a short tag on the end.

## Names and origins

**侬叫啥名子?** — what's your name. **吾叫 ...** — my name is ...

Two things to spot here. First, 啥 (*sa*) is the Shanghainese interrogative for "what", where Mandarin uses 什么. Second, first person appears as 吾 rather than the Mandarin 我 in many written representations, and the reading is a Wu one either way.

**侬是啥地方人?** — where are you from, literally "you are what-place person". The word for "person" here comes out closer to *ning* than to Mandarin's *rén*, which is a good example of a sound correspondence that is regular between the two but not guessable without knowing the rule.

## Where Mandarin misleads you, in three places

**The pronouns.** 侬 for *you*, not 你. This is not a pronunciation variant of the same word, it is a different word, and it is the most frequent word in any conversation.

**The question particle.** 伐, not 吗.

**The character readings.** Every character on this page has a Shanghainese reading that a Mandarin speaker will not produce by default. The characters are shared and the sound is not, which is why [Shanghainese vs Mandarin](/blog/shanghainese-vs-mandarin-differences/) is worth reading before you build a study plan on your existing Chinese.

## A note on the spellings

Every romanisation in this article should be read as "one scheme's version of this". Shanghainese has no single standard, and the same word appears with visibly different spellings across sources — not because anyone is wrong, but because the schemes assign different values to the same letters.

The practical instruction is to pick a system and stay in it. [Romanising Shanghainese](/blog/shanghainese-romanisation-choices/) explains which one we use and what it costs.

## Learn the contour, not the syllables

One more piece of mechanics, because it changes how you should drill these.

Shanghainese word-level tone sandhi is left-prominent: the first syllable sets the tone contour for the whole word, and the underlying tones of the following syllables have essentially no effect on the result. So 侬好 is not the tone of 侬 followed by the tone of 好. It is one shape.

Treat every phrase on this page as a unit with a melody. Imitate the whole thing. Do not assemble it from parts, because the parts, said alone, are pronounced in a way that rarely occurs in speech. [Shanghainese tones and tone sandhi](/blog/shanghainese-tones-and-tone-sandhi/) works through why the tone count itself is a contested number.

## Why we open with 侬好 specifically

The first unit of [Learn Shanghainese Wu Chinese](/apps/learn-shanghainese/) opens on greetings, which is unremarkable. What was chosen deliberately is that the very first line contains **a character whose Mandarin reading is actively misleading**, and that the lesson points this out rather than glossing over it.

The reasoning is about habit formation. The learners most likely to start a Shanghainese course are the ones with some Mandarin, and the default behaviour of that learner is to read a character, retrieve the Mandarin pronunciation, and apply a mental accent filter to it. That behaviour produces something between forty and eighty per cent right often enough to feel like it is working, and it is very hard to dislodge once it has been rewarded for a few weeks.

Opening on 侬 breaks it on line one. The Mandarin reading is not a close approximation that needs adjusting; it is a different word that Mandarin speakers rarely say. There is no accent filter that gets you there.

The trade-off is that the first lesson is harder than it needs to be. A gentler opening — a phrase whose characters have transparent Mandarin cognates — would feel more encouraging and would produce a better first session. We took the sharper version because the alternative teaches, in the most memorable slot in the course, exactly the habit the rest of the course has to undo.
