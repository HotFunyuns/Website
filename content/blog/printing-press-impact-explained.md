The printing press did not begin the history of printing, and it did not begin it alone. Printing from carved wooden blocks was established in East Asia centuries before anything comparable existed in Europe, and the oldest surviving book printed with movable metal type is Korean, not German: the Jikji, printed at the Heungdeok-sa temple in Cheongju in 1377. What happened in Mainz in the 1450s was the assembly of a production system that suited an alphabetic script and a particular European market, and its consequences were large, uneven and slower than the standard telling suggests.

## Printing before Mainz

Block printing — cutting a whole page in reverse into a wooden block, inking it and pressing paper onto it — was a working technology in Tang China. Columbia University's Asia for Educators places the Tang dynasty at 618 to 906 CE, and printed material from this period survives in quantity because of an accident of preservation: the sealed library cave at Dunhuang on the western trade routes, whose contents were walled up and stayed dry. The Asian Civilisations Museum in Singapore has built an exhibition around exactly that material, connecting Dunhuang, Buddhism and the Silk Road, and it is the clearest way to see that printing began as a religious reproduction technology rather than a commercial one. For the wider network that carried it, see our explainer on [what the Silk Road actually was](/blog/the-silk-road-for-beginners/).

Movable type followed, and it was tried in several forms and materials across East Asia before it was tried in Europe. The Jikji is the decisive surviving artefact. The Bibliothèque nationale de France, which holds the volume, describes it as the oldest known book in the world printed with movable metal type, compiled by the monk Baegun Hwasang and his disciples and printed in 1377 — nearly eight decades before the Gutenberg Bible. It was inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2001; that register, running since 1995, now carries several hundred inscriptions of documentary heritage.

## What was actually new in Mainz

Saying Gutenberg did not invent printing is not the same as saying he did nothing. The system produced in Mainz solved several problems at once: a hand mould that could cast large numbers of identical, reusable metal sorts quickly; a metal alloy that cast cleanly and stood up to repeated impressions; an oil-based ink that would adhere to metal type rather than beading off it; and a press adapted from existing screw presses to apply even pressure across a forme.

The critical variable was the script. An alphabet needs a few hundred sorts to set any text in the language; a writing system with thousands of distinct characters needs a correspondingly larger and more expensive font before a single page can be set. That is an engineering and capital argument, not a claim about the sophistication of any culture, and it goes a long way to explaining why movable type became the dominant European method while block printing remained efficient for many East Asian purposes.

The output is well documented. The Harry Ransom Center at the University of Texas at Austin, which holds one of the copies, records that the Gutenberg Bible was published between 1454 and 1456 in Mainz by Johann Gutenberg and Johann Fust, and that it is one of only twenty complete copies surviving intact.

## The Reformation, and the limits of "the press caused it"

The most repeated claim about printing is that it caused the Reformation. It is better described as an amplifier that changed the tempo of a conflict whose causes lay elsewhere.

Reform arguments had circulated in manuscript for generations. What changed after 1517 was the economics of controversy: short vernacular pamphlets were cheap to set, fast to produce and highly saleable, and competing print shops reprinted successful titles without waiting for permission. A dispute that would once have moved at the speed of correspondence between universities now moved at the speed of a trade in small books, and it reached people who had never read a theological argument before.

The causation runs both ways, which is why the simple version misleads. Printers needed something people would buy, and religious controversy sold. Historians of the book have spent decades arguing about how much of the Reformation's spread is attributable to the medium and how much to the message, the politics and the pre-existing networks, and the honest answer is that the weighting is still contested.

## Where printing did not take hold the same way

Printing arrived everywhere eventually, but the timetable varied enormously, and the older explanations for that variation have not held up well.

In the Ottoman lands and across much of the Islamic world, printing in Arabic script began on a substantial scale considerably later than in Europe, and the story is often told as a straightforward prohibition. Recent scholarship has complicated it: non-Muslim communities in Ottoman cities were printing in Hebrew, Armenian and Greek much earlier, and the manuscript economy itself was large, skilled and commercially organised, which weakened the business case for a press rather than making one impossible. In South Asia, presses were operating from the sixteenth century, but large-scale vernacular printing came later, again alongside a substantial and functioning scribal sector.

The useful generalisation is economic. A press pays when a market wants many identical copies of the same text and no cheaper supply exists. Where copyists were numerous and competent and demand was specialised, the calculation changed.

## Reading early print as evidence

Early printed books are primary sources in their own right, and they reward the same scrutiny as any other document: who paid for it, who was meant to read it, what it was arguing against, and what survives of the run. A pamphlet's print run, its typeface, its errors and its later reprints all carry information about circulation that the text alone does not. Our guide to [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) sets out that distinction in general terms, and the history of the book is one of the areas where it pays off most obviously.
