You are 85 yards out. Your sand wedge goes 75 with a full swing and your gap wedge goes 100. There is no club in the bag for the shot in front of you, so you either decelerate a full swing or you take something too much and hope.

That is a gapping problem, and it has nothing to do with how far you hit a driver. Gapping is about the **spacing** of your distances, not their maximum, and it is the most common set-level fault in amateur golf.

## Why the set has the shape it has

A standard set contains 14 clubs, and irons are by far the most common type within it: a set will usually contain between 7 and 11 irons, including wedges. Irons are numbered from 1 to 10 — most commonly 3 to 9 — with the number indicating the relative loft on the face, and clubs with more loft than the numbered irons are called wedges and marked with letters instead.

Two consequences follow from that layout, and both are worth internalising before you change anything.

**Most of your set is already devoted to scoring distances.** The long end of the bag is two or three clubs. The rest of it covers approach and short-game yardages, because that is where scores are made.

**Fourteen is a hard ceiling.** Adding a club always means removing one. Gapping is therefore a budget problem: every slot you spend covering one distance is a slot not covering another.

## Measuring: carry, not total

The number you need is **carry** — how far the ball flies before it lands — rather than total distance, which includes however far it rolled on whatever the ground was doing that day.

Carry is what clears a bunker. Carry is what holds a green. Total distance is useful information about a specific course on a specific afternoon and close to useless as a planning number.

A workable method:

1. **Hit enough shots per club to see a pattern.** Ten or so. Fewer than that and you are measuring noise.
2. **Throw out the mishits and the one perfect one.** You are looking for the distance you produce on an ordinary swing, not your best ever.
3. **Take the typical carry of the remainder**, not the average of everything.
4. **Write it down in a fixed order** and read the gaps between neighbouring clubs.

The step people skip is the third one. A distance chart built on your best strike with each club describes a golfer who does not exist, and it will have you short of every green on the course.

## Where the blind spots actually are

Two places, almost always.

**Between the longest iron and the fairway woods.** This is the classic gap, and it is where the hybrid category came from. A 3-iron and a 5-wood can easily differ by more than the gap between any two irons in the set, and for many amateurs the long iron is also the least reliable club in the bag — so the gap is both wide and unreliable.

**Inside full wedge range.** The more common one, and the more expensive. Under full-swing distance, every yardage has to be produced by a partial swing, and the shorter the distance the more the outcome depends on technique rather than club choice. Golfers routinely carry three wedges with even spacing at full swing and still have nothing for 85 yards, because 85 falls between two of them.

## Fixing a gap without breaking something else

Four options, in rough order of how cheaply they solve the problem:

**Rearrange what you already have.** Removing a club you use twice a season frees a slot. The 3-iron you cannot hit is a slot, not a club.

**Change loft.** A wedge of intermediate loft, or a small loft adjustment to an existing club, can move a distance a few yards. This is the least dramatic intervention and often the right one.

**Substitute a category.** Replacing a long iron with a hybrid of comparable loft usually produces a similar distance with a higher, softer flight — which changes not just where the ball stops but whether it will hold a green.

**Learn a deliberate partial shot.** Not a decelerated full swing — a *repeatable* three-quarter swing with a specific club that you have measured. This is a real solution and the only one that costs nothing, but it has to be practised, because a partial swing you have never rehearsed is simply a bad full swing.

What does not work, reliably, is trying to hit the shorter club harder. Swinging harder changes strike quality before it changes distance, which is how an 85-yard shot becomes a 60-yard one.

## Gapping without a swing model

[Golf Career Simulator Pro Tour](/apps/golf-career-simulator/) has no swing to model. There is no timing meter, no club-fitting screen and no equipment inventory. Each shot is a decision about how to play a hole from this lie, at this distance, in this wind, with this golfer.

So how does gapping exist in a game with no clubs?

It exists as a **distance profile**. Your golfer's driving, accuracy and iron skills combine into a description of which distances they can cover comfortably, which they can cover with an aggressive line, and which they cannot cover well at all. That profile is not shown to you as a bag of fourteen numbers. It surfaces where it actually matters: in the options you are offered on a given shot.

Faced with a shot at an awkward distance, a golfer whose profile covers it will be offered an attacking line as a reasonable option. A golfer whose profile does not will find the attacking line priced as a gamble, and the sensible options will be about position rather than the pin.

**This was a deliberate choice and it has a cost.** We considered a club-and-loadout system, and rejected it because it would have turned a decision game into an equipment game — the most interesting choice would have become which fourteen clubs to carry, made once, rather than how to play this hole, made forty times a round. Equipment optimisation is a genuinely enjoyable genre. It is not this game.

What is lost is the specific satisfaction of solving your own gapping problem. You cannot add a wedge to fix an 85-yard hole in your bag, because you do not have a bag. What you can do is train the skills that widen the profile, which is slower and more abstract than buying a club.
