**A batter can run on a third strike the catcher fails to catch cleanly only when first base is empty, or when there are two out.** With a runner on first and fewer than two out, the batter is out whether or not the ball is held. Those are Rules 5.05(a)(2) and 5.09(a)(3) of the [2026 Official Baseball Rules](https://mktg.mlbstatic.com/mlb/official-information/2026-official-baseball-rules.pdf), the edition in force for the 2026 MLB season, and every variant of the question follows from those two facts.

## The whole rule in one table

| Outs | First base empty | First base occupied |
| --- | --- | --- |
| None or one | Batter may run | Batter is out |
| Two | Batter may run | Batter may run |

Note what the table does not ask about: second and third base. Only first base matters, because the batter's run can force other runners to move only when first base is occupied. The situations people actually search for sort themselves out quickly:

| Situation | Can the batter run? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bases empty, any number of outs | Yes | First base is empty |
| Runner on second only, one out | Yes | First base is still empty |
| Runners on second and third, nobody out | Yes | First base is still empty |
| Runner on first, nobody or one out | No, he is out | First occupied with fewer than two out |
| Bases loaded, nobody or one out | No, he is out | Same reason |
| Runner on first, two out | Yes | Two out |
| Bases loaded, two out | Yes, and every runner is now forced | Two out |

[MLB's glossary](https://www.mlb.com/glossary/standard-stats/strikeout) puts the same thing in one line: the batter may try for first if the base is open or if there are two outs.

## Why first base and two outs decide it

The exception is easier to remember once you see what it blocks. With a runner on first and fewer than two out, a catcher who could choose whether to hold strike three would sometimes prefer to drop it. The batter would have to run, the runner on first would be forced, and the defence could start a force play at second that a clean catch never offers. Rule 5.09(a)(3) removes the choice by declaring the batter out.

With two out there is nothing to exploit, because one out of any kind ends the inning. With first base empty there is no force to manufacture. In both cases the rule lets the batter run, and the defence simply has to finish the play it failed to finish with the glove.

The same logic sits behind the infield fly rule, which stops a fielder dropping an easy pop-up to turn a double play. Baseball repeatedly refuses to let the defence earn extra outs by failing to catch the ball.

## What counts as "not caught"

The rule is not only about balls that roll to the backstop. The comment on Rule 5.09(a)(2) defines "legally caught" as in the catcher's glove "before the ball touches the ground", and adds that it is not a legal catch if the ball lodges in his clothing or equipment, or if it bounces off the umpire before the catcher grabs it.

That produces some situations that surprise newcomers:

- **A strike-three pitch in the dirt that the catcher blocks and picks up is not caught.** The batter can run if the table allows it, and the catcher has to tag him or throw to first.
- **A swing at a bouncing pitch.** The 2026 edition, in Rule 5.05(a)(3), says that if a batter swings at a pitch that has touched the ground after two strikes, the ball "cannot be caught" for the purposes of this rule.
- **A foul tip is different.** A foul tip held by the catcher on strike three is a legal catch, and the batter is out.
- **A third strike that deflects off the umpire** stays in play. If a fielder catches the rebound before it lands, the batter is not out on that catch, but he can still be thrown out at first or tagged (comment on Rule 5.06(c)(7)).
- **A third strike that sticks in the umpire's or catcher's mask** and stays out of play entitles the batter to first base, and every runner moves up one base (same comment).

## How the batter is put out

Once the batter becomes a runner, Rule 5.09(a)(10) makes him out if he, or first base, is tagged before he reaches it. Two further rules catch batters who do not handle the moment well:

- **Not running.** The comment on Rule 5.05(a)(2) says a batter who does not realise his situation, and is not running to first, is declared out once he leaves the dirt circle around home plate.
- **Getting in the way.** Under Rule 6.01(a)(1), a batter-runner who clearly hinders the catcher's attempt to field the ball is out for interference, the ball is dead and the other runners return to their bases.

With two out and the bases loaded, the catcher has the easiest out on the field. Every runner was forced the moment the batter became a runner, so stepping on home plate with the ball before the runner from third arrives ends the inning.

Little League's rules FAQ adds a point for the opposite case. When first base is occupied with fewer than two out, the batter is already out, and if he runs anyway the catcher's throw to first is not needed. [Its guidance](https://www.littleleague.org/help-center/rule-6-00-the-batter-faqs/) is that the run is not interference, the defence is responsible for knowing the batter is out, and the plate umpire should announce it clearly.

## How it is scored

This is where the rule leaves its mark on statistics. Rule 9.15(a) tells the official scorer to record a strikeout whenever a batter is put out on a caught third strike, is put out on an uncaught one with first occupied and fewer than two out, or "becomes a runner because a third strike is not caught". The batter is charged with a strikeout even if he ends up standing on first.

The scorer then has to explain how he got there:

| What happened | How it is scored |
| --- | --- |
| The pitch was too wild for the catcher to control with ordinary effort | Strikeout and wild pitch, charged to the pitcher (Rule 9.13(a)) |
| The catcher should have held the pitch | Strikeout and passed ball, charged to the catcher (Rule 9.13(b)) |
| The catcher recovers and throws the batter out, but another runner advances | Strikeout, putout and assists, with the other runner's advance scored as a fielder's choice (Rule 9.12(f)(2)) |

The distinction matters for earned runs. Rule 9.16 counts a wild pitch on strike three among the things that can make a run earned, while passed balls are removed when the scorer reconstructs the inning. [MLB's passed ball entry](https://www.mlb.com/glossary/standard-stats/passed-ball) puts it plainly: a batter who reaches on a wild pitch could count against the pitcher's ERA, and one who reaches on a passed ball cannot. A pitcher gets an assist on the play only if he fields the loose ball himself and makes the throw that retires the batter.

### Four strikeouts in one inning

Because a strikeout is recorded even when the batter reaches base, an inning can contain more strikeouts than outs. A hypothetical half-inning:

1. The first batter strikes out on a caught third strike. One out, one strikeout.
2. The second batter does the same. Two out, two strikeouts.
3. The third batter swings through a pitch in the dirt. With two out he may run, and he beats the throw to first. Still two out, but three strikeouts and a wild pitch.
4. The fourth batter strikes out on a caught third strike. Three out, four strikeouts.

That is why a pitching line can show more strikeouts in an inning than there are outs to go round — a footnote worth adding to the counting statistics in [baseball stats explained for beginners](/blog/baseball-stats-explained-for-beginners/).
