"Built by" is a single phrase covering four very different evidential situations. Sometimes a building carries a foundation inscription naming its patron and year. Sometimes a contemporary writer names the designers. Sometimes a name arrives through a documentary tradition written down much later. And sometimes there is no name at all, only inference from style. Knowing which case you are in is the whole skill, and it is the same evidence ladder set out in [how we know what historical figures actually did](/blog/how-we-know-what-historical-figures-did/), applied to buildings instead of biographies.

## The ladder, briefly

From strongest to weakest: an inscription or document made at the time of construction and physically attached to the project; an account by a contemporary who was not involved in it; a tradition recorded generations later; and attribution on stylistic grounds alone. Each step down widens the gap between what is recorded and what happened.

One more distinction matters throughout. Patron, designer and workforce are three separate questions, and the evidence for them is usually of three different qualities. Societies that meticulously recorded the first often recorded nothing about the third.

## Giza: a workforce we can see, a designer we cannot

The Giza pyramids are the case where the popular story is furthest from the evidence. Since 1988, Ancient Egypt Research Associates has excavated Heit el-Ghurab, a settlement about 400 metres south of the Sphinx, and found an urban site sprawling over more than seven hectares. It contains craft workshops, industrial yards, bakeries, commissaries, kitchens, warehouses, small houses and larger residences for site administrators. Royal silos stood in a compound from which grain was distributed to make the bread and beer that fed the workers, and graffiti and gang marks record labour organised into named groups working on the pyramids of Khufu, Khafre and Menkaure.

That is the material signature of an administered, provisioned, state-organised workforce. It is not the signature of the mass chattel slavery of popular imagination, and that image comes from much later retelling rather than from Egyptian evidence. The records themselves — excavation archives, plans, photographs and object records — are being assembled by Harvard's Giza Project, which describes itself as an international collaboration bringing together the archaeological record of the plateau.

What no evidence gives us is a designer. We know an enormous amount about how the labour was fed and organised, and essentially nothing about who drew the thing.

## Hagia Sophia: two names, one interested witness

Hagia Sophia is the opposite case. UNESCO's description of the Historic Areas of Istanbul places it among the Justinianic churches and notes that its vast dome "reflects the architectural and decorative expertise of the 6th century". The names of its builders, though, come from a single literary source.

Writing in the sixth century, Procopius names "Anthemius of Tralles, the most learned man in the skilled craft which is known as the art of building" and "Isidorus by name, a Milesian by birth". He describes Anthemius regulating the tasks of the various artisans and preparing designs in advance.

Read that source the way you would read any other. Procopius' *Buildings* is written for the emperor, and the same passage credits Justinian with the wisdom to select the right men and with interventions when the dome ran into difficulty. None of that makes the names wrong. It does mean the attribution rests on a contemporary account with an obvious interest, not on a contract, and that the emperor's share of the credit is the least checkable part of it. That is exactly the kind of judgement [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) is for.

## Sinan: the best-documented pre-modern architect

For the Ottoman architect Sinan the evidence is unusually good, because he held an institutional office and his buildings are recorded as a body of work. UNESCO's inscription of the Selimiye Mosque and its social complex at Edirne, commissioned by Selim II, describes the complex as "the culmination of the great body of work by Sinan, the most outstanding architect of the Ottoman Empire", and records that Sinan himself regarded it as his most important architectural work. The building supports this claim structurally: a single great dome 31.5 metres in diameter carried on eight pillars over a prayer space of roughly 45 by 36 metres, with an innovative structural design that allowed many windows.

UNESCO's separate description of Istanbul names the Süleymaniye and Şehzade complexes as works of "the chief architect Sinan". An architect with a documented office, a corpus and a personal assessment of his own work is a rarity before the modern period, which is why Sinan turns up as the standard example.

## The Taj Mahal: firm patron, softer architect

For the Taj Mahal the patron and chronology are secure and the design attribution is looser. UNESCO records that it was built by Shah Jahan in memory of Mumtaz Mahal, with construction starting in 1632 and completed in 1648, and with the mosque, guest house, main gateway, outer courtyard and cloisters added and finished by 1653. It notes that several historical and Quranic inscriptions in Arabic script "facilitated setting the chronology" — the building dates itself.

On authorship UNESCO states that "Ustad-Ahmad Lahori was the main architect of the Taj Mahal". Note what kind of claim that is: a name transmitted through documentary tradition, not an inscription carved into the monument alongside the dates. And note what UNESCO says immediately before it — that masons, stone-cutters, inlayers, carvers, painters, calligraphers, dome builders and other artisans "were requisitioned from the whole of the empire and also from the Central Asia and Iran". A single name is a convenience for a project on that scale.

## Angkor: attribution written into the stone

At Angkor, attribution runs through epigraphy. The Corpus des inscriptions khmères, a project of the École française d'Extrême-Orient with the École pratique des hautes études, describes Khmer inscriptions as "véritables archives de pierre" — genuine stone archives — and as the primary written sources for reconstructing Cambodian history across the pre-Angkorian and Angkorian periods, from roughly the fifth to the fourteenth centuries.

UNESCO records Angkor as a complex of scores of temples and hydraulic works representing the full range of Khmer art from the ninth to the fourteenth centuries, naming Angkor Wat, the Bayon, Preah Khan, Ta Prohm and Banteay Srei among them. Where a specific temple is attributed to a specific king, that attribution normally traces back to a foundation inscription read by an epigrapher — which is a strong form of evidence, and one with its own difficulties of language, damage and formulaic praise. The related question of how such inscriptions anchor a chronology is covered in [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/).

## What to do with a "Built By" label

Monument databases, guidebooks and apps all carry a "built by" field, and the field cannot show you which rung of the ladder its answer came from. Our own app is no exception, and we should say so: World History Timeline Sim is built by Reign Creative, and its 168 landmark records include "Built By" and "Architectural Style" sections alongside history and preservation notes.

Use a field like that as an index, not as an answer. The app's content carries no citations and has had no external review, so a name in it is a prompt to go and find the inscription, the excavation report or the museum description that supports it. And no app replaces a teacher, a scholarly book, an archive or a primary source — the app's own disclaimer screen says as much.

A short checklist covers most cases. Ask whether the name comes from the building, from a contemporary text, from a later tradition or from style alone. Ask whether the claim is about the patron, the designer or the workforce. Ask who benefited from the credit being recorded that way. And where several thousand people worked for two decades, be suspicious of any account with only one name in it.
