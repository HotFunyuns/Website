**In this category a paywall almost never buys you a different kind of exercise. It buys access to more of the same catalogue, sessions chosen for you rather than by you, more detailed reporting, and the removal of a daily cap.** Knowing that in advance turns "is it worth it" into a question you can answer, because you can check whether you would actually use those four things.

Two disclosures before anything else. Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, published by Reign Creative, so we are not a neutral party in a discussion about paywalls. And there are no prices anywhere in this article — not ours and not anyone else's — for the reason given below.

## What was checked, and what was deliberately left out

Everything stated here about another company's product comes from that company's own official pages, read on 21 September 2026. Reviews were not used as evidence of what a product contains, and nothing is inferred from what a page does not mention.

Prices are excluded on purpose. They differ by region, by platform, by whether you buy monthly or annually, and by whatever promotion is running the week you look. A number published here would be wrong for a meaningful share of readers on the day they read it, and a wrong number is worse than none. The place to find the current figure is the publisher's own plan page or the store listing on your device.

For what each product actually contains, rather than what it charges for, see [brain training apps compared](/blog/brain-training-apps-compared/).

## The four things a paywall usually gates

### Catalogue access

This is the most common gate and the one that most affects whether a free tier is usable.

Lumosity's own brain games page describes the situation in general terms: "Most apps — including Lumosity — have free versions with a handful of games you can try out," with more available by subscribing. Peak's site puts full access explicitly in its paid tier, listing "Access to the complete catalogue of more than 40 games and activities" among what Peak Pro includes.

The practical question for you is whether the sample rotates. A free tier that shows you three different games each day gives you variety without depth; one that gives you the same three forever gives you neither.

### Session selection and personalisation

The second gate is who decides what you play.

BrainHQ's FAQ describes its free version as offering "the Daily Spark exercise to train with until you earn a total of 5 stars each day", with the full range of exercises and benefits behind a subscription. Its personalised training is described as a feature that "continually assesses your progress and serves up the exercises where you can improve your performance the most". Peak lists "Dozens of tailored workouts" under Pro.

Whether that is worth paying for depends on something only you know: whether left to yourself you would practise your weak areas or your favourite ones. Automated selection is genuinely valuable for people who would otherwise replay what they are already good at. It is worth nothing to someone who has decided to drill division for a month.

### Reports and analysis

The third gate is the depth of the record. Peak lists "Insightful analysis of your performance" under Pro. Lumosity's site describes interpreting your scores "to offer actionable feedback and rich insights into your cognition". BrainHQ's site describes tracking progress over time with the option to drill down into performance reporting on individual exercise levels.

Before paying for reporting, be honest about whether you will read it. A weekly report you glance at for four seconds is an expensive habit. If you do want to read your own numbers properly, [how to track practice progress without fooling yourself](/blog/how-to-track-practice-progress/) covers which metrics repay attention and which flatter you, and most of it can be done with whatever figures a free tier already shows.

### Volume caps

The fourth gate is how much you can play. A daily-session cap, like BrainHQ's five-stars-a-day free structure, limits quantity rather than content. This is the least painful form of gate for most learners, because the evidence on practice dose does not favour long sessions anyway — five to fifteen minutes covers most people, as [how long a brain training session should be](/blog/how-long-a-brain-training-session-should-be/) sets out. A cap that stops you after ten minutes is only a problem if you were going to do more than ten minutes.

## Where our app sits

Mental Math & Memory Games is free, contains ads and offers in-app purchases. The important structural point is that gameplay is not the paywall: every arithmetic mode, every memory mode and the whole technique library are playable, and replaying anything is always free.

We are not publishing our own price here for the same reason we are not publishing anyone else's, and you should check the current figure on the store listing rather than in an article.

That structure is a choice with visible trade-offs, and it is worth naming them rather than presenting it as obviously superior. An ad-supported free tier means interruptions that a subscription-funded product does not have. And a product with no algorithmic session selection puts the work of choosing what to practise back on you — which is exactly the thing some readers would happily pay to avoid.

## How to check before you subscribe

A short routine that works across the category:

1. **Read the publisher's own plan page.** Look for a specific list of what the free tier includes, not a general promise of "more".
2. **Work out which of the four gates applies.** A content gate makes a free tier a demo. A volume gate often leaves it genuinely usable.
3. **Check the cancellation route before you commit, not after.** The FTC's consumer advice on subscriptions recommends finding the terms and conditions for the offer, researching the company online, looking for information on how to cancel, and watching for pre-checked boxes that might authorise extra charges. It also defines a negative option plainly: you are "automatically billed for something when you didn't specifically say not to bill you", and states that "businesses must make canceling simple for you. It's the law."
4. **Know how the store handles it.** Google Play's official help page states that "when you cancel a subscription, you'll still be able to use your subscription for the time you've already paid", with cancellation preventing charges at the next renewal date. In practice that means you can cancel the moment you decide and still use what you bought.
5. **Set a reminder before any trial ends.** The FTC's advice on free trials is specifically about the deadline: the offer has a time limit, and once it passes you may be billed.

## When a subscription is genuinely worth it

There is a real case for paying, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

If you want breadth across several cognitive domains, the publishers that document that breadth put most of it behind a subscription, and there is no free route to it. If you know you will not choose your own sessions sensibly, algorithmic selection is a service with value. If you will read a weekly report and act on it, reporting depth is worth something. And if ads genuinely disrupt your concentration during a timed task, removing them is not a trivial benefit.

Equally, if what you want is arithmetic drill and you will happily pick your own mode, you do not need to pay anyone — us included — to get it.

## What a paywall never buys

Evidence. The question of whether any of this transfers beyond the exercises themselves is unresolved across the whole category, and a subscription does not resolve it.

The clearest external marker remains the US Federal Trade Commission's January 2016 action, in which Lumos Labs [paid $2 million to settle charges](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program) over advertising that claimed training would improve performance on everyday tasks and delay age-related cognitive decline. The Commission's summary was that the company "simply did not have the science to back up its ads". That history applies to the category, not to one company, and it applies to us as well: we make no cognitive claim at all, which is a position rather than an achievement.
