Mongol armies moved faster and further than any contemporary state could match, and the usual explanation — mounted archers — is the least interesting part of the answer. Steppe cavalry had existed for a thousand years without producing an empire on that scale. What was new was the organisation around the cavalry: remount systems, dispersed marching, a relay communications network, the systematic absorption of other people's engineers and administrators, and a command structure that could coordinate columns hundreds of miles apart. The same structure contained the weakness that repeatedly stopped the advance, which was politics at the centre rather than resistance at the front.

## Start with the sources, not the story

Almost everything popular writing says about the Mongols comes through three chronicle traditions — Persian, Arabic and Chinese — plus a small number of European eyewitness reports. Knowing which is which changes how much weight a claim can carry.

The two earliest direct European accounts are both Franciscan. John of Plano Carpini travelled as a papal envoy between 1245 and 1247 and produced the first direct European description of the empire; his report survives in translation and describes Mongol administration, military organisation and territorial conquests, including regional commanders with very large forces under them. William of Rubruck, a Flemish Franciscan sent at the instigation of Louis IX of France, travelled from 1253 to 1255 and became the first European to visit and describe Karakorum.

What is striking about both is how administrative they are. Rubruck describes official messengers and translators, correspondence rendered into several languages, and pasture allocated by captains who knew the seasonal limits of their grazing. This is a report about a state, not about a horde. Reading a contemporary account before a modern summary is a habit worth building, and our guide to [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) sets out why the order matters.

Standard chronologies place the unification of the Mongol confederation under Chinggis Khan in 1206 and his death in 1227. Those dates come from later chronicle traditions rather than from contemporary documentary records, which is worth knowing before treating them as fixed points on a line.

## Logistics first: what actually produced the speed

Four things compound.

**Remounts.** A force in which each rider has several horses can sustain a pace that a single-mount army cannot, and it can do so without the grain supply that fodder-fed cavalry requires. That converts a tactical advantage into an operational one.

**Dispersed movement.** Columns that travel separately and converge for battle spread the forage burden across a wide front. The limit on a horse army is grass, not roads, and dispersal is how you stretch that limit.

**Relay communication.** Rubruck's account of official messengers points at the system usually called the *yam*: staged posts allowing despatches to move faster than any individual traveller. Coordination is the thing that separates a raid from a campaign, and this is the mechanism that supplied it.

**Absorption.** The empire recruited or conscripted the specialists of every society it defeated — engineers, siege technicians, scribes, artisans, translators. The Mongols did not arrive with the ability to reduce a walled city. They acquired it, and then applied it to the next walled city.

That last point is the one most often missed, and it reframes the whole question. The conquests were not fast because of one weapon system. They were fast because each conquest supplied capabilities used in the next one, which is closer to a compounding process than to a series of battles. The Eurasian networks that carried those capabilities are the same ones described in our introduction to [the Silk Road](/blog/the-silk-road-for-beginners/), and the exchange ran in both directions.

## The massacres, and the numbers attached to them

Mongol campaigns included the mass killing of civilian populations after the fall of cities. That is documented across independent chronicle traditions and it is not seriously disputed. It should be stated plainly rather than softened into "destruction" or "pacification": people who had surrendered or been captured were killed in large numbers, and the practice was deliberate, intended partly to make later resistance less likely.

The figures are a separate question. Medieval chroniclers routinely give totals for individual cities that exceed any plausible estimate of those cities' populations, and modern scholarship — the kind published in peer-reviewed venues such as the *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society* — treats those numbers as rhetorical statements of scale rather than as counts. Two things are therefore true at once: the massacres happened, and the arithmetic in the sources is unusable. Handling that combination honestly means reporting what is documented, attributing the figures to whoever produced them, and refusing to launder a chronicle's round number into a modern statistic. The same discipline applies wherever a dramatic figure has been repeated for long enough to feel like data — it is what makes the argument over [what caused the Bronze Age collapse](/blog/what-caused-the-bronze-age-collapse/) worth reading, since a majority of the destruction counts usually quoted for that event do not survive re-examination.

It is also worth refusing the opposite move. "Barbarian horde" framing was a device of the societies the Mongols attacked and of nineteenth-century writers who inherited it, and it obscures the administrative, commercial and legal history that specialists in the period actually work on — much of it published in journals such as the *Journal of Chinese History* for the Yuan period.

## Why the advance kept stopping

A campaign that ends is usually assumed to have been defeated. Mongol campaigns frequently ended because a khan died.

The succession system required senior figures to return for the assembly that chose the next ruler. A commander in the middle of a successful advance was pulled back by an event two thousand miles behind him. Add terrain and climate — the grass and water requirements of a large horse army set hard limits in forested, mountainous or humid country — and organised resistance by states that had learned from earlier defeats, and you have a sufficient explanation for the stopping points without inventing a decisive battle for each one.

This is also why the empire has no clean end date. It fragmented into successor khanates, and each ended at a different time in a different way. Ask "when did the Mongol conquests end" and the honest answer is "which one, and by what criterion".

## What to take away

**Organisation beats cavalry as an explanation.** Remounts, dispersal, relay communication and the absorption of enemy specialists account for the speed; the bow accounts for very little of it on its own.

**Document the atrocity, distrust the arithmetic.** Both halves are required. Dropping either one produces a false account.

**The empire has no single end date**, and any source that gives you one without saying which polity it means is simplifying something you should be able to see.
