They share a script and they are not the same language. A Hindi reader opening a Marathi newspaper can pronounce every word on the page and will follow perhaps the gist of a headline. Put the same person in a Marathi conversation at normal speed and the gist evaporates.

That gap — high on the page, low in the ear — is the single most useful thing to understand before you start, because it predicts exactly which parts of your Hindi will help and which will quietly mislead you.

## What is genuinely shared

**The script.** Both use Devanagari. Marathi uses the Balbodh variant, which adds a few letters and takes Western punctuation, but a Hindi reader is decoding Marathi within minutes rather than weeks. This is not a small gift; for most learners the script is the largest fixed cost of an Indian language, and you have already paid it.

**A large slice of the vocabulary.** Both draw heavily on Sanskrit for formal and technical registers. Both absorbed a great deal of Persian during the sultanate period, and Marathi still uses that layer in ordinary speech — *bāg*, *shahar*, *bāzār*, *dukān*, *kāgad*, *hazār* — all of which will look immediately familiar to a Hindi speaker.

**Word order.** Both are subject–object–verb, and both are postpositional rather than prepositional.

## Where they part company

### Three genders against two

This is the difference with the widest blast radius. Hindi has masculine and feminine. Marathi kept the Sanskrit neuter as well, which makes it unusual among modern Indo-Aryan languages.

Gender in Marathi is not a label sitting quietly on a noun. Declinable adjectives — the ones ending in -आ — inflect for it. Verbs agree with it. Possessives agree with it: चा, ची and चे are the masculine, feminine and plural forms of the same marker. So a Hindi speaker who has internalised a two-way system is not learning a new label; they are learning a third slot that everything downstream has to agree with.

### Two words for "we"

Marathi distinguishes an inclusive *we* (आपण, *āpaṇ* — you and I) from an exclusive one (आम्ही, *āmhī* — us but not you). Hindi does not make this distinction, and neither does English, so there is no translation to lean on; there is only the habit of deciding, every time, whether your listener is inside the group you are describing.

It is a feature Marathi shares with Dravidian and Austroasiatic languages rather than with its Indo-Aryan relatives, and it is the clearest signal that Marathi has been sitting at a linguistic crossroads for a very long time.

### Verb agreement does not behave the same way

Marathi follows a split-ergative pattern: it is ergative with perfective transitive verbs and with obligative constructions, and nominative elsewhere. Hindi also has ergativity in the perfective, so the concept is not new to a Hindi speaker — but the distribution is not identical, and Marathi additionally allows a construction where the verb agrees with neither the subject nor the object.

In practice this means the instinct "the verb matches the subject" is right often enough in Hindi to be a habit and wrong often enough in Marathi to be a problem. [Marathi verbs for beginners](/blog/marathi-verb-basics/) works through the agreement patterns with paired examples.

### The schwa is pronounced

Hindi drops final schwas in speech; Marathi frequently does not. रंग is *rang* in Hindi and *ranga* in Marathi, and खरं keeps its final vowel — the anusvara there is doing the specific job of blocking the deletion.

A Hindi speaker reading Marathi aloud will systematically clip word endings until somebody points this out. It is a small thing that makes a large difference to how you sound.

### Sounds Hindi does not have

Marathi contrasts an alveolar lateral ल with a retroflex ळ, and contrasts apico-alveolar affricates with alveopalatal ones, so च and ज each cover two pronunciations. Neither contrast exists in standard Hindi, and neither is recoverable from the spelling.

## The comparison in one table

| | Marathi | Hindi |
|---|---|---|
| Script | Devanagari (Balbodh variant, extra letters, Western punctuation) | Devanagari |
| Grammatical genders | Masculine, feminine, neuter | Masculine, feminine |
| "We" | Inclusive आपण vs exclusive आम्ही | One form |
| Word order | Subject–object–verb | Subject–object–verb |
| Schwa at word end | Often retained in speech | Usually deleted |
| Lateral contrast | ल vs retroflex ळ | ल only |
| Official status | Official in Maharashtra, additional official in Goa | Official language of the Union, official in several states |

## What we changed in the course because of all this

A meaningful share of people starting a Marathi course already have some Hindi, whether from school, from film and television, or from a parent. That shaped two decisions in [Learn Marathi Language Fast](/apps/learn-marathi/).

The first is the split alphabet unit described in [the Marathi alphabet guide](/blog/marathi-alphabet-and-devanagari/): shared letters first, Marathi's additional letters as their own lesson, so a reader with Hindi cannot skim past precisely the characters they do not know.

The second is about vocabulary selection, and it is the more consequential one. When two Marathi words were both usable for a concept, we did not automatically pick the one closest to the Hindi. In some places we deliberately introduced the less Hindi-like item first.

That is a genuine cost to the learner who has Hindi — it removes a free win — and it was not an easy call. We made it because the alternative produces a learner who can compose Marathi-shaped sentences out of Hindi vocabulary, is understood by Marathi speakers out of politeness, and never finds out that they are doing it. Being handed a familiar word early feels like progress and quietly delays the moment you start hearing Marathi as Marathi.

Where prior Hindi genuinely does help, the lessons say so explicitly rather than pretending the languages are unrelated.
