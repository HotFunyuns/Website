Multiply the ingredients. Do not multiply the time.

That is the short version, and it covers most of the damage people do when scaling a recipe. Ingredient quantities are linear — twice the people, twice the rice. Cooking time is not linear, because heat arrives through a surface rather than being distributed evenly through a mass, and seasoning is not linear either, because it is perceived relative to volume rather than added to it.

Here is what actually changes, and what stays put.

## What scales cleanly

**Ingredient quantities**, almost all of them. Flour, rice, vegetables, stock, protein: multiply and move on.

**The batch total for nutrition.** If a recipe's figures are calculated from its ingredients, doubling the ingredients doubles the total.

**Cost.** Rarely linear in practice, because of pack sizes, but the underlying maths is.

That is a short list, and it is the entire set of things you can multiply without thinking.

## What does not

**Cooking time.** This is the big one. Heat reaches food through its surface, so the governing variable is depth and exposed area, not total mass. Twice the quantity spread across twice the pan area cooks in roughly the original time. Twice the quantity in the original pan is twice as deep and takes longer — but nowhere near twice as long, and the outside will overcook while you wait.

The practical rule: **scale the pan, not the clock.** Then cook to the recipe's indicator — internal temperature, colour, texture, a skewer — rather than to a number you multiplied.

**Seasoning.** Salt and strong spices are perceived relative to the volume of the dish and interact with each other. Doubling them often produces something noticeably saltier rather than identically seasoned. Scaling up, go to roughly 1.5× on the assertive seasonings and adjust at the end. You can add; you cannot subtract.

**Raising agents.** These respond to the depth and shape of the batter as well as its quantity. A doubled cake batter in one deep tin is a different problem from the same batter in two tins, and the second is almost always the right answer.

**Reduction and evaporation.** A sauce reduces from its surface. Double the volume in the same pan and it takes much longer to reduce; double the pan and it may reduce faster than you expect.

**Equipment capacity.** The unglamorous constraint that stops more scaled recipes than any of the above. A pan that is too full steams instead of browning, and no amount of extra time fixes that.

## Halving is harder than doubling

Scaling down runs into indivisible ingredients, and eggs are the usual culprit.

The method is straightforward and people avoid it because it feels fussy: **beat the egg, then weigh or measure half.** A large egg is roughly 50 grams out of the shell, so about 25 grams is a half. The same approach works for anything that arrives in units — a tin, a sachet, a clove of something large. Combine first, measure the fraction second.

The other half-recipe trap is tin size. Halving the batter and keeping the tin gives you a thin, fast-cooking, dry result. Halve the volume, halve the area.

## Where the nutrition figures actually move

This is the part that generates the most confusion, and the answer is simpler than it looks.

**Per serving, nothing changes** — provided the number of servings scales along with the ingredients. Double the ingredients and double the stated yield, and each serving is identical to before.

**Per batch, everything scales.** Obviously.

The confusion comes from recipes that were scaled while the stated yield stayed where it was. Then the per-serving figures really did change, because each serving is now bigger, and the change is arithmetic rather than culinary.

It is worth separating two things that use the same word. The **serving size on a packaged-food label** is standardised: shown as a common household measure appropriate to the food — a cup, tablespoon, piece, slice or jar — followed by the metric amount in grams, and designed so that similar products can be compared. The FDA is explicit that this figure is **not a recommendation of how much to eat or drink**. It also points out the arithmetic directly: if a label's serving is one cup and you eat two, you are eating two servings, which means doubling the calories, the nutrients and the percentage Daily Values shown.

A **recipe serving** is a different thing entirely — a division of a batch, chosen by whoever wrote the recipe, with no standard behind it at all. Two recipes claiming to serve four can produce quite different amounts of food. The distinction between a defined serving and the amount a person actually puts on a plate is worked through in [serving size versus portion size](/blog/serving-size-vs-portion-size/).

## Why our recipes start at one serving

[Easy Recipes & Meal Planner](/apps/easy-recipes-meal-planner/) stores every recipe at a **one-person serving** and scales upward, with ingredient quantities and available nutrition adjusting to the serving size you choose.

That is an unusual baseline. Most recipe collections are written at four or six and expect you to divide.

Two reasons we went the other way.

**Scaling up is more reliable than scaling down.** Multiplying a quantity keeps it measurable — 2 becomes 4, 30 grams becomes 60. Dividing produces fractions of indivisible things and quantities below the resolution of ordinary kitchen equipment. A base of one means every scaling operation the user performs is multiplication.

**One serving is the honest unit for a lot of people.** Cooking for one is common and badly served by a collection that assumes a household of four and leaves you dividing everything by four in your head.

The cost is that a recipe written at one serving sometimes reads oddly — a quarter of an onion, a single egg used across a scaled batch — and it puts more weight on the scaling being right.

### The rounding rule

Which brings us to the least glamorous design decision in the app and one of the most useful.

Scaling a one-serving recipe by 3 produces numbers like 0.83 of a teaspoon and 47 grams. Both are arithmetically correct and neither is a thing anyone does in a kitchen.

So scaled quantities are rounded to amounts that stay **measurable with ordinary equipment**: to the nearest practical spoon fraction for small volumes, to a sensible weight increment for larger ones, and to whole units for things that come in units. The principle is that a scaled ingredient list should read like something a person wrote, not like the output of a multiplication.

The trade-off is real and worth stating: rounding introduces a small error, and it accumulates slightly across a long ingredient list. We think a recipe you can follow beats a recipe that is precise to two decimal places and unusable, particularly since domestic measuring is not that accurate to begin with. Where nutrition figures are shown, they follow the same scaling, which is one of several reasons two apps can report different numbers for what looks like the same dish — that whole question is [why recipe nutrition figures move](/blog/recipe-nutrition-figures-and-why-they-move/).

## A checklist for scaling anything

1. Multiply the ingredients.
2. Scale the **pan**, not the clock.
3. Hold back on assertive seasoning; adjust at the end.
4. Split rather than deepen for anything baked.
5. Beat and weigh indivisible ingredients when halving.
6. Round to amounts you can actually measure.
7. Scale the stated yield too, or the per-serving figures stop meaning anything.

Most scaled-recipe disappointments trace back to step two or step seven.
