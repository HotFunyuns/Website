To decide which of two fractions is larger, you almost never need a common denominator. Four strategies cover the ground, and they are worth learning in order of cost: benchmark both against one half, match the numerators, compare each fraction's distance from a nearby landmark, and only then cross-multiply. The first three are usually a glance; the fourth always works and always costs something.

The reason this matters is that the common-denominator method is the one most people were taught and the one that scales worst. Finding the lowest common multiple of 15 and 17 to compare 7/15 with 9/17 is a minute of work for a question that takes two seconds by another route.

## 1. Benchmark against one half

A fraction is greater than one half exactly when its numerator is more than half its denominator. That is a comparison, not a calculation.

**7/15 against 9/17.** Half of 15 is 7.5, and 7 is below it, so 7/15 is under a half. Half of 17 is 8.5, and 9 is above it, so 9/17 is over a half. The verdict is immediate: **9/17 is larger.** Decimal values confirm it at 0.4667 and 0.5294, but you never needed them.

**When it fails:** both fractions land on the same side. Then you have narrowed the field and learned nothing else, and you move on.

The same move works against any convenient landmark — a third, a quarter, three quarters — which is why the percent anchors are worth having in memory. [Percentages in your head](/blog/percentages-in-your-head/) covers that anchor set from the percentage side.

## 2. Make the numerators match

Everyone is taught to match denominators. Matching numerators is often easier and reads just as cleanly, with one inversion to remember: **once the numerators agree, the fraction with the larger denominator is the smaller number**, because the same quantity has been cut into more pieces.

**4/9 against 4/11.** Numerators already match. Eleven pieces are smaller than nine pieces, so 4/9 wins. In decimals, 0.4444 against 0.3636.

**3/7 against 5/12.** Scale to a shared numerator of 15: multiply the first by 5/5 to get 15/35, and the second by 3/3 to get 15/36. Thirty-five pieces beat thirty-six, so **3/7 is larger** — 0.4286 against 0.4167, a genuinely close call settled without a single awkward multiplication.

Matching numerators is often cheaper because numerators tend to be smaller numbers with more obvious common multiples.

## 3. Compare the distance from a landmark

When two fractions are both close to the same value, compare the gaps instead of the fractions. The gap is small and easy, and **the fraction with the smaller gap below a landmark is the larger fraction.**

NRICH's *Ordering Fractions* problem is built on exactly this move: rather than comparing 11/6, 5/3, 7/4, 9/5 and 3/2 directly, the published solution measures how far each one sits below 2, and orders the gaps.

**8/9 against 11/12.** The first sits 1/9 below 1, the second 1/12 below 1. A twelfth is a smaller gap than a ninth, so **11/12 is larger** — 0.9167 against 0.8889.

The method scales to a whole list. Put 5/6, 7/8, 9/10 and 11/12 in order by their gaps below 1: 1/6, 1/8, 1/10 and 1/12. The gaps shrink from left to right, so the fractions grow from left to right, and the order is as written. The decimals run 0.8333, 0.875, 0.9, 0.9167.

It does not need the numerator to be one below the denominator. The distance from 1 is always (denominator − numerator) over denominator. **5/7 against 7/10:** the gaps are 2/7, about 0.286, and 3/10, exactly 0.3. The smaller gap belongs to 5/7, so **5/7 is larger** — 0.7143 against 0.7.

## 4. Cross-multiply, as the fallback

Compare a/b with c/d by comparing a × d against c × b. Whichever product is larger belongs to the larger fraction.

**7/16 against 9/20.** Seven times twenty is 140; nine times sixteen is 144. The larger product sits with **9/20**, and the decimals agree at 0.4375 and 0.45.

Two caveats. It is only valid when both denominators are positive, which is easy to forget once negatives enter. And it returns a verdict with no sense of scale: you learn which is bigger and nothing about by how much. The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide on fractions instruction makes a related recommendation for teaching — develop conceptual understanding of strategies for ratio, rate and proportion problems before introducing cross-multiplication as a procedure — and the reasoning carries over to an adult trying to work quickly. A procedure you can execute without understanding is also a procedure you cannot sanity-check.

## The procedure, compressed

Run these in order and stop as soon as one settles it.

1. Are they on opposite sides of one half? Done.
2. Do the numerators match, or scale easily to match? Larger denominator means smaller fraction.
3. Are both near the same landmark? Compare the gaps; smaller gap below the landmark means larger fraction.
4. Otherwise cross-multiply.

## The trap: reading the two halves separately

The dominant error is treating a fraction as two numbers rather than one. Comparing 2/3 with 5/9 by observing that 5 beats 2 and 9 beats 3 produces the wrong answer, because 2/3 is 6/9 and therefore larger.

This is not only a beginner's slip. In a study of sixth and eighth graders published in *Developmental Psychology*, Siegler and Pyke reported that roughly a third of the children based their fraction magnitude estimates primarily on the numerator alone or on the denominator alone, and that the pattern went with weaker fraction arithmetic. It is a finding about how fractions get read, not a claim about any individual, and the practical lesson is that every strategy above works by forcing the two parts to be considered together.

Reducing a fraction first often makes the comparison obvious, which is why the ordering heuristic in [simplifying a fraction quickly](/blog/simplifying-fractions-quickly/) pairs naturally with this article. Converting to a familiar decimal or percent is the other escape route, and [converting between fractions, decimals and percents](/blog/fraction-decimal-percent-conversions/) sets out which conversions are worth knowing outright.
