Cricket can look impenetrable from the outside: matches that last five days, scores like 287 for 6, and a vocabulary of overs, wickets and maidens. Underneath, the idea is simple. One team bats and tries to score runs; the other bowls and fields and tries to get the batters out. Then they swap. This guide follows the Laws of Cricket, written by Marylebone Cricket Club (MCC), in their 2026 edition, which took effect on 1 October 2026.

## The teams and the innings

Law 1 says a match is played between two sides of 11 players, one of whom is the captain. Each side's turn to bat is an **innings**. Law 13 allows a match of one or two innings per side, and the sides bat in turn.

| | Batting side | Fielding side |
| --- | --- | --- |
| On the field | Two batters | All 11 players |
| Aim | Score runs without losing wickets | Take wickets and stop runs |
| Key role | The striker faces the ball; the non-striker waits at the other end | One bowler bowls; one player keeps wicket; nine field |

## The pitch and the wickets

The action happens on the **pitch**, which Law 6 defines as a rectangle 22 yards (20.12 m) long and 10 ft (3.05 m) wide, in the middle of a much larger field. At each end stands a **wicket**: three stumps topped by two bails, 28 in (71.12 cm) high and 9 in (22.86 cm) wide under Law 8. Painted lines called creases mark where batters are safe and where bowlers must deliver from.

## Overs: how bowling works

The bowler delivers the ball from one end towards the batter at the other. Law 17 groups deliveries into **overs** of six balls. Two details matter:

- **Only legal balls count.** A wide or a no ball does not count as one of the six, so it must be bowled again.
- **No bowler bowls two overs in a row.** After each over, the ends switch and a different bowler takes over. A bowler can bowl again from the next over but one.

In limited-overs cricket, each bowler also has a ceiling: the ICC's playing conditions cap a bowler at 10 overs in a one-day international and four in a T20 international.

## Scoring runs

Batters score in two main ways:

1. **Running.** After hitting the ball, the two batters run to swap ends. Each completed swap is one run.
2. **Boundaries.** Law 19 awards four runs if the ball crosses the boundary after touching the ground, and six if it clears the boundary without bouncing.

Runs can also come from **extras** that the fielding side gives away, such as a one-run penalty for every wide or no ball. [How cricket scoring works](/blog/how-cricket-scoring-works/) covers every way to score, with a worked over.

## Getting out

A batter who is dismissed is "out", and the fielding side has taken a **wicket**. The most common ways are being bowled, caught, leg before wicket, run out and stumped; the Laws list more. [Ways to get out in cricket](/blog/ways-to-get-out-in-cricket/) explains each.

Because batters bat in pairs, an innings is over when the tenth batter is out: the side is **all out**. Law 13 also ends an innings when the agreed overs are used up, or when the captain declares or forfeits it.

## Winning, tying and drawing

Law 16 sets out the results:

| Result | When it happens |
| --- | --- |
| Win by runs | The side fielding last wins: the other side's innings ends without passing its total |
| Win by wickets | The side batting last passes the target; the margin is the wickets it still had in hand |
| Tie | All innings are completed and the scores are exactly equal |
| Draw | The match ends without being won, tied, conceded or awarded, for example when time runs out |

A team that scores 250 and dismisses its opponent for 220 wins by 30 runs. A team chasing 251 that reaches 252 for 6 wins by 4 wickets, because four of its ten wickets were still to fall.

## Formats

The same Laws underpin very different games. A Test match gives each side two innings over up to five days; a one-day international gives each side one innings of up to 50 overs; a T20 gives each side 20.
