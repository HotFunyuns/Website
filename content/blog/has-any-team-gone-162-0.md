**No major-league team has gone 162-0, and none has come close.** The most wins in a season is 116, reached twice: by the 1906 Chicago Cubs, who went 116-36, and the 2001 Seattle Mariners, who went 116-46. Baseball-Reference lists the 2001 mark as the best record over a 162-game schedule, and MLB.com lists the 1906 winning percentage of .763 as the highest on record since 1900. Even the best team over 162 games lost 46 times — more than one game in every four.

The season itself has not always been 162 games. It became that long in 1961 in the American League and 1962 in the National, when expansion to ten teams changed the arithmetic of who played whom.

### Why "116" hides two different achievements

The two 116-win seasons were not played on the same terms. SABR's history of the schedule shows the 154-game season was standard from 1904 to 1960, so the 1906 Cubs were playing a shorter year than the 2001 Mariners. The sources also count that 1906 season differently: Baseball-Reference says the Cubs reached 116 wins "in just 155 games," while MLB.com describes it as a 152-game schedule. The difference is the gap between games played and decisions — 116 wins and 36 losses make 152 decisions, so three of Baseball-Reference's 155 games ended with neither result.

Either way, the 1906 Cubs lost ten fewer games than the 2001 Mariners. That is why they hold the percentage mark, while the Mariners own the 162-game mark outright.

The same tension shows up elsewhere in MLB.com's list. The 1927 Yankees' 110-44 works out to a .714 winning percentage, slightly better than the .704 of the 1998 Yankees' 114-48, despite four fewer wins. When schedules differ, wins and winning percentage can point at different "best" seasons, and both answers are defensible as long as you say which one you mean.

### Streaks are a different record

A season record measures sustained quality; a winning streak measures a hot stretch. MLB.com credits the 1916 New York Giants with the longest run, 26 consecutive victories, and describes Cleveland's 22-game run in 2017 as the longest in American League history. Twenty-six straight wins is still only about a sixth of a 162-game season. A perfect season would be a streak more than six times longer than anything in the record book.

## Why the season is 162 games

The number is the product of league size. SABR's article on schedule changes, published in its 1973 *Baseball Research Journal*, traces the history:

| Period | What the schedule looked like (per SABR) |
| --- | --- |
| 1876 | 70 games: eight National League teams playing each other 10 times |
| 1877–78 | 60 games, the shortest ever, with six teams |
| 1879–1891 | Gradually lengthened from 84 to 140 games |
| 1892 | The first 154-game schedule, in a 12-team National League |
| 1904–1960 | The "traditional" 154: eight teams, 22 games against each opponent, interrupted only in 1918 and 1919 |
| 1961 | The American League expands to ten teams and plays 162 |
| 1962 | The National League follows |

The arithmetic is simple once you see it. Eight teams playing each of seven opponents 22 times gives 154 games. Ten teams playing each of nine opponents 18 times gives 162. SABR's 1973 article notes that 1961 was, to that point, the only year in which the two leagues played schedules of unequal length, because only one of them had expanded.

Two near-misses show the number was never sacred. In 1946, according to SABR, the majors tentatively agreed to a 168-game schedule before opposition from the public and players brought them back to 154. In 1968 the National League tentatively agreed to 165 games for its first 12-team season, before going along with the American League's divisional plan. And 162 has not always been played in full: SABR records that a strike at the start of the 1972 season cancelled games and left clubs with uneven totals of 153 to 156.

## Is 162-0 possible?

In principle, yes; in practice, no. The probability arithmetic is set out in [understanding probability in sports simulations](/blog/understanding-sports-sim-probability/), so one line will do here: a team that won each game independently with a 0.763 probability — the best winning percentage MLB.com lists — would sweep 162 games roughly once in 10 quintillion seasons (0.763 raised to the 162nd power). Real games are not independent coin flips with a fixed probability, so treat that as an illustration of scale rather than a calculation of anything real.

The more intuitive version is in the record book. The best team over a 162-game season still lost 46 games, and the best team by percentage still lost 36. Every opponent is also a major-league team, and across a full season even the strongest clubs lose regularly.
