Wu Zhao — known to history by her posthumous title, Wu Zetian — is the only woman to have ruled China as emperor in her own right. She took the title in 690 under a new dynastic name, Zhou, and held it until a palace coup forced her abdication in 705. Almost everything narrative we know about her was written by men, centuries later, inside a tradition that regarded a female sovereign as a category error. That makes her one of the best cases in world history for a skill every reader of biography needs: how to use a source that is clearly against its subject without either swallowing it or flipping it.

## The record, and when it was made

Start with the dates of the documents rather than the dates of the events, because that comparison does most of the work.

Wu governed in the second half of the seventh century. The principal narrative sources are the *Jiu Tangshu* (Old Book of Tang), compiled in 945; the *Xin Tangshu* (New Book of Tang), completed in 1060; and Sima Guang's *Zizhi Tongjian*, the great chronological history finished in 1084. The earliest of those is roughly two and a half centuries after her death. The latest is nearly four.

None of them is a neutral chronicle. The *Zizhi Tongjian* in particular was written as an instructive work for rulers — its title translates as something like "comprehensive mirror in aid of governance" — and its purpose shapes its selection. Scholarship on Sima Guang's project treats it as a work with a historical argument, not a container of facts, which is the right way to read any great compilation.

There is also a second-order problem. Compilers of later dynastic histories worked from earlier state records, court diaries and veritable records, most of which do not survive independently. So we are usually reading an eleventh-century editor's use of a tenth-century compiler's use of records that have since been lost. That is a long chain, and the general principle described in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) applies at every link in it. The broader framework for judging this kind of claim about a person is laid out in [how we know what historical figures actually did](/blog/how-we-know-what-historical-figures-did/).

## What the hostility looks like in practice

The most notorious accusation against Wu is that she killed her own infant daughter and arranged for the blame to fall on the empress she was working to displace. It is repeated constantly in popular retellings. It reaches us through the later compilations rather than through any contemporary document, and the accounts do not agree with one another.

That combination — a charge that is most vivid in the latest telling and absent or unclear in the earliest surviving one, with no contemporary corroboration — is the signature of an accretion rather than a report. Historians do not therefore declare her innocent. They say the evidence cannot bear the weight the story puts on it, which is a different and more honest claim.

The same caution applies to the general picture of a court soaked in poisonings and purges. Purges certainly happened; the political violence of the period is well documented and she was a participant in it. But a hostile tradition tends to convert ordinary factional brutality — which every Tang reign had — into a specific personal pathology when the person at the centre is a woman. Modern Tang scholarship, including Andrew Eisenberg's work on factional politics in Gaozong's court, reads her rise as an episode inside a struggle between court factions rather than as an eruption of individual character.

## Reading a hostile source without inverting it

The tempting correction is to reverse every negative claim and produce a feminist hero. That is a second distortion, and it borrows its structure from the first: it still treats the source as a reliable guide, just with the sign changed.

Three moves are more useful.

**Separate what the source had no motive to invent.** Institutional details, appointments, dates, building projects, grants and titles are usually recorded because a bureaucracy recorded them, not because they make anyone look good or bad. They survive comparatively well.

**Ask what the charge is doing.** In a Confucian dynastic frame, disorder at the top requires a moral explanation. A female emperor is disorder by definition. So the tradition needs her to be monstrous, and the stories that do that job are precisely the ones that grow.

**Look for material evidence that is not narrative at all.** Inscriptions, sculpture, coinage, temple foundations and administrative documents were produced at the time and by different hands. Wu's patronage of Buddhist art is a case in point: the Longmen Grottoes outside Luoyang, which UNESCO describes as holding the largest and most impressive collection of Chinese stone carving from the late Northern Wei and Tang periods, contain work from her era that art historians have studied as evidence in its own right. Sculpture does not moralise about its patron.

## Why Buddhism mattered to her position

This is where the hostile record and the material record explain each other.

Confucian dynastic theory offered no legitimate slot for a woman holding the imperial title. There was no precedent, no ritual script and no argument available inside that system. Buddhism did offer one: a universal-monarch ideal and a body of scripture in which a female ruler is not an impossibility. Wu's court circulated an interpretation of a Buddhist scripture presenting her rule as prophesied, and she backed monastic foundations and large-scale religious sculpture.

Read cynically, that is propaganda. Read structurally, it is a ruler using the only available legitimating vocabulary — which is what rulers in difficulty always do. The point is not to decide whether she was sincere. It is that the choice of vocabulary is itself evidence about the constraints she was operating under, and those constraints were not of her making.

This is the difference between a biography that explains a person through personality and one that keeps them inside the structures they acted within. The second is harder and almost always more accurate, and it is a habit worth carrying into every figure you read about, as [studying history on your own](/blog/how-to-study-history-on-your-own/) tends to teach fairly quickly.

## What is reasonably secure

Stripping back to what the evidence supports without much strain:

- She entered the palace as a junior consort under Taizong, and returned to court under his successor Gaozong, becoming empress in 655.
- Gaozong's health declined badly from the 660s, and she exercised practical authority during that period.
- After his death in 683 she controlled the succession, deposing one son and installing another.
- In 690 she took the imperial title and declared a new dynasty, Zhou, ending the Tang in name for fifteen years.
- She shifted the working centre of the empire toward Luoyang.
- She patronised Buddhist institutions and monumental sculpture on a large scale.
- In 705 a coup forced her abdication; the Tang was restored and she died later that year.

Almost everything beyond that list — her motives, her cruelty or otherwise, the scale of her administrative reforms, the claim that she transformed the recruitment of officials — is interpretation, and different scholars weight it differently.

## The lesson worth taking

You will rarely have a friendly source and an unfriendly source to set against each other. More often you have several versions of the same unfriendly one.

When that happens, date the documents, notice which claims grow over time, keep the institutional record separate from the moral commentary, and look hard for evidence that was never meant as testimony — a wall, a coin, a statue, a tax exemption. Wu Zetian is the extreme case that makes the method visible. Most of the figures you read about are milder versions of the same problem.
