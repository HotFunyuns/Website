A Thai greeting has two parts: the words, and often a gesture, the *wai*. This guide covers the phrases for meeting and leaving, how to address people, and when to give or return a *wai*. Romanisations follow RTGS, which does not show tone.

## Sawatdi: hello and goodbye

The all-purpose greeting is **สวัสดี**, *sawatdi*, often romanised *sawasdee*. Wiktionary lists it for **greeting or farewell**, **answering the phone** and **attracting attention**, so one word covers arriving, leaving and picking up a call. The casual form หวัดดี, *watdi*, is slang.

The word is younger than it seems. Wikipedia's article on the *wai* says it was **coined in the mid-1930s** by Phraya Upakit Silapasan of Chulalongkorn University, from Sanskrit *svasti*, "well-being"; Wiktionary dates its first use to 1933 and its first official use to 1943. Before that, the *wai* article adds, the word had appeared only as a formulaic opening to inscriptions, until Plaek Phibunsongkhram's government promoted it in the early 1940s as part of a set of cultural edicts.

## Add khrap or kha

Thai greetings end with a polite particle chosen by the **speaker's** gender. The *wai* article notes that *sawatdi* is usually followed by **kha** from a woman and **khrap** from a man:

- a man says **สวัสดีครับ**, *sawatdi khrap*;
- a woman says **สวัสดีค่ะ**, *sawatdi kha*.

[Khrap and kha](/blog/thai-polite-particles/) explains the particles in full.

## The phrases for a first conversation

| Thai | RTGS | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| สวัสดี | sawatdi | hello, goodbye |
| สบายดีไหม | sabai di mai | how are you? |
| สบายดี | sabai di | I'm well |
| ขอบคุณ | khop khun | thank you |
| ขอโทษ | kho thot | sorry, excuse me |
| ลาก่อน | la kon | goodbye (said by the person leaving) |
| อรุณสวัสดิ์ | arun sawat | good morning |
| ราตรีสวัสดิ์ | ratri sawat | good night |

Add the particle to any of them: **สบายดีไหมครับ** from a man, **สบายดีไหมคะ** from a woman, with the high-tone *kha* because it is a question. Wiktionary notes that **ลาก่อน is used only by the person leaving**, and that plain ขอบคุณ and ขอโทษ suit people of equal or lower status; more formal forms exist for seniors.

## Names and khun

Wikipedia's article on Thai honorifics explains that a Thai name is a **given name followed by a surname**, and that people are addressed by their **given name**, often preceded by **คุณ khun** with friends or acquaintances. *Khun*, said with a mid tone, is a courtesy title used to address **nearly anyone** politely, and is not the old noble title ขุน. The article adds that **most people also have a nickname**, which is affectionate, used between people who are close, and generally **takes no title**.

So a colleague called Somchai Jaidee is *khun Somchai*, not "Mr Jaidee".

## The wai

Wikipedia describes the *wai* as **a slight bow with the palms pressed together** in a prayer-like position, related to the Indian *namaste*. The key rule: **the higher the hands relative to the face, and the lower the bow, the more respect** the person giving it is showing.

For visitors, its advice is practical:

1. **Always return a wai** that is offered as a sign of respect.
2. **Do not wai someone younger** than you, except in return for theirs.
3. **Shop wais**, such as a convenience store cashier's, are generally acknowledged with **a smile or a nod**.

The *wai* is traditionally made on **formally entering a house**, and when the visit ends the visitor **asks permission to leave** and repeats it.

## A worked example: visiting a friend's family

You are a woman visiting a friend's home, and her mother answers the door.

1. **On arrival**, wai the mother and say *sawatdi kha*. The wai is traditional on formally entering a house.
2. **If she asks** *sabai di mai kha*, answer *sabai di kha*, then return the question.
3. **When given a drink**, say *khop khun kha*.
4. **When leaving**, ask to take your leave and wai again: *sawatdi kha*, or *la kon kha* as the one leaving.

A man would use *khrap* in each of the same places.
