A career simulation asks a narrower question than a management game does. A general manager game asks whether you can assemble a roster. A career sim asks whether one player, whose talents you chose and whose development you directed, can turn a set of situations into a career worth reading back.

[Baseball Career Sim 2026](/apps/baseball-career-sim/) is the second kind. This guide covers the decisions that actually change the outcome — position, archetype, development priorities and in-game situational choices — and is honest about what the model behind them is and is not doing.

## The one decision that changes everything else

Position is not a cosmetic choice in this game. It determines three things at once:

1. **Which situations the simulator hands you.** A hitter is asked about counts, runners and swing decisions. A pitcher is asked about pitch selection, pitch count and when to hand the ball over.
2. **Which attributes are load-bearing.** Contact, power, plate vision, speed, baserunning, fielding and arm strength matter to different degrees depending on where you play. Control, velocity and stamina matter almost exclusively to pitchers.
3. **How often you appear.** A position player is in most games. A starting pitcher appears on a schedule. A closer appears in a specific situation that may not arise.

That third point surprises people. Two careers of identical length can contain wildly different amounts of gameplay, because a closer who pitches one inning in high-leverage situations sees far fewer decisions than an everyday second baseman.

### What each path actually feels like

| Path | Frequency of decisions | What you are mostly deciding | Difficulty for a first career |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Corner outfielder / first base | High | Swing decisions, baserunning | Lowest |
| Middle infield | High | Swing decisions plus defensive positioning | Moderate |
| Catcher | High | Offence, plus game management behind the plate | High |
| Starting pitcher | Periodic, dense | Pitch selection, sequencing, when you are done | High |
| Closer | Rare, high-stakes | One inning, maximum leverage | Highest variance |
| Two-way | Very high | Both of the above | Hardest to develop |

The two-way path is the most interesting and the least forgiving. Development points are a fixed budget, and a two-way player needs both a hitter's toolkit and a pitcher's. It rewards a second or third career, once you know what you are giving up.

## What the simulator is actually modelling

The game resolves plate appearances with the state of the inning in view: outs, count, runners on base, position in the batting order, pitch counts and bullpen availability. That matters because it changes which choice is correct, not just how likely it is to succeed.

Two situations, same swing:

- **Two out, nobody on, 1-2 count.** The cost of a strikeout is one out and nothing else. Aggression on a borderline pitch is cheap.
- **One out, runner on third, 1-2 count.** A strikeout wastes a run that a ball in play would probably score. The correct decision changes even though your attributes have not.

If you take one habit from this guide, take that one. Read the situation before you read the pitch. Players who plateau usually do so because they play every plate appearance the same way.

## Development is a budget, not a wish list

Attributes improve through a combination of position, performance, age and training. Three consequences follow, and all three are worth internalising early.

**Specialise before you generalise.** A player who is good at one thing gets used in the situations where that thing matters. A player who is average at six things is a bench player with a diversified portfolio of mediocrity.

**Age is a real constraint.** Improvement is easiest early. A skill you neglect for four seasons is not simply four seasons behind — it is being developed in the part of a career where development is hardest.

**Performance feeds back into development.** Results affect playing time, playing time affects opportunity, and opportunity affects how quickly you improve. This is why a modest position with a clear path to playing time often out-develops a glamorous one where you sit.

### A workable first-career plan

1. Pick a corner outfield or first base spot with a contact or power archetype.
2. Put early development into the two attributes your archetype already leans on. Do not spread.
3. Take playing time over prestige whenever the choice appears.
4. From roughly your third professional season, start putting points into the weakest attribute that is actually costing you outcomes — usually plate vision or baserunning.
5. Leave the two-way experiment for career number two.

## Reading your own statistics honestly

The game tracks hits, home runs, RBIs, stolen bases, batting average, on-base percentage, slugging, ERA, WHIP, strikeouts, wins and saves. Some of those tell you about your decisions. Some tell you about your team.

- **On-base percentage** is the closest thing to a direct measure of your plate discipline. It reflects walks, which is exactly what swing decisions control.
- **Slugging** reflects the outcome of contact rather than the frequency of it. MLB's own glossary defines [on-base plus slugging](https://www.mlb.com/glossary/standard-stats/on-base-plus-slugging) as the sum of the two, which is why it is a reasonable single summary of an offensive season.
- **RBIs** depend heavily on who bats in front of you. A great season on a bad team produces a modest RBI total, and that is not a verdict on you.
- **Wins**, for a pitcher, are the most team-dependent number on the list. [ERA](https://www.mlb.com/glossary/standard-stats/earned-run-average) — earned runs allowed per nine innings — is the more informative one.

If you want the broader vocabulary, [baseball statistics explained for beginners](/blog/baseball-stats-explained-for-beginners/) works through what each number is measuring and what it misses.

## What the model is not

The simulator assigns outcomes according to its own assumptions. It is not a forecast of any real athlete's development, it is not a prediction about any real season, and it is not guidance for wagering on anything. Every league, team and player is fictional; the pool is generated, which is precisely what makes the drafting and development decisions interesting rather than a memory test.

Season-to-season variation is a feature of the model rather than a fault in your plan. A career that produces one outlier season and four ordinary ones is a normal output, not evidence that you played the outlier season better.
