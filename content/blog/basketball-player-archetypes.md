A basketball player archetype is a shorthand for a bundle of two or three skills and the job those skills let a team do. "3-and-D wing" means a player who makes threes and defends the perimeter. "Rim protector" means a big who deters and blocks shots near the basket. "Stretch big" means a power forward or centre who shoots from distance. Archetypes describe what a player does, not where he stands, and they are most useful for working out who fits next to whom.

This is the companion to [basketball positions explained](/blog/basketball-positions-explained/), which covers the five numbered positions and how they have loosened. Positions answer "where does this player line up?". Archetypes answer "what does this player give the team, and what does he need around him?"

## How to read an archetype

Every archetype in this guide comes with three things:

- **Core skills.** The two or three abilities that define it. A player missing one of them is a different archetype.
- **Stat signature.** Where the archetype shows up in the numbers. The rates used here are defined in Basketball-Reference's glossary. Usage estimates the share of team plays a player uses, assist percentage the share of teammates' baskets he assisted while on the floor, and the rebound percentages the shares of available rebounds he collected.
- **What it needs.** The teammates who make it work. This is where team-building happens.

One caution applies to every defensive label. The box score records steals, blocks, rebounds and fouls, and little else on defence. Box Plus/Minus's own author writes that "on defense the box score is quite limited." A defensive archetype is often visible on film long before it is visible in a table.

## Perimeter archetypes

| Archetype | Core skills | Stat signature | Needs around him |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary creator | Handling under pressure, passing reads, scoring off the dribble | High usage and high assist percentage | Shooters to punish help, and a screener |
| 3-and-D wing | Catch-and-shoot threes, perimeter defence | Low usage; a large share of shots from three | A creator who draws help and kicks the ball out |
| Movement shooter | Shooting off screens and on the move | Many threes, few dribbles | Screeners and an offence that moves him |
| Slasher | Attacking closeouts, finishing at the rim, drawing fouls | Many free-throw attempts; shots near the rim | Spacing, so the lane is open |
| Point-of-attack defender | Guarding the ball-handler, getting through screens | Steals sometimes, but mostly invisible | A rim protector behind him |

### Why 3-and-D lives in the corner

Court geometry explains where the 3-and-D wing stands. Under the NBA's 2025-26 rules, the three-point arc is 23 feet 9 inches from the basket at the top, but only 22 feet in the corners, where the line runs straight along the sideline. A wing who spots up in the corner takes the shortest three on the floor, which is why the corner is home for a player whose job is to make threes without needing the ball. Under FIBA's 2026 rules the gap is much smaller: the arc is 6.75 m and the corner lines sit about 6.60 m from the basket.

### Primary creator against slasher

Both archetypes attack the defence with the ball, and they differ in how the possession ends. A primary creator finishes possessions for others as often as for himself, which shows up as a high assist percentage alongside high usage. A slasher ends possessions at the rim or the free-throw line. A team can have one of each comfortably. Two primary creators who both need the ball can work, but only if at least one of them also shoots well without it.

## Frontcourt archetypes

| Archetype | Core skills | Stat signature | Needs around him |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rim protector | Timing, length, vertical contests, defensive rebounding | Blocks and a high defensive rebound percentage | Perimeter defenders who funnel drives toward him |
| Rim-running big | Screening, rolling hard, finishing lobs | Efficient shooting close to the basket; offensive rebounds | A ball-handler for the pick and roll |
| Stretch big | Shooting from distance, screening | Three-point attempts unusual for his size | Drivers who use the space he creates |
| Playmaking big | Passing from the high post or elbows, decision-making | High assist percentage for a big | Cutters and shooters to find |
| Post scorer | Scoring with his back to the basket, footwork | High usage near the basket | Shooters, so help defenders cannot double him freely |

### What the rules ask of a rim protector

A rim protector's job is defined as much by the rulebook as by athleticism. FIBA's "principle of verticality" protects a defender who jumps straight up within his own space: he "must not be penalised" for leaving the floor vertically. In the NBA, a help defender who sets up inside the 4-foot restricted arc under the basket cannot draw a charge from a player driving straight to the rim. Both rules push rim protection toward contesting straight up rather than stepping in front to take a charge.

The NBA adds one more constraint. Its defensive three-second rule requires a defender in the lane to be within arm's length of an opponent within three seconds, so an NBA rim protector cannot simply stand in the paint guarding nobody. FIBA has no equivalent rule, which makes a stationary rim protector easier to use internationally.

### Stretch big against rim-running big

These two are the classic either-or at centre, and the choice shapes the whole offence. A rim-running big pulls the defence toward the basket and opens the perimeter. A stretch big pulls a defender away from the basket and opens the lane. Neither is better in general; what matters is which space your creators need. A slasher wants the lane empty, so he wants a stretch big. A pull-up shooter wants the defence's big drawn to the rim, so he wants a roller.

## The connectors

Some of the most valuable archetypes have the least obvious numbers.

- **Connector or glue player.** Quick decisions, extra passes, cuts and screens. His usage is low and his assist-to-turnover ratio clean, and his own line is usually modest, so judge him by how the lineup plays with him on the floor.
- **Two-way forward.** Defends several positions and does enough on offence to stay on the floor. He is the archetype that makes switching defences possible.
- **Rebounding specialist.** Ends defensive possessions and extends offensive ones, so his rebound percentages stand out even in limited minutes.

## Which archetypes fit together

| Pairing | Why it works | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary creator + rim-running big | The core of the pick and roll; the roll threat pulls the defence in | Needs shooters around it, or the defence packs the paint |
| Slasher + stretch big | The stretch big empties the lane the slasher wants | Rebounding near the rim can suffer |
| Rim protector + point-of-attack defender | One pressures the ball, the other erases mistakes | Both may be limited shooters |
| Playmaking big + movement shooters | The big finds cutters and shooters coming off screens | Needs a big who can actually pass under pressure |
| Primary creator + 3-and-D wings | The creator draws help; the wings punish it | Asks a lot of one creator if he is the only one |

The poor fits mirror these: two non-shooting bigs on the floor together crowd the lane, two ball-dominant creators with no off-ball game take turns watching each other, and five shooters with no rim protection give up the basket. Archetypes are simply a quick way to see which jobs a player covers.
