The Marshall Plan — officially the European Recovery Program — was a US aid programme for Western Europe, proposed by Secretary of State George C. Marshall in June 1947 and signed into law by President Truman on 3 April 1948. The National Archives says that over the next four years Congress appropriated $13.3 billion for European recovery, providing "much needed capital and materials that enabled Europeans to rebuild the continent's economy." It was also an instrument of the early Cold War: the Soviet Union refused it and kept its Eastern European satellites out.

Here is what it was for, who received the money, and why even its total is quoted in more than one way.

## Europe in 1947: why visible damage was not the main problem

The National Archives describes a continent in ruins in 1945 — cities shattered, economies devastated, people facing famine — with Soviet control of Eastern Europe sharpening the sense of crisis. The Office of the Historian adds the rapid deterioration of European economies in the winter of 1946–47.

Marshall's own diagnosis, in the speech transcript held by the National Archives, was subtler than rubble. The visible destruction of cities, factories, mines and railroads, he said, "was probably less serious than the dislocation of the entire fabric of European economy." His example was the breakdown of trade between town and country: factories were not producing goods that farmers wanted, so farmers withdrew land from crops and fed grain to livestock, while cities went short of food and governments spent scarce foreign currency on imports instead of reconstruction. Europe's needs for the next three or four years, he argued, were "so much greater than her present ability to pay" that it must have substantial help. Reading the speech in full, as [a primary source](/blog/how-to-analyse-a-primary-source/), shows how much of the argument was economic rather than military.

## From a Harvard speech to a law, 1947–1948

The sequence, from the National Archives and Wikipedia:

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| 5 June 1947 | Marshall's speech at Harvard proposes that Europeans draw up a recovery plan, with US aid |
| July 1947 | Paris talks; Molotov rejects the plan and leaves |
| 12 July 1947 | A wider Paris meeting convenes, without the Soviet bloc |
| 19 December 1947 | Truman sends Congress a message on aid to Europe |
| 3 April 1948 | Truman signs the Economic Cooperation Act |

Marshall framed the policy as "directed not against any country or doctrine but against hunger, poverty, desperation, and chaos." The National Archives notes that Congress passed the act overwhelmingly, extending wartime bipartisanship into the postwar years; Wikipedia adds that Republicans controlled Congress while a Democrat held the White House.

## Who got the money

Wikipedia records that aid was divided roughly per head, with larger amounts for the major industrial powers and somewhat more per head for wartime Allies than for former Axis states or neutrals. Its top three recipients, applied to the National Archives' $13.3 billion total, work out as follows:

| Recipient | Share (Wikipedia) | Approximate amount of $13.3 billion |
|---|---|---|
| United Kingdom | about 26% | about $3.46 billion |
| France | 18% | about $2.39 billion |
| West Germany | 11% | about $1.46 billion |

Those three took about 55% between them. Wikipedia also cites a Congressional Research Service report that 83% of Marshall Plan funds were spent within the United States — which fits the Office of the Historian's point that the plan "was also a stimulant to the U.S. economy by establishing markets for American goods."

## Three totals, one programme

The headline figure is less settled than it looks. The National Archives gives $13.3 billion appropriated over four years. The Office of the Historian says funding "would eventually rise to over $12 billion." Wikipedia gives $13.3 billion to 17 countries in its summary, says "some eighteen" countries received benefits, and elsewhere states that the United States donated $17 billion in economic and technical assistance over the four years — while noting a further $17 billion of aid between the war's end and the plan's start, counted separately. The sources do not reconcile the figures, but they differ in what they include and over which years; the plan was largely replaced by the Mutual Security Act in 1951. Treating a total as a claim to check, rather than a fact to copy, is the habit described in [reading a history dataset critically](/blog/reading-a-history-dataset-critically/).

## The Soviet refusal and the dividing of Europe

The Office of the Historian says Soviet and Eastern European participation was initially possible, but Soviet concern over US economic domination of its satellites and Stalin's unwillingness to open his "secret society" to westerners doomed the idea — adding that Congress would probably not have funded the plan so generously had aid gone to the Soviet bloc. Wikipedia describes Molotov leaving the Paris talks, the Czechoslovak and Polish delegations being prevented from attending the next meeting, and Finland declining to avoid antagonising Moscow. The Soviets later set up COMECON as their response. The result, in the Office of the Historian's words, was a plan applied solely to Western Europe.

## Did it work?

The Office of the Historian is careful: "economic historians have debated the precise impact of the Marshall Plan," though it calls the plan "a great humanitarian effort" and notes that Marshall became the only general ever awarded the Nobel Peace Prize. Wikipedia gives a sceptical measure: aid amounted to about 3% of the recipients' combined national income in 1948–1951, implying an increase in GDP growth of less than half a percentage point.
