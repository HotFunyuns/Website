A timeline of inventions that starts with the printing press is starting about 2.6 million years too late. The oldest technology with a continuous archaeological record is stone toolmaking, and the Smithsonian's Human Origins Program places its beginning at least that far back — before any species most readers would call human. Building the timeline honestly from there does two things: it spreads invention across every inhabited continent, and it makes clear that the question "who invented this" almost never has a single answer.

## The deep end of the timeline

Stone tools dominate the earliest record for an unglamorous reason: they survive. As the Smithsonian's summary puts it, stone artefacts are less susceptible to destruction than bone, so they typically offer the best evidence of where and when early humans lived and how they spread. Oldowan toolkits — hammerstones, stone cores and sharp flakes — mark the beginning, and by about 1.76 million years ago Acheulean handaxes and other large cutting tools appear.

The same page includes a caution worth carrying through the entire subject. Multiple hominin species often existed at the same time, so it can be difficult to determine which species made the tools at any given site. Before we have names for inventors, we frequently do not even have species. Organic technologies of the same age — digging sticks, containers, cordage — presumably existed and left nothing behind, which means the deep timeline is shaped as much by what preserves as by what happened.

## Why "who invented it" usually fails

Most technologies are processes, not events. A technique is tried, fails, is tried again with a variation, spreads, is improved by people who never met the first maker, and at some point becomes reliable enough to change what a society can do. The moment a name gets attached is usually the moment the technology became institutionally visible — a patent, a court record, a published description — which is a fact about archives, not about invention.

The distribution of surviving documentation is itself lopsided. Industrialising Europe generated patent registers, trade periodicals and company archives in enormous quantity, and those records are catalogued and searchable. The absence of an equivalent paper trail elsewhere says something about record-keeping institutions and preservation, not about who was solving problems. Reading a technology timeline critically starts with asking whose documents produced it, a habit that runs through everything in [how to learn something from a world history timeline](/blog/world-history-timeline-guide/).

## The inventions that were not machines

Popular invention lists are dominated by devices, which quietly excludes some of the most consequential technologies ever developed: ways of recording, measuring and organising.

UNESCO's description of Caral-Supe, the 5,000-year-old site in the Supe valley of Peru, notes that a quipu — the knotted-cord system used in Andean societies to record information — was found there, alongside monumental platform mounds, sunken circular courts and six large pyramidal structures, in one of eighteen urban settlements in the same area. A recording technology is not writing, and it is not a machine, and a society that has one can do things a society without one cannot.

The same is true of standardised weights and measures, of canal and reservoir systems, of calendars, of double-entry accounting, and of the institutional inventions — guild training, apprenticeship, patent law — that determine whether a technique spreads or dies with its maker. An invention timeline that only counts objects will always look like a parade of Western machinery, because that is the shape of the object record.

## Correcting the regional spread

The corrective is not to add token entries; it is to take other regions' technological histories as research fields in their own right. East Asian history of science is exactly that. The Needham Research Institute in Cambridge describes itself as a centre for the study of the history of science, technology and medicine in East Asia, houses the East Asian History of Science Library, and is the home of the *Science and Civilisation in China* project.

Its current work also illustrates how badly one-directional diffusion models fit. A forthcoming institute-affiliated study examines foreign astral sciences in China from the Six Dynasties to the Northern Song, tracing knowledge carried by monks, travellers and merchants from across Eurasia through the first millennium CE, and comparing surviving Chinese texts with parallel material in Sanskrit and Pāli and with works by Nestorian, Zoroastrian and Manichean authors. Technical knowledge moved along the same routes as goods and in as many directions — a point developed for the commercial side in [what the Silk Road actually was](/blog/the-silk-road-for-beginners/).

Chemistry and industry have their own dedicated archive in the Science History Institute, whose stated remit is the lesser-known and overlooked stories of science and technology and how science and society intersect. That framing is doing real work: the overlooked stories are where most of the invention actually happened.

## Attribution in the modern era: the transistor

If single attribution worked anywhere, it would work in the twentieth century, where documentation is dense and dated. It does not. The Nobel Prize in Physics for 1956 was awarded jointly to William Bradford Shockley, John Bardeen and Walter Houser Brattain, in the Nobel Foundation's wording, for their researches on semiconductors and their discovery of the transistor effect. Three names, one award, one of the most consequential devices ever made — and the prize citation describes research on a phenomenon rather than the construction of a gadget.

The far end of the timeline shows a different kind of change: not only in the devices but in who owns them. Reusable launch vehicles are the engineering headline, and the arrangement underneath them is the more unusual development. NASA now describes its commercial space programme as enabling industry to build, own and operate space systems while the agency purchases services for its science and research needs, with public-private partnerships as the mechanism and the International Space Station approaching the end of its operations. That is an institutional invention as much as an engineering one, and it will look like a hinge to whoever writes this timeline in fifty years.

## Reading an invention chain without over-claiming

Consequence is the part of invention history that repays attention, and it is where most popular accounts stop. World History Timeline Sim — our own app, built by Reign Creative — includes 1,041 invention chains, each laid out as a sequence: the world before, the immediate shock, fifty years later, the long ripple, the twist nobody saw, and what would have followed if the thing had never existed. Working through that structure is a genuinely useful exercise in causal reasoning, because it forces you to distinguish the immediate effect from the second-order one.

Two honest caveats. The chains are a learning and exploration tool, not a source: they carry no citations and no external review, and the app's own disclaimer screen says it does not replace textbooks, peer-reviewed scholarship or primary sources. And the counterfactual step — what if it never existed — is a thinking exercise, not a finding; nothing in a scenario tells you what would actually have happened.

Used with those limits in mind, the format is a good antidote to the great-man version of technology.
