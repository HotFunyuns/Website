A professional athlete's contract may be negotiated by someone the fans rarely see: the **agent**. Wikipedia describes a sports agent as a legal representative for professional athletes and coaches who negotiates employment and endorsement contracts on their behalf. In the NFL and the NBA, the players' own unions certify agents and cap what they may charge. This guide explains the job and the rules, using the NFL Players Association's and National Basketball Players Association's published regulations.

## The job

Wikipedia's summary of the role:

- **Negotiate contracts** with teams, and endorsement deals with sponsors.
- **Understand the system**: salary-cap rules, league regulations and the economics of sports labour markets.
- **Represent several clients**, sometimes starting while athletes are still amateurs or college players, within league and state rules.

The contract work is technical. An NFL agent negotiates inside a salary cap and rookie-pay rules, which [the NFL salary cap](/blog/how-does-the-nfl-salary-cap-work/) and [the NFL rookie contract](/blog/nfl-rookie-contract-explained/) explain, and an NBA agent works through a calendar of options, free agency and trades; [how the NBA offseason works](/blog/how-the-nba-offseason-works/) sets it out.

## How agents are regulated

Wikipedia notes that in the United States, agents are governed by both **state law** and **league certification systems**:

| Layer | What it requires |
| --- | --- |
| State law | Most states have enacted the Uniform Athlete Agents Act, requiring registration, disclosure of fees and written contracts between agents and athletes |
| Players' associations | Unions such as the NFLPA and NBPA require certification before an agent can negotiate player contracts |

Outside the US, football has its own system. Wikipedia notes FIFA's Regulations on Working with Intermediaries, introduced in 2015, required national associations to publish data on agent fees and transactions, and that from 10 July 2029 an EU regulation adopted in 2024 brings football agents within anti-money-laundering rules.

## What NFL agents can charge

The NFLPA's Regulations Governing Contract Advisors, in the edition the union's website publishes (as amended through August 2016), set the cap: the maximum fee is **3% of the player's compensation** for each playing season covered by the contract the agent negotiated. Lower caps apply to one-year tenders:

| Situation | Maximum fee |
| --- | --- |
| A negotiated player contract | 3% |
| One-year tender as a franchise or transition player, or restricted free agent | 2% |
| Franchise or transition tender, second time tagged | 1.5% |
| Franchise or transition tender, third time tagged | 1% |

The NFLPA's standard representation agreement goes further: the fee is **1.5%** unless both the agent and player initial a different figure, up to the cap.

## What NBA agents can charge

The NBPA's regulations, as amended in September 2025, set a cap of **4%** of the compensation negotiated for each season, and **2%** for any season in which the player receives only the league minimum. Each season is judged on its own: on a three-year contract above the minimum in year one and at the minimum in years two and three, the agent may charge up to 4% in year one and 2% in the other two.

First-round draft picks are treated differently. Their pay follows the rookie scale in Article VIII of the collective bargaining agreement, and the NBPA's standard agent contract caps the fee at the **greater** of two amounts: 4% of the compensation **in excess of the 80% amount guaranteed under the rookie scale**, or the 2% fee a rookie on only the minimum salary would pay. Suppose, as a simplified illustration, a pick's 80% amount is $4 million and the agent negotiates $5 million. Four per cent of the $1 million difference is $40,000, against $200,000 for 4% of the full $5 million. If the minimum salary were $1.2 million, the alternative would be 2% of it, $24,000, so the cap would be $40,000. For every one of these calculations, the regulations count as compensation only the base salary, signing bonus and any performance bonus the player actually receives.

## Worked examples

| Contract | Rate | Fee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NFL, $10 million for one season | 3% maximum | $300,000 |
| NFL, $10 million for one season | 1.5% default | $150,000 |
| NFL, $12 million a year for four years | 3% maximum | $1.44 million in total |
| NBA, $10 million for one season | 4% maximum | $400,000 |
| NBA, $1.2 million minimum-salary season | 2% maximum | $24,000 |

The caps are ceilings: both unions' rules let players and agents agree a lower percentage.
