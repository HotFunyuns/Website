Aksum was a state in the northern Ethiopian highlands that UNESCO dates from the first to the eighth centuries CE, positioned at the junction of Africa, Arabia and the Greco-Roman world. Its fleets controlled Red Sea trade through the port of Adulis, it commanded the ivory trade with Sudan, and it adopted Christianity in the fourth century CE. UNESCO's own assessment calls it the most powerful state between the Eastern Roman Empire and Persia.

Almost none of that appears in a standard ancient-history syllabus, which is the interesting problem.

## What the site actually contains

The remains of Aksumite civilisation cover a wide area of the Tigray Plateau. UNESCO's inscription, made in 1980 under criteria (i) and (iv), lists monolithic obelisks, giant stelae, royal tombs and the ruins of ancient castles, with the massive ruins dating from between the first and the thirteenth centuries CE and the palace remains from the sixth and seventh.

The stelae are the part most people have seen a photograph of. Several standing in the town date to the third and fourth centuries CE. The largest still upright rises more than 23 metres and is carved to represent a nine-storey building, complete with the pattern of beam-ends and false doors that Aksumite builders used in timber-and-stone construction. A larger one, roughly 33 metres long, lies broken where it fell. UNESCO's careful phrasing is that it fell "perhaps during the process of erection" and that it is possibly the largest monolithic stele ancient people ever attempted to raise.

That "perhaps" is doing honest work. It is a hypothesis about an event nobody recorded, offered as such.

Aksum is also not Ethiopia's only stele tradition, and the comparison is a useful check on how much is actually known. About 200 kilometres away in the Soddo region south of Addis Ababa, the site of Tiya holds 36 monuments including 32 carved stelae covered with symbols. UNESCO describes most of those symbols as difficult to decipher and states plainly that the stelae are the remains of an ancient Ethiopian culture "whose age has not yet been precisely determined" — one of roughly 160 archaeological sites so far recorded in that region alone. Aksum is the best-documented part of a landscape that is mostly still open.

## Ezana's inscriptions and why they matter

A series of inscriptions on stone tablets has, in UNESCO's assessment, proved of immense importance to historians of the ancient world. Some are trilingual, carrying the same text in Greek, Sabaean and Ge'ez, and were inscribed by King Ezana in the fourth century CE.

Trilingual royal inscriptions are among the most useful objects an archaeologist can find, for the same reason the Bisotun relief matters for Persia: they anchor a language, a ruler and a set of claims to each other. Greek tells you who Aksum expected to read it internationally. Sabaean points across the Red Sea to South Arabia. Ge'ez is the local literary language, and it is still the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church today.

These are royal texts, which means they are statements a king wanted carved in stone rather than neutral reports. What they establish securely is the existence of the king, the languages of his court and the terms in which he wished to be understood.

## An early Christian state

UNESCO records that the introduction of Christianity in the fourth century CE resulted in church building at Aksum, including Saint Mary of Zion, which was rebuilt in the Gondarian period in the seventeenth century and which tradition holds contains the Ark of the Covenant.

Treat those three things as three different kinds of claim, because they are. The fourth-century adoption of Christianity is a documented historical development supported by inscriptions and church archaeology. The seventeenth-century rebuilding is a datable architectural fact. The Ark is a religious tradition held by a living community, and an article like this one has no business either endorsing or mocking it — the correct description is that it is believed, by whom, and since when.

The timing is what makes Aksum matter for world history. A state adopting Christianity in the fourth century CE places it in the same window as the Roman Empire's own shift, which is precisely why "the ancient world" as usually taught — a Mediterranean story with an eastern frontier — is too small a frame. The same problem shows up in [the Silk Road for beginners](/blog/the-silk-road-for-beginners/), where the Eurasian overland routes get a name and the Red Sea and Indian Ocean networks often do not.

## Why the stelae fell: a case study in competing explanations

This is the best small example of historical disagreement the site offers, and it is worth working through.

Ethiopian tradition offers two culprits. One is Yodit, also called Gudit, a queen remembered in later accounts as having brought down the pagan stelae in the tenth century CE. The other is Imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi, Amir of Harar, who is said to have ordered them felled by cannon fire in the sixteenth century.

A study published in *Annals of Geophysics* took a different approach: it examined the alignment of the various fallen members, found a prevailing orientation, and argued from that pattern that earthquakes brought several of the great stelae down. Its conclusion is not a clean replacement of the traditional accounts. The authors note that inadequate systems of anchorage and the mediocre mechanical qualities of the soil made collapse likely from natural causes — earthquakes and erosion of foundations — *and* from deliberate demolition.

So the honest answer to "why did the stelae fall" is: probably several reasons, over several centuries, with the physical evidence pointing more strongly to seismic collapse than the traditions do, and with neither tradition proven false. That structure — multiple partial causes, evidence that constrains rather than settles — is the same one you find in [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/), and it is much more typical of real historical problems than a single decisive cause.

One stele has a modern history too. An Aksumite obelisk removed to Italy was returned to Ethiopia, with UNESCO handling its reintegration into the World Heritage site in 2005.

## What came after

UNESCO's summary notes that Aksum declined politically in the tenth century, and that Ethiopian emperors continued to be crowned there long afterwards. Political power and ritual authority came apart, and the ritual authority outlasted the power by many centuries.

The next chapter is visible about 645 kilometres from Addis Ababa, where eleven medieval monolithic churches were carved out of solid rock at Lalibela. UNESCO attributes their construction to King Lalibela, who set out in the twelfth century to build a "New Jerusalem" after Muslim conquests halted Christian pilgrimage to the Holy Land, and states directly that Lalibela flourished after the decline of the Aksumite empire. Those churches were not built from blocks; they were hewn downward from living rock and then hollowed out, with drainage ditches, trenches and ceremonial passages cut around them.

## Fitting Aksum into a world timeline

The practical difficulty with Aksum is placement. Readers know roughly where the Roman Empire and Han China sit on a mental timeline and have nowhere to put a Red Sea power that peaks between them.
