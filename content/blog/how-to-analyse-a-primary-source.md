Analyse a primary source in five fixed steps: establish where it came from, place it in its moment, read what it assumes as well as what it states, corroborate it against something independent, and write down what it cannot show. The order matters, because the first step changes the meaning of everything that follows. If you already know the difference between a document made at the time and a later account of it, you have what you need to start — [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) covers that distinction, and this article does not repeat it.

## Step 1: Source it before you read it

Establish who made the document, when, for whom, and why, before you read a word of the content. The US National Archives builds its worksheets around four stages that begin with "Meet the document" and only reach "Use it as historical evidence" at the end. The Digital Inquiry Group's historical thinking chart puts the same move first and calls it *sourcing*.

The reason is not procedural tidiness. A sentence such as "the harvest was sufficient" means one thing in a private letter, another in a provincial governor's report to a capital that punishes shortfalls, and another again in a pamphlet arguing against a tax. Same words, three different evidentiary values. Sourcing first means you never read a document innocently.

Four questions do most of the work. Who is the author, and what position were they writing from? When was it made, relative to the events it describes? Who was the intended audience? And what was the document *for* — to record, to persuade, to instruct, to account, or to accuse?

## Step 2: Place it in its moment

Contextualising means reconstructing what was going on around the document, and what its writer could and could not have known. A dispatch written three weeks before news of a defeat arrived is not ignorant; it is simply earlier than the information.

This is where a chronology earns its keep. Knowing the sequence around a document — what had just happened, what was about to — often explains its tone better than anything in the text does. That is the practical use of a timeline, and it is why chronology is scaffolding for source work rather than an end in itself. Where the dates themselves are uncertain, [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) sets out what dating methods can and cannot settle.

## Step 3: Read for what it assumes, not only what it states

The stated content of a document is usually the part its author controlled most carefully. The assumptions are not. A ration list assumes a system of measurement, a payment schedule, a hierarchy of recipients and a bureaucracy capable of enforcing all three — none of which it argues for, because none of it was in dispute.

This is why administrative records so often outperform narratives as evidence for ordinary life. They were not written to convince anyone of anything. As a rule of thumb: a document is most reliable about the things it had no reason to lie about, and least reliable about the thing it was created to argue.

Read for silences too. Who does not appear? Whose labour is implied but unnamed? The Historical Thinking Project treats perspective-taking as a distinct competence precisely because the people missing from a record are rarely missing by accident.

## Step 4: Corroborate against something independent

Corroboration means finding a second source that could not have copied the first. Two later books quoting the same chronicle are one source wearing two jackets. Real corroboration crosses evidence types: a tax roll against a narrative account, an excavation report against a chronicle, a shipping manifest against a memoir.

Where genuinely independent sources agree on an incidental detail, confidence rises sharply. Where they disagree, you have not failed — you have found the interesting part, and the next question is *why* they differ. Distance from the event, intended audience, date of composition and the writer's interest account for most disagreements.

## Step 5: Say what the source cannot show

Finish by writing one plain sentence about the limits of the evidence. "This register shows what the monastery recorded receiving; it does not show what was produced, consumed or concealed." That sentence is the difference between an interpretation and a guess, and it is the step most often skipped.

The UK National Archives builds the habit into its classroom material, which repeatedly asks what a poster, a telegram or a photograph was designed to achieve, and how a caption changes what an image appears to prove. Its lessons on Elizabethan and Jacobite propaganda, on wartime government posters, and on faked photography from the South African War all make the same point: a source can be entirely authentic and still not evidence for the thing it seems to show.

## Practising the procedure without an archive

You do not need special access to practise. Both national archives publish digitised documents alongside free analysis worksheets, and large quantities of treaties, newspapers, inscriptions and photographs are online. Work through a document cold, write your five answers, and only then read the catalogue description to see what you missed.

A history app can supply practice scaffolding, and this is the point to disclose an interest: World History Timeline Sim is our own app, built by Reign Creative. Its Daily Life mode is relevant here because each of its 478 daily-life profiles carries a "HOW WE KNOW" note naming the kind of evidence a claim rests on, and grades its own confidence as high, medium or low. Seeing a claim tied explicitly to cuneiform ration tablets, or to excavated housing, or to a single later chronicle, makes the evidence question concrete in a way a bare statement of fact never does.

Two honest limits apply. The app's content carries no citations and has had no external review, so it is a prompt for the question rather than an answer to it — use it to notice that a claim has an evidence type, then check the claim elsewhere. And no app replaces a teacher, a scholarly monograph, an archive, or the document itself. The app's own disclaimer screen says the same thing.

## Turning the five steps into a habit

The procedure is short enough to run from memory, which is the point. Written out, it is: source, place, assume, corroborate, limit. Applying it to one document a week does more for your reading than applying it once to twenty.

If you are building a self-directed reading plan around it, [studying history on your own](/blog/how-to-study-history-on-your-own/) covers choosing a spine and keeping notes that survive, and the [history learning methods hub](/blog/topics/history-learning-methods/) collects the rest of the method articles. More history and study writing sits in the [education and brain training section](/blog/category/education-brain/), and the app is described on the [World History Timeline Sim page](/apps/world-history-timeline-sim/).

One last caution. The five steps produce a defensible reading, not a certain one. Historians working from the same document reach different conclusions all the time, and the professional standard is to say so rather than to flatten the disagreement. A good analysis ends with a position, and with a note of what would change it.
