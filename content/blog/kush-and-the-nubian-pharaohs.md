The Nubian pharaohs were kings of the Kingdom of Kush — a state on the middle Nile in what is now northern Sudan — who took control of Egypt and ruled it as pharaohs for several generations in the eighth and seventh centuries BCE. Egyptian chronology numbers them the Twenty-fifth Dynasty. They were not a brief foreign interruption in someone else's story: they came out of a sequence of Nubian states that lasted, in one form or another, for more than two thousand years.

That last point is the one most often missed. Nubia is usually taught as Egypt's southern margin, a corridor for gold and soldiers. The archaeological record describes something else.

## Kush was a sequence, not a single kingdom

UNESCO's inscription for Gebel Barkal and the Napatan sites describes the Napatan culture as running from about 900 to 270 BCE and the Meroitic culture from about 270 BCE to 350 CE, and explicitly calls both the cultures "of the second kingdom of Kush". The phrasing matters: a *second* kingdom implies a first, and that earlier state is the one centred on Kerma, upstream of the third cataract.

So the shape of Nubian history is three long phases with different capitals rather than one continuous polity. UNESCO's assessment of the Island of Meroe puts it plainly, calling Meroe the heartland of the Kingdom of Kush and "a major power in the ancient world from the 8th century BCE to the 4th century CE", with Meroe becoming the principal royal residence and, from the third century BCE, the site of most royal burials.

Gebel Barkal itself was already sacred long before the Napatan kings made it a political centre. UNESCO dates its religious significance to New Kingdom times, around 1500 BCE, when Egyptians held that the state god Amon resided in the flat-topped mountain. Napatan kingship attached itself to that existing sanctity rather than inventing it.

## How Kushite kings came to rule Egypt

By the eighth century BCE the Napatan kings were powerful enough to move north. UNESCO records that the site is "connected with the greatest Kings of the Middle Nile Region, whose political power extended up to the Egyptian Delta and Palestine" — which is a compact way of saying that a Sudanese dynasty governed Egypt and projected force into the Levant.

The best-documented of these kings is Taharqa. A rock-cut temple at Gebel Barkal preserves a scene of him worshipping Amon seated inside the mountain, and UNESCO notes that he is the only Sudanese sovereign mentioned by name in the Old Testament. That biblical mention is worth pausing on, because it is a good example of how the evidence reaches us: a Kushite king is known to many readers through a Hebrew text written by people with their own political concerns, not through anything he wrote.

Kushite rule over Egypt lasted only a few generations. It ended in the seventh century BCE under Assyrian military pressure, and almost everything we know about that campaign comes from Assyrian royal inscriptions — victory documents composed for an Assyrian court, which is exactly the kind of source that needs reading against the grain. Anyone weighing that material will find the framework in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) useful, because the question is not whether the Assyrians won but how much of their account of the winning to believe.

The kingdom itself did not end. It moved its centre of gravity south and carried on for roughly another thousand years.

## What the Kushites left, and how it is read

The Napatan kings adopted Egyptian forms deliberately. UNESCO's criteria for the site note the revival of "the Egyptian old script and the worship of the State God Amon", and the corbel-vaulted tombs at el-Kurru are singled out as a building technique that influenced Mediterranean architecture from the seventh century BCE onward.

That adoption creates a real interpretive problem. When a Kushite king writes in Egyptian, in Egyptian script, invoking an Egyptian god, it is genuinely hard to separate political claim from cultural identity — and historians disagree about how much of it reflects belief and how much reflects a bid for legitimacy in a language Egypt would recognise.

The material record is less ambiguous. UNESCO describes Meroe's remains as including pyramids, temples, palaces and industrial areas, with iron-working and ceramics production attesting to the wealth and power of the Kushite state, plus water reservoirs that tell us about the region's ancient climate. The Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures in Chicago, whose Nubian gallery holds one of the more complete collections of Nubian material outside Sudan, lists among its highlights the Qustul incense burner — one of the earliest records of Nubian kings — along with A-Group ceramics, C-Group figurines, a bronze statue of a Nubian king and brightly painted Meroitic pottery.

## The historiography is part of the subject

You cannot write honestly about Nubia without saying that Egyptology spent a long time getting it wrong, and that the errors were not random.

UNESCO's own description of the Nubian monuments between Abu Simbel and Philae preserves the older framing in fossil form: it describes how, in each great period of Egyptian history, there was a seizure of Nubia, "which became a natural annex to the Kingdom and later a colony whose fiscal and commercial income was transferred to Aswan." Read as a description of Egyptian imperial practice, that is accurate. Read as a description of Nubia, it defines a whole region by what was done to it.

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship frequently went further, treating Nubian achievement as borrowed and Nubian states as derivative. The corrective work has come substantially from Sudanese institutions and from long-running excavation programmes on the middle Nile, and UNESCO's own [General History of Africa](https://www.unesco.org/en/general-history-africa) — a multi-volume corpus begun in 1964 and written substantially by African scholars — exists precisely because the continent's history had been narrated from outside.

There is also a physical loss to record. Between 1960 and 1980 UNESCO ran an international campaign to relocate temples threatened by the reservoir behind the Aswan High Dam. Abu Simbel, Amada, Wadi Sebua, Kalabsha and Philae were moved. Many other sites, and the homes of the Nubian communities living among them, were not. Decisions made by the Egyptian and Sudanese governments of the period, with international backing, determined what was saved and what went under the water.

## Why this one is worth the effort

Kush is the clearest available test of whether a world history is actually a world history. It has monumental architecture, its own writing practice, a documented conquest of a more famous neighbour, a thousand-year afterlife and an excavated urban record — and it is still routinely left out.

The same problem shows up wherever a well-documented power overshadows its neighbours, which is why the argument in [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/) about choosing your question before choosing your answer applies here too. Ask when Egypt was conquered and Kush is an interruption. Ask what states existed on the Nile between 2000 BCE and 350 CE and it is one of the main answers.
