Malay pronouns are short words with a lot of social information in them. There is a polite "I" and a casual one, two words for "we" that English cannot tell apart, and more ways to say "you" than most learners expect, plus a habit of avoiding "you" altogether. This reference covers the common forms, the possessive endings and when to leave the pronoun out. Spellings are standard Malaysian Malay; Wikipedia's grammar article writes ê for the schwa sound, as in *mêreka*, which ordinary spelling writes *mereka*.

## The core set at a glance

| Person | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st, polite | saya | kami (excluding you) |
| 1st, familiar | aku | kami (excluding you) |
| 1st, inclusive | — | kita (including you) |
| 2nd, familiar | kamu | kalian |
| 2nd, polite | anda | anda sekalian, anda semua |
| 3rd | dia, ia | mereka (formal), dia orang (colloquial) |

This is the table of common pronouns in Wikipedia's Malay grammar article. The same article stresses that personal pronouns are a subset of nouns rather than a separate part of speech, and that they are frequently omitted.

## Saya or aku: choosing your "I"

**Saya** is the safe default. Wikipedia describes it as the form used with some family members, elders and new acquaintances and in formal settings. **Aku** is informal or casual, the "I" you use with friends, though the article adds that, depending on the speakers, aku with a new acquaintance is not necessarily taken as disrespectful.

Saya can also stand in for "we" when followed by *sekalian* or *semua*, "all", but that form does not say whether the listener is included. For rarer forms, Wikipedia lists *beta*, a royal speaking of themselves, and *patik*, a commoner addressing a royal, among others.

## Kami or kita: two words for "we"

Malay splits "we" in two:

- **kami**: us, but not you. "We are leaving" said to the person staying behind.
- **kita**: you and me, or us and you. "Shall we eat?" to the person you will eat with.

Wikipedia notes that this inclusive and exclusive distinction is increasingly confused in colloquial Indonesian but not in Malay, which is one of the small differences covered in [Malay vs Indonesian](/blog/malay-vs-indonesian-differences/).

## Anda, kamu, awak and the habit of not saying "you"

Wikipedia gives three common forms of "you": **anda**, polite; **kamu**, familiar; and **kalian**, "you all", slightly informal. Anda belongs to formal contexts such as advertisements and business, or to showing respect. Kamu is used in informal situations. **Engkau**, often shortened to *kau*, is used to social inferiors or equals, and **awak** to equals. **Encik**, contracted to *cik* before a name, is polite and traditionally used for people without a title.

A second-person pronoun is not always needed. Wikipedia notes that the person's name, a title, a title with a name, or an occupation is commonly used, and that kin terms, including ones for people who are not relatives, are extremely common. The effect is close to the kinship-based system in [Vietnamese pronouns](/blog/vietnamese-pronouns-explained/), though Malay also keeps its dedicated pronouns.

## He, she, they and the respectful beliau

The common third-person form is **ia**, with **dia** as its object and emphatic form; Wikipedia notes that, as a result, ia has recently been used for animals as well. **Beliau** is respectful, roughly "his or her honour", and **baginda** is used for royalty. For "they", **mereka** is the written form, while **dia orang**, contracted to *diorang*, is common in speech.

Wikipedia's Malaysian Malay article lists three newer colloquial plurals built with *orang*, "person": **korang** (*kau* + *orang*) for "you all", **kitorang** in place of *kami*, and **diorang** in place of *mereka*.

## A worked example: four ways to say "my table"

Only **aku, kamu, engkau** and **ia** have short possessive endings. Every other pronoun simply follows the noun, as Wikipedia shows with *meja*, "table":

1. **mejaku**: *meja* + *-ku* (from aku), "my table", casual.
2. **meja saya**: "my table", polite. Saya has no ending, so it follows the noun.
3. **mejamu**: *meja* + *-mu* (from kamu), "your table".
4. **mejanya**: *meja* + *-nya* (from ia), "his, her or their table".

The same pattern gives *meja kita*, "our table" including you, and *meja anda*, "your table" politely. Aku and engkau also have prefix forms, *ku-* and *kau-*, attached to a verb when the pronoun is not emphasised.

## Leaving the pronoun out

Because agent and object can both be omitted, a sentence may contain no pronoun at all. Wikipedia's example is a polite shop assistant asking **Boleh dibantu?**, literally "can be helped?", meaning "Can I help you?".
