These tips focus on three beginner mistakes: building too much too soon, laying roads that cannot grow, and putting services in the wrong place. These tips draw on how the genre works, using Wikipedia's articles on city-building games and on *SimCity*, the 1989 game that established the genre.

## Know what success looks like

Wikipedia notes that *SimCity* emphasised continuous building rather than a victory condition, and that its indicators of success were **a positive budget and citizen satisfaction**. Judge each decision by what it does to those two measures, and keep both in view.

## Tip 1: start small and compact

In a builder where roads and buildings cost money, a sprawling first town is expensive: every extra road tile adds cost, and distant buildings stretch services thin. Build a compact first neighbourhood, get it working, then expand. *SimCity*'s objective, as Wikipedia describes it, involved maintaining a balance between the different sectors, and that is easier at a small scale.

## Tip 2: lay roads you can extend

Roads are the skeleton of the town, and once buildings line them, moving a road means moving those buildings too. Lay them so they can continue in the directions you will grow. Where every building must touch a road, block depth matters:

| Block shape (lots) | Lots per block | Road share of land | Lots without road access |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 deep × 6 long | 12 | 43% | 0 |
| 4 × 4 | 16 | 36% | 4 |
| 6 × 6 | 36 | 27% | 16 |

**How the numbers work.** Put a one-tile road around each block, shared with its neighbours, so each block plus its road takes one extra row and column. A 4 × 4 block then occupies a 5 × 5 cell: 16 of the 25 tiles are lots, so 36% is road, but the 4 lots in the middle touch no road. Blocks two lots deep keep every lot on a road at the cost of more land for roads. Bigger blocks save road but strand lots in the middle.

## Tip 3: put services where they reach

Services decide who is served. Wikipedia describes two traditions in the genre. In *SimCity*-style games, services cover an area around them. In the historical builders that followed, such as *Caesar*, services were delivered by **walkers** moving along the streets, which made street layout, with its loops, gates and choke points, central to the game. Either way, place services near the homes they serve, and check coverage before building more housing.

## Tip 4: give residents what they ask for

In *SimCity*, Wikipedia explains, residents decide what to build and upgrade based on factors such as **traffic levels, electrical power, crime and proximity** to other buildings; housing next to a power plant, for example, seldom reaches its highest grade. The lesson carries across the genre. If an area will not grow, look for the missing need, or the unwelcome neighbour, before adding more of the same.

## Tip 5: build supply chains early

Some builders run on production chains. Wikipedia describes *The Settlers* as turning commerce into a watchable machine: woodcutter, then sawmill, then planks, then building. A chain takes time to fill, so start the next one before you need its output, and keep a little of each key material in reserve.

## Tip 6: grow in stages

Expand when the current district runs well, not when the budget first allows. A simple rhythm:

1. Build a district.
2. Connect and serve it.
3. Wait for the budget and satisfaction to settle.
4. Expand.

Wikipedia notes that the 2010s brought casual mobile city builders built around time-based "produce and upgrade" loops, in which waiting for production is part of the design, so pacing growth to your output is the natural way to play them.
