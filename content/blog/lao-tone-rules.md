The tone marks in Lao do not name tones. ◌່ (*mai ek*) is not "the falling tone mark"; it is one of four inputs into a calculation, and its output changes depending on what it is sitting on.

That single fact is the difference between a learner who can read Lao aloud and one who has memorised a chart and still guesses.

## Three inputs, one tone

A Lao syllable's tone is determined by the tone class of its initial consonant, working **in conjunction with** the four tone marks and the distinction between short and long vowels. Three inputs, combined.

Remove any one of them and the answer is indeterminate. That is why tone in Lao cannot be studied as a topic separate from reading: working out a syllable's tone *is* reading it.

## The three consonant classes, and why they exist

The Lao script has 27 consonant letters, 7 consonantal ligatures, 33 vowels and 4 tone marks. The 27 consonants are divided into three tone classes: high (ສູງ, *sung*), middle (ກາງ, *kang*) and low (ຕ່ຳ, *tam*).

Here is the detail that explains the whole design. Those 27 letters represent only **21 distinct consonant sounds**. The surplus is not redundancy and it is not historical clutter that nobody cleaned up. Two letters that write the same sound belong to different classes, and choosing one rather than the other puts the syllable into a different tone category.

Read that again if you have come from an alphabetic language, because it inverts the usual assumption. In English a letter's job is to tell you a sound. In Lao a letter tells you a sound *and* a tone class, and sometimes the tone class is the more informative half — it is the reason the letter was chosen.

The classes are historical residue. The distinction originally tracked voicing in the ancestral language; when the voicing distinction was lost, the tones it had conditioned remained, and the spelling preserved the old categories. The Thai script encodes the same history with the same three-way class system, which is one reason the two writing systems feel related even where the letters do not resemble each other. [Thai tones explained](/blog/thai-tones-explained/) works through the Thai version of the same machinery.

## The marks, and what they actually do

Lao has four tone marks. The two you meet constantly are *mai ek* (◌່) and *mai tho* (◌້).

Set out against the three classes in Vientiane Lao, for syllables with a long vowel and no final stop:

| Initial class | No mark | Mai ek ◌່ | Mai tho ◌້ |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | Low-rising | Middle | Low-falling (glottalised) |
| Middle | Low-rising | Middle | High-falling (glottalised) |
| Low | High-rising | Middle | High-falling |

Three things to take from that table.

**Mai ek collapses the distinction.** All three classes give a middle tone under *mai ek*. Where the mark is present, the class stops mattering — for that one mark.

**Mai tho does not.** A high class initial gives low-falling; middle and low give high-falling. Same mark, opposite direction, and the only thing that decided it was a property of the letter at the front of the syllable.

**The unmarked case is not the default case.** With no mark at all, high and middle class initials give low-rising while low class gives high-rising. An unmarked syllable is fully specified — it is not "neutral", it is carrying a tone determined by its class.

Anyone who has internalised "the mark tells you the tone" will be right about *mai ek*, wrong about *mai tho* two times out of three, and wrong about every unmarked syllable in the language. That is a lot of confident errors from one plausible assumption.

## Vowel length, the third input

Length matters most where there is no tone mark, and the standard descriptions of Vientiane Lao set the long-vowel and short-vowel cases out separately for exactly that reason.

On a high class initial, the long-vowel case gives low-falling and the short-vowel case gives mid-rising. On a middle class initial, long gives high-falling and short gives mid-rising. On a low class initial, long gives high-falling and short gives a middle tone.

So the same letter, the same absence of a mark, and a difference only in how long the vowel is, produces two different tones. Lao vowel length is contrastive anyway, so you are learning it regardless — this is simply another reason it cannot be treated as a fine detail to tidy up later. [The Lao alphabet explained](/blog/lao-alphabet-explained/) covers the vowel system and how the symbols attach.

## Which Lao, though?

The table above describes Vientiane Lao, which the written standard is based on and which television and radio broadcast from the capital.

Northern Lao — the speech of Luang Prabang — has **five** tones rather than six, along with other distinctive features. Luang Prabang was the original prestige variety, as the first capital of Lan Xang, and its speech remains quite distinct despite proximity to Vientiane.

Knowing this early saves a specific frustration: a learner who has drilled the six-tone system and then hears a Luang Prabang speaker will find the categories not quite landing, and will usually conclude their own ear is at fault. It is not.

## Why we teach class before marks

The tone material in [Learn Lao Language Fast & Easy](/apps/learn-lao/) covers **consonant classes before tone marks**. That is not the intuitive order — marks are visible, discrete and feel like the obvious place to start — and it is the order we arrived at after the intuitive one failed.

The failure was specific and it was worse than a simple mistake.

Teaching the marks first produces learners who are **confidently wrong**. A learner who has been given four marks and their tones has a rule that is complete-looking, easy to apply, and accurate often enough to keep being trusted. It works on *mai ek*. It half-works on *mai tho*. It fails silently on every unmarked syllable, which is most of them, because there is no mark present to trigger the rule and so nothing tells the learner that a decision was even required.

Silent failure is the expensive kind. A learner who knows they cannot read a word will ask. A learner who reads it wrong without hesitating will keep doing so, and the error hardens.

Teaching classes first inverts that. The learner's first model of a Lao syllable is "the initial consonant carries tone information", and marks arrive later as a modifier on a system that already exists. The rule they end up with is harder to state and it is correct on unmarked syllables, which is where the volume is.

The cost is that the first tone lessons are slower and less satisfying. Memorising which letters are high, middle and low class is dry work with no immediate payoff, and a learner cannot read anything new at the end of that session. We would rather pay that than ship a rule that breaks quietly.
