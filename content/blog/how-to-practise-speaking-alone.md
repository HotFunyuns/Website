How to practise speaking a language alone is a practical question for anyone without a regular conversation partner, and the honest answer has two halves. Some parts of speaking can be practised on your own, and there is research suggesting it is worth doing. Other parts need another person, and no solo routine replaces them. This guide sets out what the research says, with its limits, and then a concrete 20-minute session built from it. The examples use Italian, but nothing in the routine is specific to it.

## Why speaking to yourself counts as practice

The case for solo speaking rests mainly on one idea in second-language research: the **comprehensible output hypothesis**, developed by Merrill Swain. Wikipedia's summary says it holds that learning can take place when learners meet a gap in their knowledge of the language, notice it, and modify what they say. Swain describes three functions of output:

- **Noticing**: trying to speak shows you the gap between what you want to say and what you can say.
- **Hypothesis testing**: each sentence you produce tests a guess about the grammar, and feedback from a listener confirms or corrects it.
- **Metalinguistic**: reflecting on the language you produce helps you control and internalise it.

The first and third of these can happen alone. The second, as Swain frames it, needs an interlocutor.

The hypothesis is not settled. Wikipedia records **Stephen Krashen's** criticism that output is rare and comprehensible output rarer, and his argument that more comprehensible input is a better strategy than more output. It also notes that students in one survey placed speaking at the top of the list of what made them anxious in foreign-language learning, and that pushing learners to speak may make them uncomfortable and so hamper acquisition. Solo practice is one way to speak without an audience, but it is fair to say the field is divided on how much output matters.

## What research on private speech suggests

Speaking to yourself has a name in psychology: **private speech**, which Wikipedia defines as speech spoken to oneself, audible but neither intended for nor directed at others. It was first studied by Vygotsky and Piaget in children. Wikipedia notes that in a self-reported questionnaire young adults also reported high levels of private speech, particularly during tasks with cognitive, memory and attention components.

For second-language learners there is some direct evidence. A 2025 study by Jiang and colleagues in *Brain Sciences* asked **64 Chinese-speaking learners of English** to complete a **picture-description task**, and reported that **private speech facilitated their oral production**. In a second experiment, brain imaging of 32 learners who naturally used private speech found it produced connectivity patterns similar to inner speech. The authors suggest its role changes with proficiency, supporting basic language processing in lower-proficiency learners.

That is one study, with one task and one learner group, so it supports trying private speech rather than proving it works for everyone.

## Reading aloud and shadowing

Two more structured forms of solo speaking have been studied. A 2021 study by Takeuchi and colleagues in *Brain Imaging and Behavior* gave young adults **four weeks** of intensive training. Compared with training groups that did not speak (listening to compressed speech, or an active control task in the second language), the groups who practised **shadowing or reading aloud** in a second language showed a tendency towards greater increases in digit-span scores and significantly greater improvements in reaction time on a working-memory task. The study measured working memory, not conversational skill, so it is evidence that these exercises engage the system they are meant to, not that they make you fluent.

Wikipedia's article on speech shadowing describes it as a technique to practise speech in language learning, with tasks of increasing difficulty. How to do it without drilling errors is covered in [the shadowing technique explained](/blog/shadowing-technique-explained/).

## A 20-minute solo speaking session

Here is a session that puts each idea above to work. The times add up to 4 + 6 + 6 + 4 = 20 minutes, and five sessions a week come to 20 × 5 = 100 minutes.

1. **Read aloud (4 minutes).** Take a short text you understand, such as a dialogue from a lesson, and read it aloud at a steady pace. This is the reading-aloud condition from the Takeuchi study.
2. **Shadow (6 minutes).** Play a recording of the same dialogue and speak along with it, a fraction behind. Repeat until you can keep up.
3. **Describe a picture (6 minutes).** Pick a photo and describe it aloud without stopping: *C'è un uomo vicino alla finestra. Ha una tazza in mano…* This mirrors the picture-description task in the Jiang study. Whenever you cannot find a word, note it. That is the noticing function at work.
4. **Repair (4 minutes).** Look up the gaps you noted, then describe the picture again using them. This is the "modify your output" step in Swain's account.

The list of gaps from step 3 is the most useful thing the session produces: it tells you exactly what to study next.

## What solo practice cannot replace

Solo practice has a clear ceiling. In Swain's framework, the hypothesis-testing function depends on **feedback from an interlocutor**. Alone, you can produce a sentence, but nobody tells you whether it was understood or whether the grammar you guessed was right.

So treat solo speaking as preparation for conversation, not a replacement.
