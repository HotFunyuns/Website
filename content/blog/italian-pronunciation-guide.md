Italian pronunciation is easier to learn from the page than English, because the spelling tells you most of what to say. Wikipedia's article on Italian orthography calls written Italian "almost completely phonemic", meaning letters and sounds line up nearly one to one. The exceptions are few and worth knowing early: stress is not marked, the vowels e and o each hide two sounds, s and z can be voiced or not, and a handful of letter combinations change the sound of c and g. This guide works through them in that order. Sounds are shown in IPA between slashes where it helps.

## Twenty-one letters and five vowel signs

Wikipedia gives the base alphabet as **21 letters**: five vowels (a, e, i, o, u) and 16 consonants. J, k, w, x and y are not native and turn up in loanwords, foreign names and scientific terms such as *weekend*, *jeans* and *km*.

The five vowel letters carry seven vowel sounds. Only **a** has one value. **E** can be closed /e/ or open /ɛ/, and **o** can be closed /o/ or open /ɔ/, but only in stressed syllables; Wikipedia notes that unstressed syllables use the closed variants only. Spelling does not normally mark the difference, so *pesca* is both "peach" (open e) and "fishing" (closed e). Dictionaries show it with accents, and so do some teaching texts.

**I** and **u** usually become glides, the sounds /j/ and /w/, when they are unstressed and come before another vowel. Wikipedia lists many exceptions, such as *viaggio* and *fatuo*.

## Hard and soft c and g

This is the rule that matters most for reading aloud. Wikipedia sets it out like this: c and g are **hard** (/k/, /ɡ/) before a, o, u and h, and **soft** (/tʃ/ as in "church", /dʒ/ as in "jam") before e and i.

| Sound | Before e or i | Before a, o, u |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hard k | ch: *china* "ink" | c: *caramella* "sweet" |
| soft ch | c: *Cina* "China" | ci: *ciaramella* "shawm" |
| hard g | gh: *ghiro* "dormouse" | g: *gallo* "rooster" |
| soft j | g: *giro* "tour" | gi: *giallo* "yellow" |

So the h in *chi* keeps the c hard, and the i in *ciao* only keeps the c soft; it is not sounded as a separate vowel. Stress decides the difference: in *camicia* "shirt" the i is silent, while in *farmacia* "pharmacy" it is stressed and pronounced.

The same pattern covers **sc**: *sc* before e or i is /ʃ/ as in "shoe" (*scerno*), and *sci* does the same before other vowels (*scialo*). Before a, o, u, or with h, it is /sk/ (*scalo*, *scherno*).

## Gli, gn and the long sounds you cannot see

Two more combinations give sounds English lacks. **Gli** spells /ʎ/, a palatal l made with the middle of the tongue on the roof of the mouth. **Gn** spells /ɲ/, a palatal n like the middle of "canyon". Wikipedia notes both are pronounced long between vowels, as in *aglio* "garlic" and *ogni* "each", even though the spelling shows no double letter.

The other letters with quirks:

- **H** is always silent. It marks hard c and g, and separates *ho, hai, ha, hanno* ("I have" and so on) from *o, ai, a, anno*.
- **S** is voiceless at the start of a word before a vowel (*Sara*) and voiced before a voiced consonant (*sbranare*). Between vowels it varies by region; Wikipedia notes that in the north it is always voiced.
- **Z** is either /ts/ (*canzone*) or /dz/ (*zanzara*), depending on the word.
- **R** is a trill. Wikipedia notes that a single r between vowels has one or two contacts, which is why some descriptions call it a tap.

## Fato and fatto: why double letters change meaning

Italian has true **double consonants**, and they are not a spelling habit. Wikipedia compares *fato* "fate" with *fatto* "fact": the doubled t is held longer, and the vowel before it is shorter. It lists more pairs: *note/notte*, *sete/sette*, *papa/pappa*, *casa/cassa*, *caro/carro*.

Doubling also happens across word boundaries. Wikipedia calls this **syntactic gemination**: after words such as *a*, *da*, *che*, *tre* and *e*, and after a stressed final vowel, the next word's first consonant is lengthened. *Casa* has a single k, but *a casa* "home" is said as if written *accasa*. Wikipedia adds that this is normal in central and southern Italy and used inconsistently in the north.

## Where the stress falls

Stress is the one thing you cannot read off an unaccented word. Wikipedia's phonology article says Italian stress is lexical, so partly unpredictable word by word, with the **second-to-last syllable generally preferred**. *Ragazzo* /raˈɡattso/, *passato* /pasˈsato/ and *canzone* /kanˈtsone/ follow that pattern, while *zoccolo* /ˈtsɔkkolo/ is stressed on the third-to-last syllable.

The written accent helps at the end of a word. Wikipedia notes that an accent is generally compulsory only to show **stress on a final vowel**, as in *sanità*, *perché* and *morì*. The grave accent (à, è, ì, ò, ù) is the usual one, and the acute appears on é in words such as *perché*.

## Reading three words aloud

Take three words whose IPA Wikipedia gives: *occhi* "eyes", *giallo* "yellow" and *aglio* "garlic".

1. **occhi** /ˈɔkki/: *cch* is the trigraph for a double hard k before i, so hold the k. The o is stressed and open. Two syllables: OK-ki.
2. **giallo** /ˈdʒallo/: gi before a is soft, with no separate i sound, and *ll* is held long. Two syllables: JAL-lo.
3. **aglio** /ˈaʎʎo/: gli before o is the palatal l, long between vowels even though only one l is written. AL-lyo.

Count the sounds against the letters and the pattern shows: *giallo* has six letters but five sounds, /dʒ a l l o/, because *gi* spells one sound. The letter set is small, too: the 21 native letters plus the five loanword letters (j, k, w, x, y) make the familiar 26, and only the 21 follow the rules above.
