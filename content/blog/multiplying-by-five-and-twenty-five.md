Five is ten halved. Twenty-five is a hundred quartered. So **86 × 5 = 860 ÷ 2 = 430** and **48 × 25 = 4,800 ÷ 4 = 1,200**, and in both cases the multiplication has been replaced by a free shift and a division nobody finds difficult. This is the mirror image of the round-then-subtract family covered in [multiplying by 9, 99 and 999](/blog/multiplying-by-nine-and-ninety-nine/): instead of rounding *up* to a power of ten and taking something off, you go *up* to the power of ten and divide.

## The family

Each of these numbers is a power of ten divided by a small factor, and the table below gives the shift and the division that each one turns into.

| Multiplier | Written as | Procedure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 10 ÷ 2 | shift one place, halve |
| 25 | 100 ÷ 4 | shift two places, quarter |
| 50 | 100 ÷ 2 | shift two places, halve |
| 125 | 1,000 ÷ 8 | shift three places, divide by 8 |
| 625 | 10,000 ÷ 16 | shift four places, divide by 16 |

The table reads the same in practice as it does on the page:

- **23 × 5** = 230 ÷ 2 = **115**
- **248 × 5** = 2,480 ÷ 2 = **1,240**
- **36 × 25** = 3,600 ÷ 4 = **900**
- **86 × 50** = 8,600 ÷ 2 = **4,300**
- **48 × 125** = 48,000 ÷ 8 = **6,000**
- **32 × 625** = 320,000 ÷ 16 = **20,000**

Odd numbers are no obstacle, since halving an odd multiple of ten is still clean: **37 × 5** = 370 ÷ 2 = **185**, and **17 × 25** = 1,700 ÷ 4 = **425**.

## Choose which number to divide

Multiplication is associative, so the shift and the division can happen in either order, and either factor can absorb the division. Pick the arrangement where the division is easiest.

For **48 × 25**, quartering 48 first gives 12, and 12 × 100 = **1,200**. Quartering 4,800 gives the same answer with an extra digit to carry through the division. The small number was the better one to divide.

For **25 × 14**, halving 14 gives 7, and 7 × 50 = **350**. Alternatively 1,400 ÷ 4 = 350. Both work; the first avoids dividing a four-digit number by four.

For **86 × 5**, halving 86 gives 43 and 43 × 10 = **430** — quicker than halving 860, though only barely.

The rule of thumb: if the other factor divides cleanly by 2, 4 or 8, divide it first and then shift. If it does not, shift first and divide the round number.

This is the same flexibility that makes arrays such a useful picture of multiplication. As NRICH's article on illustrating number properties with arrays sets out, rearranging or partitioning the rectangle changes nothing about its area, which is why the order of operations here is free.

## The same identity runs backwards

Dividing by five is multiplying by two and shifting right, because 1 ÷ 5 = 2 ÷ 10.

- **340 ÷ 5** = 680 ÷ 10 = **68**
- **1,600 ÷ 25** = 6,400 ÷ 100 = **64**
- **4,500 ÷ 50** = 9,000 ÷ 100 = **90**
- **96 ÷ 125** = 768 ÷ 1,000 = **0.768**

That last one shows the general shape: multiply by the small factor, then shift by as many places as the power of ten. Division by awkward-looking divisors in general is covered in [how to divide in your head](/blog/mental-division-tricks/), which treats factor splitting and partial quotients for the cases this shortcut does not reach.

## Combinations worth knowing

Because 75 = 3 × 25, the quartering move handles it too: **75 × 16** = 3 × (16 ÷ 4) × 100 = 3 × 4 × 100 = **1,200**. Check it as 16 × 75 = 16 × 70 + 16 × 5 = 1,120 + 80 = 1,200.

Similarly 250 = 1,000 ÷ 4, so **250 × 36** = 36,000 ÷ 4 = **9,000**, and 500 = 1,000 ÷ 2, so **500 × 47** = 47,000 ÷ 2 = **23,500**.

Money makes the same move constantly. Twenty-four items at £2.50 is 24 × (10 ÷ 4) = 240 ÷ 4 = **£60**. Anything priced at a quarter, a half or an eighth of a round figure is a candidate.

## Where these numbers actually come up

Quarters and halves of a hundred are everywhere in practical arithmetic: 25% is a quarter, 50% is a half, 12.5% is an eighth. The percentage anchors in [percentages in your head](/blog/percentages-in-your-head/) are the same identities used in the other direction, and someone who can quarter a number quickly gets 25% discounts for free.

The other common home is unit conversion and pricing — quarter-pound weights, 25-count packs, prices ending in .25, .50 and .75. Whenever a factor is a familiar fraction of a round number, converting the multiplication into a division is the shortest route.

## The mistake to watch for

Doing the division and forgetting the shift. Halving 86 gives 43, and if the ten never arrives the answer is out by a factor of ten. The same slip at 25 costs a factor of a hundred.

The guard is the estimate you make before starting. 86 × 5 must be somewhere near 86 × 5 ≈ 450, so an answer of 43 fails immediately. Fixing the magnitude first is the habit that catches nearly all shift errors, and it is the central argument of [estimation techniques that make mental math useful](/blog/estimation-techniques-explained/).

A second, quieter error is dividing the wrong factor when neither divides cleanly. For 27 × 25 there is no clean quarter of 27, so shift first: 2,700 ÷ 4 = **675**. Trying to quarter 27 produces 6.75, which is correct but harder to carry.
