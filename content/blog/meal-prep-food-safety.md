Cooked meal-prep food keeps 3 to 4 days in the fridge, should be refrigerated within 2 hours of cooking, and should be reheated to 165°F, according to USDA guidance. Cooking once and eating over several days puts cooling, storage and reheating at the centre of the job. This guide takes the rules from USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) and FoodSafety.gov and turns them into a meal-prep routine.

## The rule behind every other rule

FSIS explains that bacteria grow most rapidly between **40°F and 140°F**, doubling in number in as little as **20 minutes**, a range it calls the **danger zone**. FSIS advises keeping hot food at 140°F or above and cold food at 40°F or below, so for meal prep the aim is to move cooked food through that range quickly.

## Step 1: cook it safely

Cook every component to its safe minimum internal temperature, checked with a food thermometer: 165°F for poultry and casseroles, 160°F for ground meat, 145°F for steaks, chops and fish, with a 3-minute rest for steaks, roasts and chops. [Safe cooking temperatures](/blog/safe-cooking-temperatures/) has the full chart.

## Step 2: cool it fast

FSIS names improper cooling of cooked food as one of the most common causes of foodborne illness. Its cooling guidance:

- **Refrigerate within 2 hours** of cooking, or within **1 hour** if the temperature is above 90°F.
- **Divide large amounts into shallow containers.** A big pot of soup takes a long time to cool, inviting bacteria to multiply.
- **Cut large items** such as roasts into smaller portions.
- **No need to wait for food to cool**: hot food can go straight into the fridge, or be chilled in an ice or cold-water bath first.
- **Cover or seal** containers to keep bacteria out and moisture in.

## Step 3: store it within the window

Cooked leftovers keep **3 to 4 days** in a fridge at 40°F or below, and **3 to 4 months** in a freezer at 0°F for best quality. FoodSafety.gov's chart gives similar windows for cooked meat and poultry, soups and stews, and casseroles. [Food storage times](/blog/food-storage-times/) lists more foods.

**A worked example: the five-day lunch problem.** You cook five lunches on Sunday evening. Counting Monday as day one, the fridge window of 3 to 4 days covers Monday to Wednesday safely and Thursday at the outside. Friday's lunch is beyond it. Two fixes:

1. **Freeze the late portions on Sunday.** Move Thursday's and Friday's containers to the freezer as soon as they are cool, and thaw them in the fridge the night before.
2. **Cook twice.** Prep Monday to Wednesday on Sunday, and Thursday and Friday on Wednesday evening.

## Step 4: thaw it safely

FSIS lists three safe ways to thaw leftovers: in the **refrigerator**, which is slowest but keeps food safe throughout; in **cold water**, in a leak-proof bag; or in the **microwave**, heating straight on to 165°F. Food thawed in the fridge should be eaten within 3 to 4 days or can be refrozen; food thawed in cold water should be cooked before refreezing. FSIS adds that frozen leftovers can also be reheated without thawing, which simply takes longer.

## Step 5: reheat it properly

Reheat leftovers to **165°F**, measured with a food thermometer, and bring sauces, soups and gravies to a **rolling boil**. In a microwave, FSIS advises covering and rotating the food for even heating, arranging it evenly in a covered microwave-safe dish, adding liquid if needed and venting the lid, because microwaves have cold spots.

## Containers and labels

FSIS recommends covering leftovers, wrapping them in airtight packaging or sealing them in storage containers, which it says helps keep bacteria out, retain moisture and stop food picking up odours from the rest of the fridge, and then refrigerating or freezing the wrapped food immediately. Add one habit of your own: write the cooking date on every container. With five near-identical boxes in the fridge, a date shows at a glance which one is on day four.

## Keeping lunches cold on the move

FSIS's danger-zone guidance is to keep cold food at **40°F or below** and never to leave food out of refrigeration for more than 2 hours, or 1 hour above 90°F. A packed lunch sitting at room temperature is on that same clock, so it needs to stay cold until you eat it.
