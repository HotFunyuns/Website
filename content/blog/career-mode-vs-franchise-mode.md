Two sports games sit next to each other on a store page. Both promise seasons, drafts, contracts and championships. One of them will absorb you for months and the other will bore you inside an hour, and which is which depends entirely on a question the store page never asks: **do you want to be a person, or an institution?**

That is the whole distinction. Everything else — session length, decision density, what failure feels like — falls out of it.

## The camera position

A **career mode** puts the camera behind one player. You create them, develop them, and answer for them. The game's questions are personal: take this shot or move it, sign here or hold out, spend this off-season on your weakness or on your strength.

A **franchise mode** raises the camera above an organisation. You are not any of the people on the field. The game's questions are structural: which player do you take with this pick, can you afford this contract, is this rotation getting the minutes to the right places.

Both are legitimately "sports management". They are managing different things.

## What you actually decide

The number of decisions differs by an order of magnitude, and so does their weight.

A franchise season hands you dozens of small calls, most of them recoverable. A bad signing can be traded. A poor draft pick is one of several. The mode is forgiving in the specific sense that there is nearly always another lever.

A career season hands you a handful of decisions that cannot be undone. You cannot trade away a reputation. You cannot restructure a relationship with a coach the way you restructure a contract. Attribute development compounds, so a choice made early keeps paying — or keeps costing — for seasons.

That difference produces two very different feelings. Franchise play feels like tuning. Career play feels like committing.

## What failure looks like

Ask what going badly wrong actually means in each, and the modes separate cleanly.

- **Franchise failure** is a badly constructed team: an unbalanced roster, a cap situation you cannot escape, a season that ends early. It is legible. You can point at the decisions that caused it, and there is a next draft.
- **Career failure** is more personal and less legible: a player who never became anything in particular. Nothing dramatic happened. The opportunities just narrowed, quietly, over a long time.

People who like diagnosing systems tend to prefer the first. People who like consequences tend to prefer the second.

## How time behaves

A **career has an arc.** It starts somewhere and ends somewhere, and the shape of that arc is the content. The mental shift halfway through — from *improving* to *accumulating*, from becoming good to leaving a record — is a real part of playing one well.

A **franchise is open-ended.** There is no natural stopping point, and the interesting tension is that the roster which maximises this season and the roster that leaves you flexible next season are frequently not the same. Deciding which one you are building for, before the draft board tempts you, is the discipline the mode asks for.

This has a practical consequence people underestimate. A career abandoned partway through has shown you a fraction of what it is; a franchise abandoned after two seasons has shown you most of the loop.

## Session shape

Franchise work is naturally chunked. A draft, a set of contracts, a rotation change — each is a task with an end, and you can put the phone down between them without losing the thread.

Career decisions are less separable. They arrive in sequence and each one is context for the next, so the mode reads better in slightly longer sittings. The exception is games that hand you individual moments rather than whole matches: when the unit of play is one possession, a career mode chunks about as well as a franchise does.

| | Career mode | Franchise mode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You are | One player | The organisation |
| Decisions per session | Few, heavy | Many, light |
| Reversibility | Low | High |
| Time structure | An arc with an ending | Open-ended |
| Failure state | A player who never became anything | A badly built team |
| Natural session | Longer, sequential | Chunked, interruptible |
| Replay comes from | A different kind of player | A different construction problem |

## Where these two shapes show up

The studio publishes both patterns, which makes them convenient examples of the split.

### The career shape

[Basketball Career Sim](/apps/pro-basketball-my-career-sim/) is the career pattern in its clearest form. You create a player — position, build, jersey number, playing style, archetype and attributes — and run through youth basketball, high school, recruiting and college, the draft, and the professional years.

Its Live Sim system is what makes the mode work in short bursts: rather than playing whole matches, you step into key possessions and choose what your player does — take the shot or move it on, defend the matchup alone or call for help, play safe or take the risk — with the court view showing score, stats, momentum and positioning as the choice resolves.

Around that sit the systems that make a career a career: coach trust, team chemistry, rivals, teammates, media and fans all react to what you do and change the opportunities you are offered, while training camp, attribute upgrades, signature moves, contracts, brand deals and lifestyle purchases handle off-court progression. All of the money involved is fictional in-game currency with no real-world value. The game supports offline play and needs no account. The [career sim guide](/blog/basketball-career-sim-guide/) goes into how to play one well.

### The franchise shape

[Basketball Draft GM Franchise](/apps/pro-basketball-draft-gm-mode/) is the other pattern. Every run begins on the draft board, the roster then plays a full 82-game season and a playoff race, and contracts, trades and rotations are yours. Eight draft formats — Unlimited, Quick, Blind, Franchise and Salary Cap drafts, plus VS Computer, Battle Mode and a Daily Challenge — change the constraints, so a roster that dominates one format can fall apart in another.

The same shape recurs across the other sports with different vocabulary. Football has Franchise Mode and GM Mode extending control past draft day to the wider organisation. Hockey has Franchise GM Mode for organising forwards, defenders and goaltending across seasons. Baseball has Franchise GM Mode covering the batting order, rotation, bullpen and payroll. Soccer calls it club management and includes formations, squad depth, transfers, contracts, fitness and season progress. Combat sports has GM Mode, where you sign fighters, manage contracts and move contenders toward title shots with your decisions feeding the rankings.

## The third shape nobody names

There is a pattern between the two that gets filed under franchise and is really its own thing: **draft-and-simulate**.

You build a roster, hand it to a simulator, and get a record back. That is a complete session — problem, decision, verdict — usually in a few minutes, with no season to administer. It is the shortest closed loop in the genre, which is why it survives on a phone better than either of the other two.

Most of the studio's sports titles are built on this loop with a franchise mode attached behind it, which is a sensible arrangement: the draft is the entry point and the management is the depth. The [basketball draft and GM guide](/blog/basketball-draft-gm-guide/) covers how the two connect.

## What both kinds of simulation are assuming

Whichever shape you play, the model underneath deserves stating plainly.

These games take fictional players, apply internal rules to your decisions, and return an outcome. They are not reproducing the physics of the sport, and they are not describing, forecasting or relating to any real league, team, school or athlete. Nothing they produce is a prediction or guidance of any kind, and no approach guarantees a result — the perfect records these games are named for are deliberately hard to reach rather than the expected outcome of competent play.

What that leaves is a comparison against yourself. In a franchise, change one construction decision and see whether the model agrees. In a career, commit to an identity and see how far the surrounding systems let it go. Both are honest uses of a model; neither tells you anything about the real sport.

## Which one do you want?

Three questions settle it faster than any feature list.

1. **When something goes wrong, do you want to fix it or live with it?** Fixing is franchise. Living with it is career.
2. **Do you enjoy a spreadsheet-shaped problem, or a story-shaped one?** The first has a right answer you are searching for; the second has an identity you are defending.
3. **How long are your sessions really?** If the honest answer is a few minutes at a time, start with a draft-and-sim loop or a career built on individual possessions rather than a full-season franchise.

Getting this choice wrong is the most common reason people conclude they do not like sports sims. Usually they just tried the wrong camera position.
