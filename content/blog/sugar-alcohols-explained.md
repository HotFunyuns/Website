**Sugar alcohols are carbohydrates used as sweeteners, and despite the name they are not alcohol in the drinking sense.** The technical term is polyols, and the practical signal on an ingredient list is a name ending in **-itol**: erythritol, xylitol, maltitol, sorbitol, isomalt.

## Where they appear on a label

Sugar alcohols are counted inside **Total Carbohydrate** on a Nutrition Facts label. Some labels also break them out on a separate "Sugar Alcohol" line.

That is the source of a persistent confusion. A package can say "sugar free" — which relates to sugars specifically — while containing a substantial amount of carbohydrate in the form of polyols. The two claims are not in conflict; they are about different things.

The [FDA's guide to reading the Nutrition Facts label](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/how-understand-and-use-nutrition-facts-label) is the reference for how these lines relate, and [how to read nutrition labels](/blog/reading-nutrition-labels/) covers the full label in practical terms.

## Why they are not interchangeable

The most useful thing to know about polyols is that **they differ substantially from one another**, and treating them as one category is where people get caught out.

The main dimension of difference is how completely each is absorbed in the small intestine. That single property drives most of the practical differences:

| Property | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| More completely absorbed | Fewer digestive effects, more carbohydrate reaching the body |
| Less completely absorbed | More reaches the large intestine, more potential for digestive effects |

Published reviews of gastrointestinal effects discuss the polyols individually rather than as a group — the [Int J Dent review of gastrointestinal disturbances associated with sugar alcohols](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27840639/) is an example. The fact that the literature separates them is itself the useful signal.

## Why they can cause digestive upset

The mechanism is straightforward. Whatever is not absorbed in the small intestine continues into the large intestine, where it draws water in and is fermented by gut bacteria. That combination produces the effects some people report.

Two practical implications:

**Dose matters.** Effects reported at higher intakes may not occur at lower ones.

**Individuals differ.** Tolerance varies between people, and a polyol that causes one person no trouble at all can affect another. Your own experience is more informative than a general statement.

Anyone with an existing gastrointestinal condition should treat this as a topic for their clinician rather than for experimentation.

## The net carb question

This is where low-carb eaters most want an answer, and the honest answer is that there is not a single authoritative one.

**Net carbs is a convention, not a regulatory definition.** Different sources subtract different things:

- Some subtract fibre only.
- Some subtract fibre plus all sugar alcohols.
- Some subtract fibre plus a proportion of sugar alcohols, varying by which polyol.

That is why two apps, or an app and a product label, can report different net carb figures for the same food. Neither is lying; they are applying different conventions.

[Net carbs explained](/blog/net-carbs-explained-keto-tracking/) covers the arithmetic and its limits in detail, and [keto macros explained](/blog/keto-macros-explained/) covers where net carbs sit in a wider low-carb plan.

**What we would suggest practically:** pick one convention and apply it consistently, so your own numbers are comparable to each other over time. Comparing today's figure computed one way against last month's computed another way tells you nothing.

## What a label will and will not tell you

**It will tell you:** total carbohydrate, and often the sugar alcohol total.

**It will not tell you:** which specific polyol, unless you read the ingredient list; how you personally will tolerate it; or what net carb figure any particular diet would assign.

The ingredient list is where the specific polyol name appears, which is why it matters more here than for most nutrients.
