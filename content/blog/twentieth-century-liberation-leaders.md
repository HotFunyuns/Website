Put the twentieth century's independence leaders in one room and the interesting thing is not what they had in common but how sharply they disagreed about method. Mass non-cooperation, party-building inside a colonial constitution, protracted armed struggle, and pressure applied through international institutions are four different theories of how power is taken from an occupying state, and the people who chose between them knew they were choosing.

The scale involved is easy to underestimate. The United Nations records that when it was founded in 1945, some 750 million people — nearly a third of the world's population — lived in territories dependent on colonial powers. It now lists seventeen Non-Self-Governing Territories with fewer than two million inhabitants between them. The U.S. State Department's summary counts three dozen new states in Asia and Africa achieving autonomy or independence between 1945 and 1960 alone, and is careful to say that there was no single process: in some places the transfer was peaceful and orderly, in many others independence came only after prolonged war.

## Mass non-cooperation: the Indian model

Gandhi's contribution was a theory of leverage. Colonial administration in India rested on Indian participation — in courts, schools, councils, the revenue system and the purchase of British goods. Withdraw that participation at scale and the machinery stops without anyone firing a shot.

The tactics followed from the theory: the non-cooperation campaign of the early 1920s, the salt march of 1930 against the government monopoly on salt, and the Quit India movement of 1942. His own account of the reasoning runs through the enormous body of writing collected in *The Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi*, which is available through the Gandhi Heritage Portal and is the place to go if you want the arguments rather than the summaries.

Three qualifications belong here rather than in a footnote. First, non-cooperation was not the whole Indian movement; Congress politics, Jinnah's Muslim League, Ambedkar's campaign for Dalit rights and armed revolutionary groups all operated at the same time with different aims. Second, the colonial state's violence is part of the record: the Amritsar massacre of 1919, in which troops fired on an enclosed crowd, shaped everything afterwards. Third, Gandhi's own record is contested in India and beyond, particularly his early writings in South Africa about Black Africans and his position on caste, and readers encounter those debates fairly quickly.

Independence in 1947 came with partition, and the human cost has never been reliably counted. Published estimates of the dead range from several hundred thousand to around two million, and of those displaced from roughly ten to twenty million. Those ranges are wide because no authoritative count was ever made, and any single figure quoted without a range is being invented somewhere.

## Party-building: the Gold Coast route

Kwame Nkrumah, born in 1909 in the Nzema region of the Gold Coast, spent a decade studying in the United States, where he was active in Pan-Africanist student organising before returning home. His Convention People's Party pursued a different route from Gandhi's: it contested the elections the colonial constitution allowed, won them, and used the resulting offices as a platform while continuing mass agitation outside them.

It worked quickly. On 6 March 1957 the Gold Coast was proclaimed independent from the British Empire and took the name Ghana, and the United States recognised it the same day. Nkrumah became the first leader of the first sub-Saharan African colony to achieve independence from European rule, which gave him a continental standing far beyond Ghana's size.

The same record shows what followed, and it should not be edited out. His government's development programme depended heavily on cocoa revenue and international borrowing, and when production targets were not met the state took a coercive role in the industry. He concentrated power, and was removed by a coup in 1966. Both halves are part of the history of Ghanaian independence.

## Armed struggle: the harder cases

Vietnam and Algeria followed the fourth pattern, and it is the one where sanitised accounts break down fastest.

Ho Chi Minh declared Vietnamese independence in 1945, and the French return produced a war that ran to 1954. In Algeria, the National Liberation Front's war against France ran from 1954 to 1962. French forces used torture systematically, a fact that was documented at the time, denied for decades and eventually acknowledged by the French state. Casualty estimates for the Algerian war are contested between French and Algerian historiography, and the Algerian state's traditional figure of a million dead is substantially higher than most French scholarly estimates. That disagreement is itself a historical fact worth reporting rather than resolving.

South Africa arrived at armed struggle later and by a clearly documented route. The African National Congress operated legally for decades; Nelson Mandela, born in 1918 in the Eastern Cape, joined it in 1944 and helped to form its Youth League. The turn came after the Sharpeville killings of March 1960 and the banning of the organisation that followed — the period that South African History Online's account of the Rivonia Trial identifies as the watershed. Mandela received military training from the Algerian FLN in 1962, a direct line between two movements usually taught separately. At the Rivonia Trial, eight of the accused were sentenced to life imprisonment.

The honest formulation is this: armed struggle followed the closure of every other channel, and saying so is a causal claim supported by the sequence of events, not an excuse offered after the fact.

## Diplomacy as a fourth front

The strategy that gets least attention is the international one. In April 1955 representatives of twenty-nine Asian and African governments met at Bandung in Indonesia to discuss decolonisation, development and their position between the Cold War blocs. It was co-sponsored by Burma, India, Indonesia, Pakistan and Sri Lanka, and — a detail worth pausing on — the delegates deliberately spoke on behalf of colonised peoples who had no government to send a delegation.

The closing communique committed the participants to self-determination, an end to racial discrimination, and economic and cultural cooperation, and it laid the foundation for the Non-Aligned Movement. The United Nations itself became an arena: newly independent states voted, and as their numbers grew the institution's centre of gravity moved.

That is a strategy in the same sense the others are. It delivered no territory directly, and it changed what the imperial powers could do without cost.

## What comparison actually shows

Set side by side, several patterns come into focus that are invisible inside any one national story.

The method chosen tracks closely with what the colonial state permitted. Where elections and legal parties existed, movements used them. Where organisations were banned and demonstrations fired on, armed wings appeared. That is not a claim about national character; it is a claim about incentives.

The leaders knew each other's work. Pan-Africanist networks connected Accra, London, Harlem and Johannesburg; Bandung put Asian and African governments in one room; the ANC trained with the FLN. Treating each movement as a self-contained national awakening misses the traffic between them.

Claims about what any single leader personally decided also need the same scrutiny as any other biographical claim, which is the subject of [how we know what historical figures actually did](/blog/how-we-know-what-historical-figures-did/); movement records, state archives and memoirs written decades later do not agree with one another.

And governing turned out to be a different problem from winning. Several of these leaders presided over one-party states, detention without trial or economic programmes that failed. Those records are real and should be stated. They also do not retroactively justify colonial rule, which is what the argument is usually being recruited to do. Two separate questions — was the colonial system defensible, and did a particular successor government govern well — deserve two separate answers.

## The short version

One century, several hundred million people, and at least four serious theories of how to end an empire. The leaders were not interchangeable symbols of a single moral position; they were strategists who read each other, disagreed, borrowed, and in some cases trained together. Read them that way and the twentieth century's biggest political change stops being a series of anniversaries and becomes an argument you can follow.
