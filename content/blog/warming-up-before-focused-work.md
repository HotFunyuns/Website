A five-minute drill before focused work is a **start ritual**, not a cognitive enhancer. It does not sharpen you, prime you, or make the next hour more productive by any mechanism anyone has demonstrated. What it does is get you seated, with the screen open, doing something structured, at the time you said you would start — and for most people that is the actual obstacle.

That distinction is worth making before the routine, because it changes what a good warm-up looks like. If the goal were sharpening, you would want it hard. Since the goal is starting, you want it easy, short and impossible to fail.

## The problem a warm-up actually solves

Work you have planned but not begun tends to fail at a specific moment: the transition. Research on academic procrastination using experience sampling — asking students in real time rather than afterwards — found that whether a planned study session was delayed was predicted by within-person changes in how the task was appraised at the moment of truth. When perceived unpleasantness rose between planning and starting, the risk of delay roughly doubled; when the task's personal importance held up, delay became much less likely. Notably, general procrastination tendency measured by questionnaire did not predict actual task-specific delay.

That finding is more useful than it looks. It says the enemy is not a character trait. It is a small, local, right-now appraisal of the next action — and a small, local appraisal is something a routine can change, because you can make the next action smaller.

A five-minute drill is a way of putting something trivially startable between you and the thing that looks unpleasant. You are not tricking yourself into working. You are removing the bit of the decision you keep losing.

## What the five minutes should contain

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and the three components below exist in it. The shape is what matters, not the product — a deck of flash cards would do the same job.

**One 30-Second Lightning Round.** Thirty seconds of arithmetic under a clock, and it is over before you can renegotiate. Its whole function is to be the first completed thing of the session.

**One ten-question Accuracy Trainer set.** No timer, feedback after each answer, an explanation available. This is the part that occupies attention properly rather than just filling time. Ten questions with no clock takes two or three minutes and ends by itself.

**One technique mini quiz.** Read one method from the technique library — steps, worked example, the common mistake it names — and answer its two-question quiz. It closes the warm-up on something that felt like learning rather than repetition, which makes the next day's warm-up easier to start.

Total: about five minutes, with three natural stopping points and no possibility of an open-ended session.

## The rules that keep it a warm-up

**Set the stop before you start.** A finite, well-scored, mildly rewarding activity is a very easy thing to keep doing instead of the ambiguous, unscored work you were supposed to begin. Decide on three components, then do three components.

**Keep it below your practice difficulty.** This is the one place in a routine where you do not want a stretch. A warm-up that can go badly is a warm-up that can become a reason to stop. Everywhere else, the argument in [choosing a difficulty level](/blog/choosing-a-difficulty-level/) applies; here, deliberately, it does not.

**Do not merge it with your real practice session.** They have different goals and want different settings. If you also want a proper session, run it at a different point in the day — the ten-minute route in [brain training for adults with ten spare minutes](/blog/brain-training-for-busy-adults/) is built for exactly that slot, and it is deliberately harder than this one.

**Same three things, same order, every time.** The value is in not deciding. A warm-up you have to design each morning is another decision standing between you and starting, which is the thing you were trying to remove.

## What a warm-up is not

It is not evidence about your cognition, and the numbers it produces are not worth reading. A thirty-second round played cold, before the work you actually care about, is the least representative session you will play all week. Do not record it as a personal best attempt, and do not treat a bad one as information.

It is also not a substitute for the scheduling that makes study work. The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide on organising instruction puts spacing learning over time among its recommendations, and The Learning Scientists make the same case for distributing study across weeks rather than concentrating it before a deadline. A warm-up helps you begin a session; it does nothing about whether your sessions are spread out sensibly. The habit layer underneath both is covered in [building a daily practice habit](/blog/building-a-daily-practice-habit/).

And it makes no claim about intelligence. Practice, entertainment, educational activity and standardised psychological assessment are four separate things, and a warm-up drill is the first three at most.
