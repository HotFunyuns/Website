You are 5-5 in a qualifying final, serving at 30-40. Win the point and you are still in it. Lose it and the week is over before the main draw starts, and the trip home costs you a fortnight of preparation you cannot get back.

That is the unit a tennis career game is built out of: not a rally, but a decision at a moment when the decision is worth something. A career simulator's job is to keep handing you those moments and to make the consequences stack up over years.

## What you actually control

The honest answer to "do you play the points yourself" is: some of them, and deliberately not all of them.

[Tennis Career Simulator 2026](/apps/tennis-career-sim/) resolves matches at the points that matter. On those you decide how to serve, how to return, when to defend and when to attack, and what to do under pressure. Your attributes, your style and your preparation decide which options are on the table in the first place — a tired player at the end of a long week is simply not offered the aggressive line that a fresh one is.

Everything else resolves without you. A love hold at 3-1 in the first set is not a decision; it is bookkeeping.

That cut-off is the biggest design choice in the game and the one most worth explaining, because it is where a tennis sim either works or turns into a chore. A professional match runs to a few hundred points. Handing a player every one of them produces a game that is exhausting by the third round of the first tournament; handing them none produces a spectator. The line we drew is: if the point can change the set, you get it. If it cannot, the model takes it.

The cost of that choice is real and worth naming. You do not get to grind out a five-set epic point by point. If that is the experience you want, this is the wrong game. What you get instead is a career you can actually finish.

## The six styles, and why they are not a difficulty setting

At creation you set the portrait, the dominant hand and the backhand, and then you commit to one of six playing styles: aggressive baseliner, counterpuncher, all-court player, serve-and-volley specialist, big server or tactical shotmaker.

The important thing is that these are not attribute presets with different starting numbers. They change the menu. Faced with the same second serve on the same surface, a serve-and-volley specialist is offered a forward option that a counterpuncher is not, and a counterpuncher is offered a depth-and-patience line that the big server has no business attempting.

That makes style a genuine replay hook rather than a cosmetic one. It also makes it a commitment: you cannot quietly become a different player in season four because the one you picked is having a hard time on clay. [Tennis playing styles](/blog/tennis-playing-styles/) works through what each of the six is good at and where each gets exposed, which is worth reading before you lock one in.

## The junior circuit is a filter, not a tutorial

Careers open on the junior circuit, move through academy opportunities, and then into qualifiers, tournament draws and championship events.

It is tempting to treat the junior stage as a warm-up you click through. It is not. Two things are being decided there that you cannot easily revisit: which attributes you have trained, and which academy offers you are in a position to accept. A junior season spent chasing results in events that suit your existing strengths produces a player who is good at exactly one thing. A junior season spent building the attribute your style is weakest in produces a worse junior record and a better professional.

Neither is wrong. But the game does not tell you which one you picked until about two seasons later, which is the sort of delayed feedback a career game ought to have and a match game cannot.

## The schedule is the actual game

Here is the mechanic players notice first, usually with some irritation: ranking points expire.

They do not accumulate forever. They sit on a rolling window and then drop off, which mirrors how professional tennis rankings genuinely work — the ATP rankings are updated weekly, and points are dropped 52 weeks after they were awarded. The consequence inside a career is that standing still costs you something. A season where you play sparingly does not show up immediately; it shows up months later, when last year's good results expire and nothing has replaced them.

Once that is true, a schedule stops being a menu. Every week you are choosing between defending a result you already hold and chasing a new one, and those two goals compete for the same body. Add surface preparation, which rewards committing to a run of events on one surface, and you have a planning problem with no clean answer.

If you want the mechanics in detail, [how tennis ranking points work](/blog/how-tennis-ranking-points-work/) covers the real-world system, and [ranking decay and schedule planning](/blog/tennis-ranking-decay-and-scheduling/) is the design account of what we implemented and what we deliberately left out.

## What a career carries that a match does not

Around the tennis sits the rest of it: coaches and support staff, academy offers, rivals, sponsorships, and training aimed at the surface in front of you. Results, form, records and career history accumulate into one continuing story rather than resetting each time you open the app.

That accumulation is the point of the genre, and it is also where individual sports differ most from team ones. In a draft or franchise game, a bad season is a personnel problem — you can trade, you can draft, you can change the coach. In tennis there is one player. A slump is not something you solve by swapping someone out; it is something you schedule around, train through, or accept.

[How player career modes work](/blog/how-player-career-modes-work/) covers that distinction across sports. If you are weighing tennis against the other individual-sport career on the shelf, [Golf Career Simulator Pro Tour](/apps/golf-career-simulator/) runs on a similar principle — decisions instead of a swing meter — with a cut and a tour card doing the job that ranking decay does here.

## Who this is for, plainly

It suits people who like long arcs with consequences, who would rather make a tactical choice than time a swipe, and who enjoy replaying a career as a completely different kind of player.

It suits you less if you want to hit the ball yourself, or if you want an arcade match you can finish in ninety seconds.
