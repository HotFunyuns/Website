A tennis tiebreak is a single game played when a set reaches six games all. Under the 2026 ITF Rules of Tennis (Rule 5), the first player to win seven points takes the tiebreak and the set, but only with a two-point margin: 7-5 ends it, 7-6 does not, and play continues for as long as it takes. The player due to serve takes the first point, the opponent serves the next two, and from then on each player serves two points in a row. Players change ends after every six points, and the set is recorded as 7-6.

That is the 7-point tiebreak most matches use. There are also 10-point versions, one that replaces an entire deciding set and one played at 6-6 in the final set of a Grand Slam match, plus shortened formats the ITF approves as alternatives. All of them are defined in Rule 5 and Appendix VI of the ITF rules, which is where everything below comes from unless another source is named. The rule numbers are from the 2026 edition.

## Every tiebreak format on one table

| Format | When it starts | Target | Where it is used in 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Standard tiebreak | 6-6 in a "tie-break set" | 7 points, win by 2 | Every set of singles at ATP Tour, ATP Challenger and WTA events |
| Match tiebreak (10 points) | Instead of the deciding set, at one set all or two sets all | 10 points, win by 2 | ATP Tour and Challenger doubles, WTA main-draw doubles |
| Match tiebreak (7 points) | Instead of the deciding set | 7 points, win by 2 | Approved alternative |
| Final-set tiebreak (10 points) | 6-6 in the final set | 10 points, win by 2 | The four Grand Slams |
| Short-set tiebreak (5 points) | In short sets only | 5 points, deciding point at 4-4 | Approved alternative for short formats |

The ATP's 2026 rulebook sets singles at best of three tie-break sets and doubles at two tie-break sets with a 10-point match tiebreak at one set all, all games no-ad. The WTA's 2026 rulebook has the same singles format, and main-draw doubles of two no-ad sets followed by a 10-point match tiebreak as the third set. The USTA describes best-of-three tie-break sets as the most common format in the game generally.

Rule 6 adds one requirement that catches out club organisers: an event using tie-break sets must announce in advance whether the final set will be a tie-break set or an advantage set, which is played on until someone leads by two games.

## Who serves when

### The one-two-two pattern

Rule 5(b) sets the order. The player whose turn it is to serve takes point one. That is the player who received in the twelfth game, because service alternates every game. The opponent serves points two and three. After that, each player serves two consecutive points until the tiebreak ends. In doubles, each team keeps the rotation it used during the set.

Rule 17 adds the court half: the first tiebreak point is served from the right half (the deuce court), and every point after that alternates halves. Put the two rules together and you get this:

| Point | Server | Served from | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A | Right | |
| 2 | B | Left | |
| 3 | B | Right | |
| 4 | A | Left | |
| 5 | A | Right | |
| 6 | B | Left | Change ends after this point |
| 7 | B | Right | |
| 8 | A | Left | |
| 9 | A | Right | |
| 10 | B | Left | |
| 11 | B | Right | |
| 12 | A | Left | Change ends after this point |
| 13 | A | Right | |

After the first point, every two-point turn starts from the left and finishes on the right.

### Why the order is fair

Count the serves in that table. After two points, A and B have served once each. After four, twice each. After six, three times each. The pattern holds at every even number of points, and a level score always means an even number of points has been played. So at 3-3 or 6-6, neither player has had an extra serve. The one-two-two order exists so that the serve advantage never sits with one player for long.

### Changing ends, and the set that follows

Rule 10 has players change ends after every six points. That means after the sixth, twelfth and eighteenth points in total, not when either player reaches six. Rule 29 says play is continuous during a tiebreak, so the change happens without a seat. Appendix VI approves one alternative, changing after the first point and then after every four.

When the tiebreak ends, the player who served its first point receives in the first game of the next set. A 7-6 set has an odd number of games, so the players also change ends before that set begins, as the USTA's scoring guide points out.

## Why nobody wins a tiebreak on serve alone

Suppose both players win every point on their own serve. Play it through with the table above. A wins point one, B wins two and three, A wins four and five, and the score goes 1-0, 1-1, 1-2, 2-2, 3-2, 3-3. It never opens up by more than one point, so it can never finish.

Every completed tiebreak therefore includes at least one point won by the receiver, which is what players call a mini-break. That is why a single mini-break feels decisive at 5-5 and why commentators count them: in a format where the serve order is built to be even, the return points are where the margin comes from.

## The 10-point match tiebreak

Appendix VI allows a match to be decided by one tiebreak game instead of a full deciding set, at one set all, or at two sets all in a best-of-five match. The 10-point version is often called a super tiebreak. The first to ten points with a two-point lead wins the match, and three details from the ITF's note on it are worth knowing:

- The original order of service continues, so whoever is due to serve next starts it.
- There is a 120-second set break before it begins.
- The balls are not changed before it, even if a ball change is due.

Within the match tiebreak the serve order, court halves and six-point change of ends work exactly as in a standard tiebreak, because it is a tie-break game. The 7-point match tiebreak is also approved, though the 10-point form is the one the tours use.

## The final set at the Grand Slams

The 2026 Official Grand Slam Rule Book makes men's singles main-draw matches best of five sets and other matches best of three unless a tournament decides otherwise. Its rule on tiebreaks says the tie-breaks in the Rules of Tennis may be used, including the final set, where a 10-point tie-break will be played. The ITF defines that final-set tiebreak as starting at six games all in the deciding set.

So a deciding set at a major is still a real set. It starts as an ordinary set, and only at 6-6 does it switch to a race to ten by two. For the other sets, the rule simply points to the tie-breaks in the Rules of Tennis, whose standard form is the 7-point game at 6-6. According to the USTA, the four majors announced a consistent final-set format in March 2022. The Australian Open had been using the 10-point version since 2019, and the other three adopted it from the 2022 French Open.

## No-ad games and the deciding point

No-ad scoring is not a tiebreak, but it does the same job inside a single game, and it is half of the tour doubles format. Appendix VI scores a no-ad game normally until deuce. At deuce, one deciding point settles the game. The receiver chooses whether to take it on the right or the left half. In doubles the receiving pair cannot swap positions for it, and in mixed doubles the player of the same gender as the server receives. ATP chair umpires announce it as "Deuce, Deciding Point, Receiver's Choice."

## Short sets and the 5-point tiebreak

For shortened formats, Appendix VI defines a short set: first to four games by two, with a tiebreak at 4-4, or at 3-3 if the sanctioning body chooses. Short sets may use a 5-point tiebreak with a deciding point at 4-4, an order and number of serves set by the sanctioning body, and a change of ends only after the first four points.

## Mix-ups worth correcting

| Common belief | What the 2026 rules say |
| --- | --- |
| "First to seven wins." | First to seven with a two-point lead (Rule 5b); there is no cap. |
| "Ends change when someone reaches six." | Ends change after every six points played in total (Rule 10). |
| "New balls come out for the tiebreak." | A ball change never happens at the start of a tiebreak. It waits until the second game of the next set, and the tiebreak counts as one game for ball changes (Rule 3). |
| "A match tiebreak and a final-set tiebreak are the same." | The first replaces the deciding set. The second is played at 6-6 inside it (Appendix VI). |
| "If the wrong player served, the points are replayed." | Points already played stand. An error found after an even number of points is corrected at once; after an odd number, the altered order continues (Rule 27d). |
