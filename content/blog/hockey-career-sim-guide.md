Hockey is unusually badly served by career games. The management side is well covered — rosters, cap sheets, draft boards — but the experience of being one player, in one role, across a decade of seasons is rarer, and the goaltender's version of it is rarer still.

[Hockey Career Sim 2026](/apps/hockey-career-sim/) is built around that gap. This guide covers what each position path actually asks of you, how development is weighted, and where careers tend to stall.

## Four positions, four different games

The game supports forward, centre, defenceman and goaltender. Those are not skins on one career. Each one changes both the attributes that matter and the decisions the simulator puts in front of you.

| Path | Core attributes | Typical in-game decision | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Winger (scoring forward) | Shooting, skating, puck control | Shoot or pass, take the lane or reset | A first career |
| Centre | Passing, awareness, positioning, stamina | Support the play or drive it, defensive responsibility | Players who like being the connector |
| Defenceman | Checking, positioning, passing | Challenge the carrier or hold the gap, join the rush or stay | Players who prefer prevention to production |
| Goaltender | Goaltending, positioning, awareness | Angle, depth, hold or challenge | Players who want a position almost no other game offers |

If you have not played much hockey, [hockey positions explained](/blog/hockey-positions-explained/) covers what each role does on the ice before you commit a career to one.

### The goaltender path is genuinely different

Worth stating plainly, because it is the reason many people install a hockey career game at all. A goaltender's career has:

- **A different attribute tree.** Skating and shooting are near-irrelevant; goaltending, positioning and awareness carry the position.
- **A different decision rhythm.** A skater makes many small decisions with small consequences. A goaltender makes fewer decisions, each with a large one.
- **A different statistical fingerprint.** Save-based measures move slowly and are heavily team-dependent, so a good season on a bad team reads worse than it was.

That last point is the one that frustrates people, and it is worth accepting rather than fighting. Goaltending is the position where the gap between how well you played and what the numbers say is widest.

## Development is weighted, so spread is expensive

The game develops skating, shooting, passing, puck control, checking, positioning, stamina, awareness and goaltending. Those are not equally valuable to you — they are equally *available*, which is a different thing.

A defenceman who invests heavily in shooting is buying an attribute the simulator will rarely ask about. A winger who neglects skating is buying a ceiling. The rule is straightforward:

**Spend on the two or three attributes your position is asked about most, until they are clearly good, before you spend anywhere else.**

Then, from roughly your third or fourth professional season, start correcting the specific weakness that is losing you outcomes rather than the one that looks lowest on the sheet. Those are often different attributes.

## Playing time is the real currency

Roster spots have to be earned, and the loop is tighter than it looks:

performance → playing time → more situations → faster development → better performance

The consequence is that early seasons matter disproportionately. A player who breaks into a regular role two seasons earlier does not finish two seasons ahead — they finish with a compounding advantage, because every extra shift was also a development opportunity.

Practical version: take the role with minutes over the role with prestige, every time, until you are established.

## The parts of the career that are not on the ice

Relationships with coaches, teammates, agents, media and rivals sit alongside recovery, injuries, contracts and expectations. These are not decoration. They gate opportunities, and a player who ignores them accumulates a quiet penalty that shows up as "why am I not getting minutes".

Contract and team-change decisions give the career some of the texture of a manager game while keeping you in the player's skates. A bigger club is not automatically better: playing time, role and team quality trade off against each other, and the offer with the largest number attached is frequently the one that costs you a season of development on the bench.

## Reading a season honestly

Hockey's most-quoted individual number, plus-minus, is also its most misleading. It counts goals for and against while you are on the ice at even strength, which folds your teammates' finishing, your goaltender's night and your coach's deployment into a single figure attributed to you. A defensive specialist who starts most shifts in their own zone will look worse than a winger who starts most shifts in the offensive zone, regardless of how well either played.

Read it alongside your role rather than instead of it. [Hockey line combinations explained](/blog/hockey-line-combinations-explained/) covers how deployment works and why it distorts individual numbers so heavily.

## What this simulates, and what it does not

Outcomes are sampled from a model using your attributes, the situation and the game's own assumptions. Real hockey is governed by published rulebooks — [USA Hockey's officiating rulebook](https://www.usahockeyrulebook.com/) is a freely readable example — and the game borrows the structure of the sport rather than any league's specific rules. Every league, team and player in it is fictional, and no result it produces is a forecast of anything real.
