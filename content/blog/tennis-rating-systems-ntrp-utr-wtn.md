A tennis **ranking** and a tennis **rating** answer different questions. A ranking adds up points earned for results at eligible tournaments and puts players in order. It is what the ATP, for example, uses to decide entry and seeding. A rating estimates how well someone plays right now, by comparing their match results with what was expected. This guide compares three rating systems. The USTA's **NTRP** runs from 1.5 to 7.0. The **UTR Rating** from UTR Sports runs from 1.00 to 16.50. The ITF's **World Tennis Number (WTN)** runs from 40 down to 1, so on that scale a lower number is better.

Each is described here from its owner's own published material, checked on 2 October 2026. None of them is better in the abstract; they are built for different jobs, and the useful question is which one your league, club or event uses.

## Rankings and ratings are different tools

The professional ranking is the clearest example of the first kind. The 2026 ATP rulebook calls its rankings "the objective merit-based method" for deciding entry and seeding. The ranking period is the past 52 weeks, and the total comes from a player's results at the biggest events plus a set number of best results elsewhere. The rulebook's points tables award points by the round reached, and they drop off after 52 weeks. [How tennis ranking points work](/blog/how-tennis-ranking-points-work/) goes through the details.

A rating ignores how far you went in an event. It asks whether you did better or worse than your rating predicted against the opponents you actually faced. That leads to the effect that confuses players most: **a win can lower a rating and a loss can raise one.** The WTN's FAQ gives an example. If the algorithm gave you a 90% chance of winning and you won only in three sets, you did not meet its projection. A ranking would simply have given you the points for the round.

## NTRP: the USTA's level descriptor

The NTRP is built around **skill descriptions**. The USTA defines it as a numerical indicator of ability from 1.5 to 7.0, with each half-point level tied to a set of general characteristics. Its own examples sketch the range. A 1.5 is still working on getting the ball into play. A 3.0 is fairly consistent on medium-paced shots but lacks control of direction, depth and pace. A 6.0 typically has intensive training, international or top college competition and a national ranking behind them. The 6.5 and 7.0 levels are world-class players.

Ratings are generated from play in USTA adult divisions: 18 & Over, 40 & Over, 55 & Over, 65 & Over, and mixed 18, 40 and 55 & Over. In some USTA sections, results from sanctioned tournaments and other leagues are included too. Players can also self-rate. The USTA describes ratings that are established after only a few matches and then "slowly increase or decrease over time." Its explainer describes how the rating behaves, not the formula behind it.

**Best suited to:** adult league and team play in the USTA system, where the level bands decide who you are grouped with.

## UTR Rating: a game-by-game average

The Universal Tennis Rating puts all players on one 1.00 to 16.50 scale, "regardless of age, gender, geography or skill level," in UTR Sports' words. The company describes it as a modified Elo system. Its published method has five parts:

- Each match gives every player a **match rating** and a **match weight**. Your UTR Rating is the weighted average of up to **30** of your most recent match ratings, from the **last 12 months** only.
- The key input is the **percentage of games won** compared with what the rating gap predicted. A close match against a much stronger player can lift your rating. A narrow win over a much weaker one can lower it.
- Matches are excluded when the higher-rated player wins and the gap is more than 2.00, and when neither player won at least four games before a withdrawal or retirement. Walkovers and defaults are not used.
- Ratings recalculate **daily**, so they can move without you playing, as opponents' ratings change or old results age out.
- One match gives a **projected** rating, shown as (P), and a few more make it reliable. A separate **Verified** UTR Rating counts only results from vetted third-party events. College coaches and tournament providers use it for recruiting, entry and line-ups.

**Best suited to:** players who compete across ages, genders and countries and want one continuous number, especially juniors heading toward college tennis.

## World Tennis Number: the ITF's global scale

The WTN is the ITF's attempt at a single global standard. Its FAQ sets out the main features:

- The scale runs from **40**, a beginner, to **1**, an elite professional, for all players regardless of age, gender or ability. Each player has separate **singles** and **doubles** numbers, calculated by separate algorithms.
- The algorithm uses match results from **2016 onwards**. It predicts each match from both players' ratings, then moves the numbers according to how the actual result compares. Results are analysed set by set, with the game score factored in.
- Numbers update **weekly, every Wednesday**.
- A **confidence level** comes from the rating deviation in the Glicko-2 method. A player with a thin record will see their number move more. The FAQ gives an example of reaching a verified number: 15 completed sets within five weeks against opponents in your "Game zONe".
- The **Game zONe** is the band of opponents against whom your chance of winning sits between 35% and 65%, the most competitive matches available. A singles player whose WTN is 10 or above, numerically, has one; the strongest players, with numbers below 10, do not, and neither do doubles numbers.

**Best suited to:** players who want one scale that runs from beginner to professional across countries, which is how the ITF presents the WTN.

## Reading the numbers without getting misled

Three habits keep ratings useful:

1. **Mind the direction.** A UTR of 6 and a WTN of 6 mean very different things. One is low on its scale and the other is near the top. A "better" WTN is a smaller number.
2. **Don't convert.** The scales differ in range, direction and the matches they draw on, so a conversion chart is at best an estimate. Use the number your event asks for.
3. **Expect noise from a thin record.** UTR marks a rating as projected until a few more matches make it reliable, the WTN's confidence level is lower for a player with little history, and the USTA describes an NTRP level as established after a few matches. A rating built on two matches says little.

Ratings are also not rankings in disguise. Entry to professional events runs on rankings: the ATP's rulebook names its rankings as the method for entry and seeding. [From the junior circuit to the pro tour](/blog/junior-circuit-to-pro-tour-path/) explains how juniors cross over.
