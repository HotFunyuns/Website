Vietnamese numbers look simple: ten words and a regular system for combining them. The catch is a handful of sound changes, where one, four, five and ten change their pronunciation inside larger numbers. Learn those four and you can say any price in Vietnam. Every syllable carries a tone, shown by a diacritic or, for the level tone in hai and ba, by no mark at all, so learn each number with audio.

## One to ten

| Number | Vietnamese |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | không |
| 1 | một |
| 2 | hai |
| 3 | ba |
| 4 | bốn |
| 5 | năm |
| 6 | sáu |
| 7 | bảy |
| 8 | tám |
| 9 | chín |
| 10 | mười |

Wikipedia notes that Vietnamese historically had **two sets of numbers**, native and Sino-Vietnamese (*nhất, nhị, tam*), and that the **native set is used for everyday counting** and mathematics. Sino-Vietnamese numbers survive in fixed expressions, much as Latin *bi-* survives in "bicycle".

## Eleven to ninety-nine

Tens and units simply join: 11 is *mười một*, 19 is *mười chín*, 30 is *ba mươi*, 37 is *ba mươi bảy*. Wikipedia also notes the colloquial *chục* for ten, so twenty can be *hai chục*.

## The four sound changes

| Change | When | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| mười → **mươi** | ten as a multiplied tens word, from 20 | 20 hai mươi, 50 năm mươi |
| một → **mốt** | one as the last digit, from 21 to 91 | 21 hai mươi mốt |
| bốn → **tư** | four as the last digit after 20, more commonly | 24 hai mươi tư |
| năm → **lăm** | five as the last digit after 10 | 15 mười lăm, 25 hai mươi lăm |

Two details trip learners. Eleven keeps *một*: *mười một*, not *mười mốt*. And Wikipedia explains that *lăm* exists **to avoid confusion with *năm*, "year"**. Numbers ending in **05**, such as 605, keep *năm*, and some northern speakers say *nhăm* instead of *lăm*.

## Hundreds and the zero in between

Hundreds use *trăm*: 300 is *ba trăm*, 345 is *ba trăm bốn mươi lăm*. When the tens digit is zero, Vietnamese says so. Wikipedia gives 101 as **một trăm linh một** in the North and **một trăm lẻ một** in the South and Centre. A zero hundreds digit is spoken as *không trăm*: 1,001 is *một nghìn không trăm linh một*.

## Thousands, millions and billions

| Number | Vietnamese |
| --- | --- |
| 1,000 | nghìn (North), ngàn (South) |
| 10,000 | mười nghìn |
| 100,000 | trăm nghìn |
| 1,000,000 | triệu |
| 1,000,000,000 | tỷ |

Wikipedia notes that modern Vietnamese **groups place values in thousands**, as English does, unlike Chinese. Its example: 123,123,123 is *một trăm hai mươi ba triệu một trăm hai mươi ba nghìn một trăm hai mươi ba*, literally 123 million, 123 thousand, 123. When writing figures, Vietnamese uses a **comma as the decimal separator**.

## A worked example: four prices

1. **35,000₫**: *ba mươi lăm nghìn đồng*. The five takes *lăm*.
2. **250,000₫**: *hai trăm năm mươi nghìn đồng*. Here five is a tens digit, so it stays *năm*.
3. **1,500,000₫**: *một triệu năm trăm nghìn đồng*.
4. **21,000₫**: *hai mươi mốt nghìn đồng*, with *mốt*.

In Ho Chi Minh City you would hear *ngàn* in place of *nghìn* in each one; [northern versus southern Vietnamese](/blog/northern-vs-southern-vietnamese/) covers other differences.

## Prices in đồng

The **đồng** (₫, informally đ; code VND) once had subunits, the *hào* and *xu*, but Wikipedia notes **both are obsolete** because of inflation. Banknotes in common use run from **1,000₫ to 500,000₫**, Wikipedia notes that coins were withdrawn from distribution in April 2011, and that since 2003 **polymer notes** have replaced cotton ones for 10,000₫ and above. To ask a price, use **bao nhiêu**, "how much?" or "how many?", which Wiktionary lists for both.

## Ordinal numbers

Ordinals put *thứ* in front: *thứ ba* is "third", *thứ năm* "fifth". Wikipedia notes that for first and fourth the Sino-Vietnamese readings **thứ nhất** and **thứ tư** are more commonly used, and that second is occasionally *thứ nhì* instead of *thứ hai*.

## Counting things

In Vietnamese, a number usually goes before the noun with a classifier between them, as in Wikipedia's *một chục quả trứng*, "ten eggs", with *quả* as the classifier. [Vietnamese classifiers](/blog/vietnamese-classifiers/) explains the system.
