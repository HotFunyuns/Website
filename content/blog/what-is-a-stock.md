A stock is a share of ownership in a company. Investor.gov, the SEC's site for individual investors, defines stocks as a type of security that gives stockholders a share of ownership in a company, and notes that stocks are also called equities. Buy one share of a company with a million shares outstanding and you own one millionth of it.

That sounds simple, but "owning a piece of a company" has specific meanings: certain rights, certain ways to gain, and a definite place in the queue if things go wrong.

## What ownership gives you

Owning common stock usually brings three things.

- **A claim on the company's value.** If the business grows and investors value it more highly, the share price can rise. This gain, called capital appreciation, is realised only when you sell.
- **A possible share of profits.** Some companies pay part of their profits to shareholders as dividends. Others do not; Investor.gov notes growth stocks rarely pay them. The [dividends guide](/blog/how-dividends-work/) explains how payments are declared and who receives them.
- **A vote.** Investor.gov notes that common stock entitles owners to vote at shareholder meetings.

What ownership does not give you is a say in daily decisions, a right to the company's cash, or any promise that the shares will be worth what you paid.

## Common and preferred stock

| | Common stock | Preferred stock |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voting rights | Usually yes | Usually no |
| Dividends | Paid after preferred holders, if at all | Paid before common holders |
| In a liquidation | Last in line | Ahead of common holders |

Investor.gov summarises preferred stock in these terms: usually no voting rights, but dividends paid before common stockholders and priority over common stockholders if the company goes bankrupt and its assets are liquidated.

## Why companies issue stock

Selling shares lets a company raise money without borrowing it. The buyers become part-owners and share in the risk and any reward. A company first sells shares to the public in an [initial public offering](/blog/what-is-an-ipo/); after that, the shares trade between investors on the stock market, and the company receives nothing from those later trades. [How the stock market works](/blog/how-the-stock-market-works/) explains that second step.

## How shareholders can lose

Investor.gov is plain about the risks. Stock prices move down as well as up, and there is no guarantee that a company will grow or do well, so investors can lose money. Prices can be moved by events inside the company, such as a faulty product, or outside it, such as political or market events. Investor.gov also notes that large-company stocks as a group have lost money in about one year out of three, on average.

The worst case is bankruptcy. If a company's assets are liquidated, the order of payment is:

1. Creditors, including bondholders.
2. Preferred stockholders.
3. Common stockholders, who get whatever is left, which may be nothing.

That ordering is why stocks and bonds carry different risks; [stocks versus bonds](/blog/stocks-vs-bonds/) compares them directly.

## Ways people describe stocks

Investor.gov sorts stocks into rough categories:

- **Growth stocks**, with earnings growing faster than the market average; they rarely pay dividends.
- **Income stocks**, which pay dividends consistently.
- **Value stocks**, with a low price-to-earnings ratio, which may reflect that they have fallen out of favour.
- **Blue-chip stocks**, shares in large, well-known companies with long histories.

Stocks are also grouped by company size, measured by [market capitalisation](/blog/market-capitalization-explained/): large-cap, mid-cap and small-cap. Investor.gov warns that very small "microcap" and "penny" stocks may have little or no earnings and are highly speculative.

These labels describe characteristics, not quality. A stock in any category can rise or fall.

## Where the information comes from

Public companies register their stock with the SEC and, in most cases, file reports quarterly and annually; the annual reports include financial statements audited by an independent firm. Anyone can read them free on the SEC's EDGAR system. The SEC's *Beginners' Guide to Financial Statements* explains how to read the balance sheet, income statement and cash flow statement they contain, and FINRA's guide to evaluating stocks covers the ratios analysts commonly derive from them.
