**Pressing in soccer (association football) means putting the team with the ball under pressure in order to win it back.** A high press does it far up the pitch, close to the opponent's goal; counter-pressing — "gegenpressing" — does it in the seconds straight after losing the ball. The tactics site Spielverlagerung gives the cleanest definition: pressing is "creating tension with the intention of getting the ball back", as distinct from pressure, which is the tension itself.

That distinction is the key to the whole subject. Every movement on a pitch creates some pressure; pressing is pressure organised around a purpose, a place and a moment. This guide covers all three, then how analysts try to measure it.

## Where a team presses

The [Spielverlagerung explainer](https://spielverlagerung.com/2017/03/05/pressing-counterpressing-and-counterattacking/) by Adin Osmanbasic points to a model used by the German football association: split the pitch into attacking, midfield and defending thirds, then split each third into a high and a deep zone. That gives six possible pressing heights.

"High press" is the shorthand for the top of that range — pressing in the attacking third, where a turnover leaves the shortest route to goal. The cost of pressing high is the space it leaves behind the first line, which is why the decision is never just about courage.

## Why a press needs access to the ball

The same explainer makes a central point: pressing only works when the pressers have **access** to the ball, meaning they can actually reach it before the opponent escapes. With access, the rest of the team can push towards the ball and leave the far side open. Without it, the logic collapses — a team that keeps a high defensive line without pressure on the ball invites long passes into the space behind its defenders, and a team that crowds one side without pressure is exposed by every switch of play.

When there is no access, a team has two broad options: drop deeper and narrower to protect the space near goal, or step up together and use the offside law to catch runners — the second relying on the timing that [how the offside rule works](/blog/how-the-offside-rule-works/) explains.

## Pressing triggers

Well-drilled pressing teams do not chase every ball. They wait for moments that tilt the contest their way. The triggers Spielverlagerung lists include:

| Trigger | Why it helps the pressers |
| --- | --- |
| The receiver is facing away from his passing options | He cannot see the escape route before the press arrives |
| A poor first touch | It takes time to regain control |
| A weaker player on the ball | Pressure is more likely to force an error |
| An isolated opponent | Fewer team-mates nearby means fewer passing options |
| The nature of the pass | Pressers can close in while the pass is still travelling, because the ball cannot change direction mid-route |

Some teams go further and set **pressing traps**: they leave one option deliberately open — a pass into a crowded area, say — and close in once it is taken. Colin Trainor, who introduced the PPDA measure below, acknowledged that pressing teams may wait for triggers rather than press all the time. For analysts, that means a match-long figure mixes moments of pressing with moments of waiting.

## Counter-pressing (gegenpressing)

Counter-pressing is pressing straight after losing the ball. Will Morgan's [StatsBomb analysis](https://blogarchive.statsbomb.com/articles/soccer/how-statsbomb-data-helps-measure-counter-pressing/) describes it as "the process where a team presses the opposition immediately after losing possession", with the initial aim of disrupting the opponent's counter-attack while the team that lost the ball is defensively unstable. Analysts often treat the first **five seconds** after a turnover as the counter-pressing window.

Spielverlagerung frames it as an attempt to run the cycle of play backwards. Instead of losing the ball and retreating into defensive organisation, the team tries to go from attack, to a brief defensive transition, straight back to attack — skipping the defensive shape entirely when it works. It also has an attacking pay-off: an opponent who has just won the ball is usually spreading out to counter, so winning it back immediately catches them disorganised.

## How pressing is measured

| Measure | Data needed | What it captures | What it misses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PPDA (2014) | Event data | Defensive actions relative to opponent passes in a zone | Off-ball positioning, and whether the press worked |
| Pressure events | Event data with a logged "pressure" | Each time a defender closes in on the ball carrier | The presser's speed and movement, and other players' positions |
| Pressing intensity (2024) | Tracking data | Every defender's chance of reaching every attacker or the ball, frame by frame | Whether the press won the ball; it also needs full tracking data, not just events |

### PPDA

[PPDA](https://www.hudl.com/blog/defensive-metrics-measuring-the-intensity-of-a-high-press) — passes allowed per defensive action — divides the passes the opponent makes by the defending team's tackles, interceptions, challenges and fouls, counting only events in a chosen zone. In Trainor's version that zone was a team's attacking half plus a little of its own. Lower means more intense.

Two hypothetical teams show how it reads. Team X allows 240 opponent passes in the zone and makes 40 defensive actions there: PPDA 6. Team Y allows 150 passes and makes 15 actions: PPDA 10. Team X is the more aggressive presser — but note that Team Y allowed far fewer passes in the zone, perhaps because its opponent went long. Because the opponent's passes are half of the ratio, PPDA describes a matchup as much as a team. Joris Bekkers adds the structural limit: built from on-ball events, it has no information about the positions of players away from the ball.

### Pressure events

Event data can also log pressure directly. StatsBomb's pressure event is triggered when a player is within about five yards of an opponent in possession, a radius that stretches up to ten yards where an error would be costlier, such as against a goalkeeper. From these, Morgan built a "counter-pressing fraction": the share of possessions, starting in the zones where teams counter-press, that come under pressure within five seconds. He also warned against reading cause into its strong link with possession — teams that keep the ball may have more energy to press, and pressing may in turn win the ball back sooner.

### Pressing intensity

The newest approach uses tracking data. Bekkers's [pressing intensity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.04712) model estimates, for every frame, how long each defender would need to intercept each attacker or the ball, using positions, velocities, movement directions and reaction time, then converts those times into probabilities with a logistic function. A speed threshold filters out defenders who are not actively pressing. The author is open about one simplification — the model treats the individual probabilities as independent — and the method needs full player-tracking data, with every player's position and velocity, rather than event data alone.

## The cost: legs

Pressing is expensive. Every sprint to close a passing lane is a sprint not available later, and a press that fades leaves exactly the gaps that access was supposed to prevent. Bekkers suggests one direction for measuring "smart" pressing: weighing pressure applied against the energy spent applying it.
