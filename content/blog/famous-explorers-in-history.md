Lists of famous explorers are usually lists of the people whose names were on the ships. The navigators, interpreters and guides without whom most of those voyages would have failed appear, if at all, as a line in someone else's account. This article keeps the famous names — they did the things the records say they did — and puts the other names back where the evidence supports it, which turns out to be more often than the popular versions suggest.

It also uses a plainer vocabulary. Nobody discovered a place that had people living in it. Saying "reached", "arrived at" or "was the first European to record" costs nothing and describes what happened.

## Tupaia, and a chart nobody has finished arguing about

In July 1769 an arioi priest and navigator from Raʻiātea in the Society Islands joined HMS *Endeavour* at Tahiti. His name was Tupaia. He was not a passenger. He directed the ship to islands its crew did not know, and when the *Endeavour* reached Aotearoa New Zealand he was able to speak with Māori, because Tahitian and te reo Māori are closely related languages. That made him the expedition's interpreter at the single most consequential contact of the voyage.

He also produced, with Cook and others, a chart naming a very large number of Pacific islands. What that chart represents has been argued over for two centuries: whether it uses a single frame of reference, how its directions should be read, how much of it records places Tupaia had been and how much records inherited navigational knowledge. Lars Eckstein and Anja Schwarz published a book-length study of the question in *The Journal of Pacific History* in 2018 — ninety-five pages on one document, which is a fair index of how much is at stake.

Tupaia died at Batavia, in what is now Jakarta, in 1770, of disease contracted there. He never got home. The voyage is remembered as Cook's.

## Sacagawea, and what the journals actually say

The popular Sacagawea is a guide who led the Corps of Discovery across the continent. The National Park Service, which runs the Lewis and Clark National Historic Trail, states the position plainly: while she is often remembered that way, the expedition journals offer little evidence for it, and historians generally think she travelled because her husband had been hired as a translator.

That is a correction, not a demotion. What the record does support is substantial. She was Lemhi Shoshone, taken from her home region by a Hidatsa raiding party as a child of around eleven or twelve. She was roughly sixteen or seventeen on the expedition, and she carried her newborn son with her. She interpreted, and the National Park Service notes that her presence among the peoples the party met dispelled the idea that they were a war party — a diplomatic function that no amount of firepower could have supplied.

The reason the inflated version is worth resisting is that it is easier to dismiss. An interpreter and a diplomatic presence whose contribution is documented in the journals is a firmer historical claim than a guide the journals do not describe.

## Ootah, Egingwah, Seegloo, Ooqueah — and Peary's own book

The clearest case of the pattern is also the best documented, because the commander wrote it down himself.

In *The North Pole*, published in 1910, Robert Peary names the party that made the final march north in April 1909: Matthew Henson, an African American who had travelled with him on Arctic expeditions for years, and four Inuit men — Ootah, Egingwah, Seegloo and Ooqueah. Peary gives their ages and builds, praises their capacities, and writes that all four "have been brought up to regard me as the patron, protector, and guide of their people."

That sentence is the whole problem in miniature. It is written by the man who chose them, in a book whose title assigns the achievement to himself, and it reframes people whose skills the expedition depended on as dependents. Arctic travel in that era ran on Inuit technique — dog handling, sledge design, snow-house construction, clothing, route knowledge on sea ice — and the collections held by institutions such as the Scott Polar Research Institute in Cambridge document that material culture in detail.

Whether the party reached the geographic pole has been disputed since 1909 and is still not settled. The identity of the people who made the attempt is not disputed at all, because the commander listed them. They are simply left out of the retellings.

## Columbus, and a governorship that has to be stated directly

Columbus made four Atlantic crossings between 1492 and 1504. He was also, from 1493, the Crown's governor in the Caribbean, and what happened under that authority is documented rather than inferred.

Taíno communities on Hispaniola and neighbouring islands were subjected to forced labour, a tribute system demanding gold, and enslavement, including shipment across the Atlantic. Conflict, forced relocation and introduced disease compounded each other. The National Park Service's description of Salt River Bay on St Croix — the site of an armed encounter with Columbus's second voyage in 1493 — lists among the periods represented there "the 1493 encounter with Columbus, Spanish capture and removal of the island's Amerindian peoples." That is a United States federal agency describing the removal of a population in its own words.

Population estimates are where the scholarship genuinely divides. Figures for Hispaniola in 1492 published by different demographers span more than two orders of magnitude — the Berkeley School of historical demographers argued for millions, while others have defended figures in the tens or low hundreds of thousands. The disagreement is methodological: it depends on how you read early Spanish counts, carrying-capacity models and archaeological settlement data. What is not in dispute is the direction and the speed. Within a generation the Indigenous population of Hispaniola had collapsed.

Two further things belong in the same paragraph rather than a footnote. Columbus was removed from his governorship and returned to Spain under arrest in 1500 after an inquiry into his administration — the complaints came from Spanish colonists as well as from the treatment of Indigenous people. And Taíno descent persists in Caribbean populations today, and Taíno identity is actively claimed by living communities; the past tense is a choice, and often the wrong one.

## A guide whose story is mostly speculation

Enrique of Malacca, an enslaved man who served Magellan as an interpreter, is sometimes described as the first person to circumnavigate the globe, on the reasoning that he may have been taken from the region the expedition eventually reached. The evidence for where he came from is thin, and the claim outruns it. It is worth naming him — he was there, he interpreted, he was enslaved — and worth saying clearly that the circumnavigation claim is a hypothesis, not a finding. The discipline involved is the one described in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/): distinguishing what a document states from what a later writer built on top of it. [How we know what historical figures actually did](/blog/how-we-know-what-historical-figures-did/) works through the same test in more detail.

## Why the pattern is structural, not accidental

Guides go missing from the record for reasons that are easy to name once you look for them.

Expedition accounts were written to justify expenditure to sponsors, and sponsors wanted a hero. Naming conventions favoured people with surnames in the writer's own system. Interpreters were often enslaved or coerced, and nobody credits a tool. And the accounts were usually published long after the journey, by which time the commander controlled the narrative and the guide, frequently, was dead.

The result is that the least replaceable person on many of these voyages is the least visible one in the record. That is worth knowing before you read any expedition narrative, including the ones in which everybody behaves well.

## The rule worth keeping

When you read about an expedition, ask who else was on it, who spoke the languages, who knew the route, and who wrote the account. Then check whether the person who knew the route is named in the same detail as the person who paid for the ship.

The answer is almost never yes. Noticing the gap is not political correctness; it is the ordinary source criticism you would apply to any document with an interested author.
