Most people arrive at a draft with a ranked list and call it a board. Then the draft starts, the first four names disappear in ninety seconds, and the list stops answering any question they actually have.

A ranking tells you who is best. A board tells you what to do with the pick in front of you. Those are not the same document, and only one of them survives contact with a live draft.

## The three questions a board has to answer instantly

Everything in board construction serves this. At any moment, without scrolling and without thinking, you should be able to see:

1. **Who is left in the tier I care about**, at each position.
2. **How far the drop is** if that tier empties before my next pick.
3. **Which of my slots is still unfilled**, and how late I can afford to leave it.

If your board cannot answer those three in a glance, it is a reference document, not a decision tool. Fix that before you worry about anything else.

## Build it in five passes

### Pass one: settle the roster structure before you look at a single player

Write down the slots you have to fill and the number of picks you get. The gap between those two numbers is your slack, and it determines how much risk the whole plan can carry.

Say a format gives you fifteen picks and requires twelve filled positions. Three picks are discretionary — you can use them on depth, on upside, or on covering a position twice. Now say the same format requires fourteen filled positions. One pick of slack changes the draft from a strategy exercise into a checklist, and knowing that in advance stops you spending pick four on a luxury you cannot afford.

Football is where this bites hardest, because a team sheet carries a lot of distinct roles and every one of them needs an owner. A plan sized for a handful of slots will not cover it.

### Pass two: rank within positions, never across them

Comparing a lineman's rating to a receiver's rating is comparing two different scales that happen to share an axis. The ratings mean different things because the jobs are different, and the pools have different depths.

So: one column per position, sorted, and no cross-position ranking at this stage. You will get to sequencing in pass four, and you will do it properly there.

### Pass three: cut the tiers at the cliffs

Read down each column and look for where the numbers stop declining gently and fall off. That break is a tier boundary. Everything above it is a band you would be roughly equally content with; everything below is a meaningful downgrade.

The output is deliberately lumpy. A position might have two players in tier one and fifteen in tier three. That shape *is* the analysis:

- **A wide tier** means you can wait. Someone from it will still be there.
- **A narrow tier above a big cliff** means you cannot. If you want anyone from it, this is the pick.
- **A position with no cliffs at all** — a smooth gradient from top to bottom — is one you should almost never spend an early pick on, because the cost of waiting is close to zero.

Finding cliffs is the entire reason preparation beats improvisation. During the draft you are under time pressure and you will not see them.

### Pass four: sequence across positions using scarcity, not preference

Now you compare columns, and the comparison is not "who is better" — it is "which tier will not be there when I come back".

Lay your tiers side by side and ask, for each position: *how many picks until this tier is empty?* You will not know precisely, and you do not need to. You need the ordering. Positions whose top tier is two names deep get taken early. Positions whose useful tier runs fifteen deep get taken from the leftovers.

The uncomfortable implication is that this sometimes tells you to draft a worse player earlier. That is correct, and it is the single most reliable edge in drafting.

### Pass five: write down what you will not do

A short list of pre-commitments, made while calm:

- Positions you will not take before a given round.
- The maximum number of players you will hold at any one position.
- Any player you have decided is not for you, with the reason next to their name.

The reason matters more than the rule. During the draft you will want to break it, and the only defensible ground for breaking it is that the reason no longer applies.

## Running the board while the draft is moving

### Cross off, do not re-rank

When a player goes, strike them. Do not reorder anything. A board that is being continuously rewritten mid-draft is a board you have stopped trusting, and the rewriting eats exactly the seconds you needed for the decision.

### Check the shape every few picks, not every pick

The useful periodic question is not "who is the best available" but "which of my tiers is closest to emptying". That question takes three seconds and it is the one that prevents the classic disaster — arriving at your last four picks with two positions that have nothing left in them at all.

### Deviate only against new information

You will feel the pull to abandon the board at some point in every draft. Apply one test: *can I state in a single sentence what changed?*

Legitimate sentences: "Four managers took that position in a row and the tier is gone." "The cap means I can no longer afford anyone in tier two." "The position I planned to fill last now has fewer players left than I have picks."

Illegitimate sentence: "This one looks good."

### Keep a two-deep view of the danger positions

For any position where a single unfilled slot would sink the roster, know your fallback *before* you need it. Not the best remaining player — the one you would actually accept. Having that name in mind converts a panic pick into a shrug.

## What different rule sets demand from a board

The board-building method does not change across formats. What changes is how finished it has to be when the draft opens.

[20-0 Football Draft & GM Mode](/apps/football-draft-gm-mode/) is a good place to feel that difference, because its formats sit at opposite ends of the spectrum:

- **Unlimited Draft** lets you keep comparing options. Here a board is a convenience — you can do a lot of the thinking live, and this is the format to learn a pool in.
- **Limited Draft** removes refreshes entirely, so every pick has to count. This is where preparation is the whole game. Anything you did not tier in advance, you are guessing at.
- **Salary Cap Draft** adds a price to every name, which means your board needs a second column and your tiers need re-reading in cost terms. A tier-two player at a low price and a tier-one player at a high one are not comparable on quality alone.
- **Playoff Mode** puts the finished roster under pressure, which tends to punish the slot you left thin.

Beyond the drafts themselves the game includes Franchise Mode and GM Mode for the longer organisational decisions, VS Computer, Battle and Tournament for playing a roster against an opponent, a Daily Challenge, and Gridiron IQ tests of how well you read a team sheet. The lineup builder is where the board turns into an actual roster, position by position.

When the season simulator returns a record, treat it as a score for the roster you built rather than a forecast of anything. It is a model evaluating your construction, and reading it as a prediction about the real sport is a category error.

## Five ways boards fail

1. **It is a single list.** One column of names cannot express scarcity, and scarcity is what you are drafting against.
2. **The tiers are cosmetic.** Even bands of five, drawn to look tidy, contain no information at all.
3. **It ignores the format.** A board built for unlimited comparison is not a board for a no-refresh draft.
4. **It has no fallbacks.** Every position needs a name you would settle for, written down while you are calm.
5. **It gets abandoned at pick three.** Usually because it was never checked against the roster structure, so it started giving advice that made no sense.

## The board is also a record

Keep it after the draft. When the season result comes back, the board tells you what you knew at each pick, which is the only way to separate a bad decision from a bad outcome.

Most people improve at drafting by remembering results. Results are noisy. The board is the thing that lets you audit the reasoning instead, and reasoning is the part you can actually change.
