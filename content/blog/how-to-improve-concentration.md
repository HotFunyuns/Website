Most advice about concentration is advice about wanting it more. The research points somewhere less flattering and more useful: concentration is largely a property of the situation you are sitting in — how often it is interrupted, how clearly the task is specified, how long the session is meant to last — and those are things you can change today without becoming a different person.

## Start with the interruption

The most consequential variable is the one that arrives from outside. A notification, a question from across the room, a tab you opened for a reason you have already forgotten: each one ends the current state and requires you to rebuild it.

What that rebuild actually costs is more surprising than the folklore suggests. Gloria Mark, Daniela Gudith and Ulrich Klocke ran a controlled study of interrupted work and expected the usual story — that interruptions slow people down. They found the opposite on the clock. People completed interrupted tasks in *less* time, with no difference in quality, apparently compensating by working faster.

The cost was real; it simply landed somewhere else. Participants working under interruption reported significantly more stress, higher frustration, greater time pressure and more effort. Their summary is worth carrying around: people compensate for interruptions by working faster, but this comes at a price.

That reframes the everyday experience of a fragmented day. If you judge it by output, nothing obviously went wrong. If you judge it by how you feel at six o'clock, a great deal did. The damage is invisible in the deliverable and completely visible in the person.

The same study also found that individual differences mattered — personality measures of openness to experience and need for personal structure predicted the disruption cost — which is a reminder that the right amount of protection differs between people. Someone who needs a lot of personal structure pays more for the same interruption than someone who does not.

## Define the end of the session, not just the start

The second condition is a boundary. Open-ended work is disproportionately hard to begin, because starting it is a commitment of unknown size, and the mind is good at noticing that.

A session with a defined end is a smaller promise. It is also the only kind that can be repeated reliably, which matters because repetition is where the actual learning comes from. The IES practice guide *Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning* puts "space learning over time" first among its seven recommendations, and spacing is only available if a single session is small enough to fit into an ordinary week without a negotiation.

Two practical forms this takes:

- **A count, not a clock.** "Ten problems" ends when the tenth is done. "Twenty minutes" ends when you look up, which is a decision you have to keep making.
- **A next action, not a project.** "Revise the chapter" has no first move. "Work the six exercises on page 81" starts itself. A specified task competes far better against a notification than an unspecified one, because the notification does not have to beat your motivation — only your uncertainty about what to do next.

## Judge the session by errors, not by feel

The third condition is how you evaluate what happened, and this is where most people go wrong in an interesting way.

Feeling focused is a poor measure of being focused, because [speed and accuracy trade against each other](/blog/what-processing-speed-means/) and a fast, fluent session can be one where the error rate quietly climbed. Vallesi and colleagues' meta-analysis of sustained-attention tasks makes the trade-off visible: comparing younger and older adults across twelve studies, older adults were substantially slower on go trials but produced fewer false alarms and showed more slowing after errors. The authors read that as a more prudent strategy in the use of attentional resources — not a deficit, a different setting on the same dial.

Applied to your own work, the rule is: record what you got right and how long it took, and compare like with like. A session that felt sharp and produced more mistakes was not a good session, however it felt.

## Design details that protect a session

We build practice apps, so it is worth saying which design decisions actually help here and which are decoration.

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and three of its properties are genuinely about protecting concentration rather than about content:

- **Gameplay routes disable the back-swipe gesture**, so a stray swipe cannot abandon a run in the middle of a question. A small thing, but losing a run to a gesture is exactly the kind of avoidable interruption that makes people stop opening an app.
- **Every timed sprint has an untimed counterpart.** The accuracy rounds run without a clock, with hints and an explanation after each question. If clock pressure is the thing breaking your concentration, removing the clock is a more direct fix than trying to tolerate it.
- **It works with the network off.** Gameplay is generated on the device, so a practice session does not require a connection. The practical use of that is not travel; it is that turning the network off removes the interruption source entirely, and a session that cannot be interrupted needs no willpower to protect.

What it does not have is equally worth stating: there is no attention test in the app, no reaction test, and no assessment of any kind. It records score, accuracy, streak, response time and personal bests, and nothing in that list is a measurement of concentration. If you want the evidence on whether attention drills change anything beyond themselves, we set it out in [attention span exercises and what they do not fix](/blog/attention-span-exercises/).

## A setup you can build in five minutes

Rather than a resolution, a checklist:

1. **Put the interrupting device out of reach**, not face-down within reach. The difference is whether checking it is a decision or a reflex.
2. **Write the next action down** in specific enough terms that starting it requires no thought.
3. **Set the session size as a count** of items, problems or pages.
4. **Decide in advance what you will do when you get stuck**, because "get stuck" is when most people reach for a distraction. Having an answer — skip it and mark it, or spend two minutes then move on — removes the moment of drift.
5. **Log the result**: what you finished, and how accurate it was. This is the same discipline that makes any practice record meaningful, and our guide to [building a daily practice habit](/blog/building-a-daily-practice-habit/) covers keeping it going on the days it is inconvenient.

For self-directed study specifically, where the absence of a syllabus makes the "what next" problem much worse, our guide to [studying on your own without a syllabus](/blog/how-to-study-history-on-your-own/) works through structuring a longer project into sessions of this shape.

## What concentration is not

It is not a fixed personal quantity you either have or lack, and it is not primarily a capacity to be expanded by exercises. The background on what working memory is and how little of it practice actually changes is in our guide to [working memory and training](/blog/working-memory-and-training/), and the rest of this subject is collected in the [memory and attention hub](/blog/topics/memory-and-attention/) alongside the wider [education and brain training category](/blog/category/education-brain/). The short sessions and untimed rounds described above are in [Mental Math & Memory Games](/apps/mental-math-memory-games/).

The most encouraging finding in this area is also the least glamorous. If concentration were mostly a trait, there would be little to do about it. Because it is mostly a set of conditions, the fixes are boring, immediate and under your control.
