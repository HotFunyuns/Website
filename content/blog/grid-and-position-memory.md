A grid memory task asks you to hold *where* something was, not only *what* it was. That is a different job from recalling a list: the items arrive as a layout rather than a sequence, position has to be encoded alongside identity, and the question at recall points at a place — which cell held the 7, which squares lit up, in what order. Location is its own kind of content, and most of the tricks that rescue a string of digits do nothing for a grid.

## What the task is actually asking

Strip a grid drill down and it has three stages. A layout appears for a fixed exposure. It disappears. Then you are probed — sometimes for the whole layout, more often for one part of it.

That last stage is what separates grid tasks from list tasks. A digit-span trial asks for everything, in order. A grid trial can ask for one cell, which means you cannot get partial credit by producing the items you happen to remember; you have to have bound each item to its place. Psychologists call this *binding*: the item and its location are stored as a pair, and a memory that keeps one without the other fails the question.

This is also why "I remember there was a 7 somewhere" is such a common experience on grid drills. The identity survived and the binding did not.

## Why location behaves differently from order

A list hands you an order for free. The digits arrive one after another, so you can rehearse them as a spoken run, give them a rhythm, and chunk them into groups of two or three. A grid hands you no order at all. Nine cells have no natural first or last, and until you impose a reading order there is nothing to rehearse.

The two systems are related but not interchangeable, and the evidence is specific about where they diverge. In a review of forward and backward order recall, Donolato, Giofrè and Mammarella report overwhelming evidence for a distinction between forward and backward digit span, but no clear evidence for the same distinction in spatial span. Reversing a spoken list is reliably harder than repeating it; reversing a spatial sequence is not reliably harder in the same way. That asymmetry is a good reason to stop treating "memory" as one skill with one dial.

Capacity is the other constraint, and it is smaller than most people assume. Nelson Cowan's review of the working-memory literature argues for a central store limited to roughly three to five meaningful chunks in young adults. A 4×4 grid holds sixteen cells. You are not going to hold sixteen things. What improves on a grid drill is almost entirely how few things you can turn sixteen cells into.

## Strategies that work on a grid and not on a list

Three encoding habits do most of the work, and each one is about manufacturing structure that the display does not supply.

**Impose a scan order and never change it.** Left to right, top to bottom, every single time. A fixed route converts a two-dimensional layout into a one-dimensional list, which you can then rehearse the way you rehearse a phone number. The cost is that you spend part of your exposure scanning rather than looking; the benefit is that you have something to say to yourself during the delay.

**Anchor to landmark cells.** Corners and the centre are easier to name than the third cell in the second row. If two of the five filled cells sit on corners, encode those first and describe the rest relative to them — "top-left, centre, and the one just under centre". You are storing a short description instead of a picture.

**Turn scattered cells into a shape.** Four filled cells that happen to form a square, an L or a diagonal are one chunk, not four. Consider a 3×3 grid holding a 4 in the top-left cell, a 7 in the centre and a 2 in the bottom-right. Stored as three item–location pairs that is three chunks. Stored as "4-7-2 down the main diagonal" it is one chunk plus one three-digit run, which is comfortably inside the limits described above. The digits did not get easier; the description did.

None of these transfer to a spoken list, because a spoken list already has the order that they manufacture. This is the practical face of the finding that practice improves the trained task: what you are building is a layout-reading habit, and layout-reading habits apply to layouts.

## What a grid drill changes as it grows

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and its grid modes are a reasonable illustration because the difficulty dials are explicit rather than hidden. Number Grid Memory shows a grid of digits and then asks for one exact cell. Color Grid, Letter Grid, Color Position Recall, Memory Maze and Pattern Builder vary the content and the probe. Across the seven memory levels the grid itself grows and the exposure shrinks.

The table below lists the four dials that actually change between the easiest and hardest levels, which is useful because it shows that "harder" is not one thing.

| Level | Grid | Reveal time | Hold gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Beginner | 2×2 | 900 ms | 0 ms |
| Medium | 4×3 | 700 ms | 300 ms |
| Hard | 4×4 | 620 ms | 500 ms |
| Champion | up to 7×7 | 400 ms | 1,400 ms |

Read that as three separate problems stacking. More cells means more to bind. Less reveal time means less scanning, so your fixed scan order has to be faster or shorter. A longer hold gap means the binding has to survive an unfilled delay rather than being dumped straight out. If your accuracy collapses when you move up a level, it is worth working out which of the three broke, because the fix differs: a bigger grid wants better chunking, a shorter reveal wants a faster scan, a longer hold wants rehearsal.

The app records a largest-grid figure among its personal bests. That is a record of your play in one game under one set of settings. It is not a test result, it is not comparable to anyone else's number, and the app contains no assessment of any kind.

## How much to trust any single grid score

Less than you would like, and for a reason that has nothing to do with you. Grid tasks are sensitive to how the display is drawn. A 2026 study by Zhao and colleagues put distractor circles close to target locations in a computerised block-tapping task and watched hit rates fall from 78% at baseline to 70% with one crowded target and 68% with several. Their conclusion was that perceptual processes — specifically visual crowding — influence visuospatial working-memory assessment, which is a polite way of saying that part of a grid score is a fact about the graphics.

Two practical consequences follow. First, compare yourself only to yourself, on the same mode at the same level, which is the same discipline that makes any [practice record worth reading](/blog/working-memory-and-training/). Second, treat a bad run as noise until it repeats. A single number from a task this sensitive is a weak signal — a point that applies just as much to the [change detection formats](/blog/change-detection-tasks/) that ask which item vanished rather than which cell held it.

## The honest summary

Practising a grid task will make you better at grid tasks. The mechanism is not mysterious: you learn to impose an order, to anchor on landmarks, and to describe a layout in fewer pieces than it contains. That is a genuine skill and it is worth having, especially if you find spatial information slippery.

What it is not is evidence that your memory has grown. The capacity literature describes a small central store that strategy works around rather than expands, and improvements on a trained task are reliable in a way that improvements on dissimilar tasks are not. Practice, entertainment and educational activity are all reasonable descriptions of a grid drill. Standardised psychological assessment is not one of them, and no app score should be read as one.
