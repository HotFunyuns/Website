Positions in a race can change in the pit lane as well as on track, by timing a stop better than the car ahead. The two named strategies for doing that are the **undercut** and the **overcut**. Wikipedia's glossary of motorsport terms defines them in a line each: the undercut is making an earlier pit stop in an attempt to gain time on competitors, and the overcut is delaying a pit stop to gain time.

## Why stops can swap positions

In a dry Formula 1 race both cars must stop at least once, because the rules require two different dry tyre specifications, and each stop costs roughly the same time: the drive through the pit lane at its speed limit, set at **80 km/h** for the whole event under the FIA's 2026 Formula 1 sporting regulations, plus the tyre change. Because both cars pay that cost, it largely cancels out. What decides the battle is what happens **between the two stops**, when one car is on fresh tyres and the other is not.

**An illustrative pit-loss sum.** Suppose the pit lane, from entry line to exit line, is 400 m long. At 80 km/h, about 22.2 m/s, crossing it takes 18 seconds; at a racing speed of 240 km/h it would take 6. So the speed limit alone costs about 12 seconds, before adding the time the car is stationary and the time lost slowing down and accelerating. Real figures vary with each circuit's pit lane length and layout.

## The undercut

Wikipedia's pit stop article describes it: the car behind pits earlier than the leader and uses the advantage of fresh tyres to cut the gap. If the gap falls below the time lost in a stop, the leader rejoins behind when it pits.

**Worked example.** Car A leads car B by 2.0 seconds. Both lose the same time in the pit lane. B pits first; A stays out on worn tyres.

| Scenario | B's gain per lap on fresh tyres | Laps before A stops | Total gained | Result after both stops |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.8 s | 1 | 0.8 s | A still ahead by 1.2 s: undercut fails |
| 2 | 1.2 s | 2 | 2.4 s | B ahead by 0.4 s: undercut works |

The rule of thumb: the undercut works when the chaser's total gain between the stops exceeds the gap. The bigger the difference between new and worn tyres, the more powerful it is, which is why it suits races where tyres degrade quickly; [racing tyre strategy](/blog/racing-tire-strategy/) explains degradation.

## The overcut

The overcut is the reverse. The leading car stays out longer and laps fast enough on its older tyres to keep, or even extend, its advantage while the rival is on fresh tyres. Wikipedia lists when it can succeed:

- the leader's tyres are **still in good shape** when the rival pits, so it can push without being limited by degradation;
- the car that pitted **gets stuck in traffic**, wasting its fresh-tyre pace.

In terms of the table above, the overcut works when B's "gain per lap" is small or even negative, for example because B's first laps after the stop are slow, or because B rejoins behind slower cars.

## What breaks each strategy

| Factor | Effect on the undercut | Effect on the overcut |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High tyre degradation | Stronger | Weaker |
| Low degradation | Weaker | Stronger |
| Traffic after the stop | Can kill it | Helps it |
| Clear track for the leader | Weaker | Stronger |
| A safety car | Can reset everything | Can reset everything |

The last row can override all the others. A safety car or virtual safety car slows the whole field, so a stop made under it costs less relative to rivals; [the safety car explained](/blog/safety-car-explained/) covers the rules. A car that has just pitted under green flags can lose its gain if the field is then neutralised.

## Defending against an undercut

The leading car has two answers: stop on the next lap to "cover" the undercut, or stay out and try the overcut. Covering is safe if the leader's pit stop is quick and it rejoins in clear air; staying out is a bet that the old tyres still have pace. Slipstream on the straights can decide the laps in between; [slipstream and drafting](/blog/slipstream-and-drafting/) explains why.

The decision connects to the [points](/blog/racing-points-systems/) at stake: a team defending a championship lead may cover safely rather than gamble.
