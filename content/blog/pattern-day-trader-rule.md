For more than twenty years, anyone who day traded frequently in a US margin account ran into the "pattern day trader" rule and its $25,000 minimum. In 2026 that changed. FINRA's Regulatory Notice 26-10, published in April 2026, announced new intraday margin standards that replace the old day trading margin requirements in their entirety, including the pattern day trader designation and the $25,000 minimum. This guide explains what the old rule was, what replaced it, and what remains the same.

## The old rule, briefly

The SEC's investor bulletin on margin rules for day trading describes the requirements as they stood:

- **Definition.** A pattern day trader was any customer who executed **four or more day trades within five business days** in a margin account, provided the day trades were **more than 6%** of the customer's total trades in that period. Some brokers used a broader definition.
- **Minimum equity.** A pattern day trader had to keep at least **$25,000** in the account, deposited before any day trading and maintained at all times. Below it, no day trading until the account was restored.
- **Buying power.** Day trading buying power was up to four times the customer's maintenance margin excess from the previous day's close, with day trading margin calls for exceeding it.

FINRA's notice says customers and member firms had for some time voiced concern that these rules were restrictive, and FINRA requested comment on them in October 2024 before proposing the change.

## What changed in 2026

According to Regulatory Notice 26-10:

- The SEC approved FINRA's amendments to Rule 4210 replacing the day trading margin requirements with an **intraday margin rule**.
- The **effective date was 4 June 2026**. Firms needing more time may **phase in implementation until 20 October 2027**, so some brokers may still be operating under their older systems for part of that period.
- The new rule **does not change the regular maintenance margin requirements**; it supplements them.

FINRA describes the aim as giving customers more freedom to participate in the markets while ensuring they maintain equity commensurate with their market exposure at any point during the trading day.

## How the intraday margin rule works

The core idea is to measure margin during the day, not just at the close.

1. **Intraday margin deficit.** For each margin account and each day with a transaction that reduces the account's intraday margin level, the firm determines the deficit, broadly the largest shortfall between the margin required and the equity in the account after such transactions. Firms may monitor in real time and block trades that would create deficits, but they may also make a single calculation, as they do for maintenance margin.
2. **Satisfying a deficit.** The firm must require the deficit to be satisfied as promptly as possible, by net deposits or other increases in the account's margin level. FINRA's investor guidance describes the same choice: deposit funds or liquidate positions.
3. **The 90-day freeze.** If a customer makes a practice of failing to satisfy deficits promptly and leaves one unsatisfied past the fifth business day, the firm must prevent the customer from creating or increasing a short position or debit balance for 90 calendar days, or until the deficit is satisfied. The notice exempts small deficits (no more than the lesser of 5% of account equity or $1,000) and extraordinary circumstances.

## What has not changed

- **Margin is still borrowing.** FINRA's June 2026 guidance on frequent intraday trading warns that margin trading carries the potential to lose more than your original investment, and that you should never fund it with essential assets.
- **Minimum equity for margin.** That guidance describes a minimum of $2,000 in equity to trade on margin, with firms free to require more.
- **Maintenance requirements** and [margin calls](/blog/margin-call-explained/) work as before.
- **Cash accounts** still face settlement-based rules such as free-riding and good faith violations; see [cash account versus margin account](/blog/cash-account-vs-margin-account/) and [T+1 settlement](/blog/trade-settlement-t-plus-1/).

FINRA's guidance also states that strategies involving frequent trading on margin generally are not appropriate for investors with limited financial resources, limited trading experience or low [risk tolerance](/blog/risk-tolerance-explained/), and suggests talking to your firm about how intraday margin works in your account and to a tax professional about the tax effects of frequent trading.
