If you are stuck on a sector, the honest question is not "which mode is better" but "what exactly is killing me". Endless mode and a campaign sector train different things, and choosing between them without naming the failure is how players spend a fortnight getting demonstrably better at something that was never the obstacle.

The short version: endless is efficient practice for the general problem of surviving dense formations under rising pressure. It is not practice at all for the specific problem of a boss whose phases you have not learned, because the campaign is the only place those bosses exist.

## What each mode actually is

A campaign sector is a designed sequence. The same enemy types arrive in the same order, the hazards sit where they sat last time, and the fight at the end has a fixed structure you are meant to learn. Progress is binary — you clear it or you do not — and the run ends when the sector does.

An endless run removes that structure. In Space Galaxy Attack Hardcore, which is our own app, the mode drops the campaign entirely and keeps sending waves, so the only goal is surviving as long as possible and pushing a score. The pressure rises, the composition varies, and nothing terminates the run except failure.

Those are two different practice environments, and it turns out there is a substantial research literature about exactly that distinction — not about video games, but about how people acquire motor skills in general.

## The uncomfortable finding about practice that feels good

The relevant idea is called contextual interference. In a low-interference schedule you repeat one task many times in a block before moving to the next. In a high-interference schedule you interleave tasks so that consecutive attempts are at different things.

The consistent finding across decades of work is that these two schedules trade off in opposite directions. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of transfer in motor learning summarised the pattern directly: random practice order hinders performance during acquisition and facilitates retention and transfer, while blocked order facilitates performance and hinders retention and transfer. The overall advantage for random practice on transfer was medium in size, and larger in laboratory settings than in applied ones, where it was small and not statistically significant.

A companion meta-analysis on retention by the same group found the same reversal: participants practising in blocked schedules outperformed random-schedule participants during learning, and the ranking flipped when retention was tested later.

Two caveats before anyone over-applies this. These studies are about motor skill acquisition in laboratory and applied settings, not about shoot 'em ups, and nobody has run the experiment on this or any similar game. And the effects varied considerably by population — the same review reported large effects in older adults, medium in adults and negligible in the youngest participants. What survives the caveats is the direction of the trade, which is robust enough to be worth reasoning from: **the practice that produces your best numbers today is not reliably the practice that produces your best performance next week.**

## Why that predicts a specific mistake

Put that finding next to how players actually decide what to play, and the trap becomes obvious.

Players choose the mode where they are doing well. It is a completely reasonable heuristic — visible improvement is the only feedback available — and if performance during a session is a poor proxy for learning, it systematically selects the wrong mode. Repeating one sector produces a rising clear rate within an evening, which feels unmistakably like progress. Varied, unpredictable pressure produces messier results that look like a plateau.

There is a second version of the same error in the other direction, and it is more common with endless modes specifically: grinding a survival run is comfortable because it cannot be failed in any meaningful sense. A run that ends at four minutes instead of six is not a defeat, it is a number. Retreating from a campaign sector you keep losing into a mode where losing has no status is avoidance wearing the clothes of practice.

## Match the mode to the failure

The decision becomes tractable once you diagnose first. Watch a couple of your own deaths and place them in one of three buckets.

**You are dying to formation density.** Projectiles from several directions, no safe lane found in time, deaths that happen in the middle of a wave rather than at the end of one. This is the general problem, and endless mode is the most efficient environment for it: more waves per minute than the campaign, in varied combinations, with no cutscene or menu between attempts. This is the case where the research points clearly — varied exposure to the thing you are bad at.

**You are dying to one specific boss.** The waves are fine, you arrive with health intact, and the fight goes wrong in the same place every time. Endless mode contains no bosses, so it offers zero repetitions of the actual problem. Go back to the sector, and go back with a plan rather than a hope — which usually means a target-priority plan, covered in [boss weak points and module targeting](/blog/boss-weak-points-and-module-targeting/).

**You are dying because the ship is not yet equipped for the sector.** This is not a practice problem at all, and it is worth separating cleanly from the other two so you do not try to solve an economy problem with technique. Either mode earns upgrade currency; pick whichever earns it faster for you and stop treating the session as training.

## Difficulty selection is something you are already doing

Research on dynamic difficulty adjustment starts from the premise that a game is most engaging when its difficulty matches the player's skill — challenged without being overwhelmed — and builds automated systems to hold players in that band. Mode selection is the manual version of the same mechanism, and the modes in a shooter are a coarse but real difficulty control.

That framing makes the third option visible. In Space Galaxy Attack Hardcore the three modes are Normal for classic arcade progression, Hardcore for tougher enemies and tighter boss openings, and Endless for chasing a score. Hardcore raises enemy durability, projectile speed and formation complexity, and gives bosses extra attack patterns with fewer openings — which is to say it assumes you already know the fight. Attempting to learn a boss on Hardcore means learning a fight and surviving it simultaneously, with the learning windows removed. That is a difficulty mismatch in the exact sense the research describes, and it is a slower route to competence than clearing the same fight on Normal first. What the tiers change in general is covered in [shmup difficulty tiers](/blog/shmup-difficulty-tiers/).

## A practical schedule

If you want something concrete, this is a defensible structure that follows from everything above rather than from taste.

Open with a short endless run as a warm-up, treating it as exposure rather than as a score attempt. Move to the campaign sector you are stuck on and take a small number of deliberate attempts with one specific thing you are trying to change — an ability held back for a particular moment, a different opening target on the boss. Stop when the attempts stop being deliberate. Finish, if you want a result to look at, with a scoring run, played as a scoring run, which is a different activity again and one we treat separately in [playing for a total rather than survival](/blog/score-attack-strategy/).

The upgrade economy makes all of this low-stakes: gold collected in missions goes into permanent ship upgrades, so a failed run still moves the ship forward.
