The Columbian Exchange is the transfer of crops, animals, technologies, people, cultures and diseases between the Americas and Afro-Eurasia after 1492. It is usually taught as a tidy two-column list of foods. The largest fact in it is not a food: it is the destruction of Indigenous populations across two continents, and the magnitude of that destruction is still argued about by people who have read the same evidence.

Both halves belong in the same article, because separating them is how the comfortable version gets written.

## What moved, and in which direction

Nathan Nunn and Nancy Qian, writing in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, treat the exchange as one connected process covering disease, food crops, technologies, populations and ideas — and make the point that the consequences for the Old World have been studied less than the consequences for the Americas. Maize, potatoes, cassava, tomatoes, chillies and cacao moved east. Wheat, rice, sugar cane, cattle, pigs, sheep and horses moved west, along with the labour systems that grew sugar and the pathogens that travelled with people.

The potato is the cleanest illustration of how uneven this was. The International Potato Center records that the potato originated and was first domesticated in the Andes, and that more than 4,000 varieties of native potato are still grown there, mostly within that region. What spread outward was a thin slice of that diversity. A crop with thousands of local varieties adapted to Andean altitude became, elsewhere, a handful of cultivars — which is one reason later potato failures in Europe were so severe.

Adoption was also slow and uneven. New crops had to fit existing agricultural calendars, tastes, tax systems and land tenure before they mattered. The gap between arrival and significance is often a century or more, and dating that gap properly is the same problem our guide to [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) describes for any diffusion.

## The population question, honestly

Here is where popular accounts usually pick a number. The number is contested, and the contest is about method, not about who sounds most dramatic.

Published estimates of the pre-1492 population of the Americas differ by a large factor, and each estimate implies a different death toll — that arithmetic is unavoidable. Robert Kelly and colleagues, writing in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* in 2025, take an approach worth understanding: they use summed radiocarbon date distributions to map where and when populations rose and fell across North America, but explicitly decline to convert that into absolute population numbers, on the grounds that date frequencies align more closely with energy consumption than with headcounts.

What they do find is that decline was not synchronised. Regional declines began at very different dates — from around 770 CE in some places to after 1500 CE in others — and they identify a relative population loss of roughly 30 per cent between 1150 and 1500 CE, describing that as a minimal figure. In other words, parts of North America were already losing population before Europeans arrived, for reasons that have nothing to do with 1492, and a model that attributes every decline to contact will overstate contact's effect in those regions.

None of this makes the catastrophe smaller. It makes single-number claims harder to defend, which is the correct outcome when the underlying evidence cannot support them.

## Disease did not act alone

The most consequential recent correction concerns timing. Terry Jones and colleagues analysed 33,715 mortality records from central California and found that the plague-like mortality pattern did not appear at first contact. It appeared after permanent Spanish missions were established around 1770. In the northern mission area their figures show roughly 43,285 people at contact and about 7,800 by 1834, counting mission residents and those who had escaped.

That is a very large collapse, and it happens after sustained colonial settlement rather than after distant contact. The mechanism was not a pathogen crossing an ocean and finding a defenceless population. It was a pathogen encountering people who had been concentrated into settlements, separated from their food systems, subjected to forced labour, and prevented from leaving.

This matters for how the whole episode is described. "Virgin soil epidemics" framing, applied uncritically, turns a colonial process into a natural disaster and removes the decisions from it. The decisions — to displace, to enslave, to congregate populations, to seize land and labour — were made by people, and the evidence for them sits in colonial administrative records. Where communities survived, that too had causes worth studying rather than treating as luck.

## What the exchange did to everyone else

Nunn and Qian's emphasis on the Old World is a useful counterweight to a purely Americas-centred account. American crops supported population growth across Europe, Africa and Asia; American silver reorganised global trade; and the plantation economies that grew American and Asian crops in the Americas drove the trafficking of enslaved Africans on a scale that reshaped three continents.

That last connection is not a side-effect. The demand for labour to work land taken from Indigenous peoples is the link between the two great atrocities of the period, and any account of the Columbian Exchange that stops at foodstuffs has left it out.

For the long-distance trade networks that preceded all of this, our explainer on [the Silk Road](/blog/the-silk-road-for-beginners/) covers the overland Eurasian routes that moved goods, ideas and disease for centuries before an Atlantic crossing was possible. The comparison is instructive: exchange across Eurasia was gradual, bidirectional and rarely catastrophic. The Atlantic exchange was none of those things, and the difference is largely about the speed and the violence.
