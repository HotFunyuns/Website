Pressing "buy" in a trading app feels instant, as if you were plugged straight into the market. You are not. Investor.gov, the SEC's investor education site, makes the point bluntly: many investors who trade online assume they have a direct connection to the securities markets, but they don't. Between your tap and your shares sits a chain of firms, rules and prices, and knowing the chain explains most of what you see on a trading screen.

## Two markets: where shares are born and where they change hands

Wikipedia's article on the secondary market draws the distinction:

- **The primary market** is the initial sale of a security by its issuer, such as shares sold in an [initial public offering](/blog/what-is-an-ipo/); the proceeds go to the issuer.
- **The secondary market** is all the trading after that, where one investor sells to another, with little impact on the company that issued the shares.

Wikipedia names stock exchanges such as the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq as the most visible secondary markets, and they are the subject of the rest of this guide.

## The cast

| Participant | Role |
| --- | --- |
| You | Decide what to buy or sell and what kind of order to use |
| Broker | Holds your account, takes your order and decides where to send it |
| Exchange | A regulated marketplace, such as the New York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq, that matches buy and sell orders |
| Market maker | A firm that stands ready to buy or sell a stock at publicly quoted prices |
| Electronic communications network (ECN) | A system that automatically matches buy and sell orders at specified prices |
| Regulators | The SEC writes and enforces securities law; FINRA oversees broker-dealers |

## What happens to your order

According to Investor.gov, your broker may send your order to:

1. **The exchange** where the stock is listed, or another exchange;
2. **A market maker**, which may fill it from its own quotes;
3. **An ECN**, especially for limit orders; or
4. **Its own inventory**, a practice called internalisation.

Some market makers pay brokers for sending them orders, a practice called **payment for order flow**, which Investor.gov describes as perhaps a penny or more per share. Whatever route is used, brokers have a duty to seek the **best execution** reasonably available for their customers' orders, and to weigh the possibility of a better price against the extra time it might take.

## How the price is set

There is no single official price for a stock. At any moment there is:

- the **bid**, the highest price someone is currently willing to pay, and
- the **ask**, the lowest price someone is currently willing to accept.

The gap between them is the [bid–ask spread](/blog/bid-ask-spread-explained/). A trade happens when a buyer and seller agree, and a headline "price" may simply be the last trade.

Investor.gov stresses two consequences. A market order generally executes at or near the current ask (for a buy) or bid (for a sell), and **the last-traded price is not necessarily the price a market order will get**. And because quotes cover only a specific number of shares and prices move, you may not receive the price you saw on screen. That is the whole case for choosing an order type deliberately, the subject of [market orders versus limit orders](/blog/market-order-vs-limit-order/).

Wikipedia's article on market trends sums up what sits behind every price change: the price of an asset such as a stock is determined by supply and demand.

## When the trade actually completes

Execution is not the end. The trade must **settle**: cash and shares must actually change hands. FINRA explains that, as of May 2024, the standard settlement cycle for most US securities is the next business day after the trade, **T+1**. Sell on Tuesday and the trade settles on Wednesday. [T+1 settlement](/blog/trade-settlement-t-plus-1/) explains why that date matters, especially in a cash account.

## When the market is open

The New York Stock Exchange's core trading session runs from 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time on business days, with early and late sessions on some of its markets and closures on market holidays. Trading outside the core session works differently and carries its own risks, covered in [stock market hours](/blog/stock-market-hours-explained/).
