A to-do list and a daily planner look similar on paper, but they answer different questions. A list answers **what** needs doing. A planner answers **when** you will do it, and in doing so shows whether the day has room for it. Choosing between them, or combining them, comes down to what your days are like.

## The to-do list

A to-do list collects tasks so you do not have to hold them in your head. Wikipedia's article on time management describes the classic ways of ranking one:

- **Numbered by importance**, then done in that order as time allows, the advice attributed to consultant Ivy Lee.
- **ABC priorities**, advocated by Alan Lakein in 1973: A for the most important, B next, C least.
- **The Eisenhower matrix**, which sorts tasks by urgent or not and important or not: do urgent important tasks now, give important non-urgent ones an end date, delegate urgent unimportant ones and drop the rest.

The same article quotes David Allen, creator of Getting Things Done: "it takes more energy to keep something inside your head than outside".

## The daily planner

A daily planner puts tasks into time slots alongside appointments. One technique behind it is **timeboxing**, which Wikipedia defines as allocating a **maximum unit of time** to an activity. Wikipedia explains that with timeboxing the deadline is fixed, so the scope has to be reduced instead. In a day plan, that means that when the 9:00–10:30 block for a report ends, you stop, and what is unfinished becomes a decision rather than an overrun that eats the afternoon.

## To-do list and planner side by side

| | To-do list | Daily planner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Answers | What needs doing | When it gets done |
| Best for | Many small tasks, unpredictable days | Fixed appointments, focused work, deadlines |
| Shows overload? | No: a list can grow without limit | Yes: the hours run out |
| Handles interruptions | Well: pick the next task | Poorly: one overrun shifts every block |
| Weak spot | Important work gets crowded out by easy tasks | Plans built on optimistic estimates |

## Why plans run long

Any planner has to estimate time, and estimates are biased. Wikipedia's article on the **planning fallacy**, first proposed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1979, describes predictions of how long a future task will take as showing an **optimism bias that underestimates the time needed**, which sometimes happens even when people know similar tasks took longer before. It cites a 1994 study in which 37 psychology students estimated their theses would take **33.9 days** on average; they actually took **55.5 days**, and only about **30%** finished in the time they predicted.

**A worked illustration.** Suppose you list tasks you estimate at 5 hours in total, for a day with 6 free hours. It looks comfortable. If your estimates ran as optimistic as those students' (55.5 ÷ 33.9 ≈ 1.64), the work would take just over **8 hours** (5 × 1.64 ≈ 8.2), more than the day holds. One study cannot tell you your own ratio, but the lesson stands: leave buffers between blocks, and compare your estimates with actual times for a week.

## Which days suit which tool

**Choose a to-do list if** your day is shaped by other people's requests, your tasks are mostly short, or you work in gaps between meetings.

**Choose a planner if** you have deep work that keeps getting crowded out, several fixed appointments, or a deadline that needs steady progress.

## A list-plus-planner routine

You do not have to pick one. A simple routine uses both:

1. **Keep one master list** of everything, with priorities.
2. **Each morning, pick two or three tasks** that matter most.
3. **Give those tasks time blocks**, with buffers, around your appointments.
4. **Use the list for the gaps**: short tasks between blocks.
5. **At the end of the day, review**: move what is unfinished, and drop what no longer matters.

There is evidence that the scheduling step helps beyond the day itself. In 2011 experiments by Masicampo and Baumeister, **unfinished goals caused intrusive thoughts**, but letting people **make specific plans** for them **eliminated** the effect. The authors suggest that committing to a specific plan may free cognitive resources for other things.
