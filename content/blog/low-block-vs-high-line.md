**A low block defends deep and compact near its own goal; a high line pushes the defence far up the pitch to squeeze the space the opponent can play in.** In soccer (association football), the choice between them comes down to one question: would you rather leave space behind your defence or in front of it? A high line wins the ball higher and makes the pitch small, but leaves a large area behind the last defender. A low block protects that area and the space near goal, but hands the opponent the ball and the territory.

Neither is the "brave" or the "negative" option. Both are answers to the same problem — ten outfield players cannot cover the whole pitch — and the right one depends on the players you have.

## The trade-off at a glance

| | Low block | Mid block | High line |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Where the back line sits | Close to its own penalty area | Around its own half | Well up the pitch, sometimes near halfway |
| What it protects | The space near goal and behind the defence | A balance of both | The space in front of the defence, the opponent's build-up |
| What it concedes | Territory, possession, shots from distance | Some of each | The space between the last defender and the goalkeeper |
| Where the ball is won | Deep, far from the opponent's goal | Midfield | High, close to the opponent's goal |
| What it demands | Compactness, discipline, an outlet to counter | Coordination | Pressure on the ball, recovery pace, a line that steps together |

The middle column matters because real teams move between heights during a match. The labels describe where a team settles when it is set, not a fixed position.

## Why the offside law makes a high line possible

A high line is only workable because of [Law 11](https://www.theifab.com/laws/latest/offside/). An attacker in the opponents' half who is nearer the goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent at the moment a team-mate plays the ball, and who then becomes involved, commits an offside offence, punished by an indirect free kick. A back line that steps up together just before a pass is played can leave attackers stranded in offside positions.

Three details from the Law shape how a high line behaves:

- **The moment is the pass.** Position is judged when the ball is played, so a defender who steps up a fraction late plays an attacker onside. A high line works as a unit or not at all.
- **Nobody is offside in their own half.** An attacker who is in their own half when the ball is played cannot be offside, which limits how high a line can sit before the trap stops working.
- **No offside from a corner, goal kick or throw-in.** At those restarts the line cannot be used to catch attackers on the first ball, which is one reason corners are defended with marking schemes rather than a line.

[How the offside rule works](/blog/how-the-offside-rule-works/) covers the Law in full.

## What a high line needs

The most important requirement is not pace — it is pressure on the ball. The tactics site [Spielverlagerung](https://spielverlagerung.com/2017/03/05/pressing-counterpressing-and-counterattacking/) puts it plainly: a team that played a high defensive line without pressuring the ball "would concede a lot of goals because of allowing long passes into the space behind the defenders". A passer with time and space can pick the run in behind; a passer under pressure usually cannot.

After that come the players behind the press. Centre-backs need the recovery speed to turn and chase a ball over the top. And the goalkeeper has to defend the space between the line and the goal — the sweeper-keeper role that the [guide to soccer formations](/blog/soccer-formations-explained/) describes as the thing that turns an aggressive line from a gamble into a system.

**How a high line gets beaten:** a well-timed run that beats the step-up, a pass played early from deep before pressure arrives, a switch of play to the side the defence has left, or a ball over the top when the press fails to reach the passer.

## What a low block needs

A low block trades territory for safety. When a team cannot get pressure on the ball, Spielverlagerung's alternative is to drop deeper and more centrally, protecting the space near goal and forcing play backwards or wide. As the ball moves forward and the defence drops, the space behind the defenders shrinks until there is little left to attack.

That only works with **compactness** — little space between the lines and between players, so a pass through the block is hard to find. Spielverlagerung separates several kinds: spatial compactness within the shape, tactical compactness that offers no easy options through it, and the staggered cover that lets one defender step out while another fills in.

The second requirement is a plan for the ball once it is won. Defending deep means winning possession far from the opponent's goal, so the attack that follows has a long way to go — and its best chance is to go quickly.

### The evidence for counter-attacks

In a [study of 1,971 possessions](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0226978) from 20 randomly chosen Premier League matches, González-Rodenas and colleagues found counterattacks made up only 9.5% of possessions but had about 3.4 times the odds of creating a scoring opportunity that combinative attacks did (odds ratio 3.43). Direct attacks were less effective than combinative ones (odds ratio 0.47). The authors explain the counter-attack's edge by its aim, to "exploit imbalances in the opponent's defense", and caution that their results reflect that competition and cannot be extrapolated to other contexts.

### The cost of giving up the ball

A ten-season study of LaLiga by [Prieto-González and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12244413/) points to the price. Teams that allowed opponents more touches and more possession tended to concede more goals — moderate correlations of about 0.41 for touches and 0.36 for possession — and opponents' touches and shots conceded together explained about a quarter of the variation in goals conceded. Set-piece measures showed little relationship with goals conceded over a season. Correlation is not cause, though: team quality could drive both, so the study cannot say whether giving up the ball makes a team concede more or whether teams that concede more also tend to give up the ball.

## Marking: zonal, man and everything between

Both blocks need a marking scheme, and the labels are less tidy than they sound. In **man marking**, defenders follow particular opponents — rigidly, or passing them on to team-mates as players swap positions. In **zonal marking**, defenders guard areas and shift together towards the ball, which keeps the block compact but asks for constant movement as the ball circulates. Spielverlagerung describes hybrids in between: zones that turn into man marking once an opponent enters them, and schemes oriented around the ball's likely next destination. Most teams mix them, and different players in the same team can be doing different things at the same moment.

## Choosing: five questions for any squad

1. **Can your front players get pressure on the ball?** If not, a high line is exposed by every long pass.
2. **Do your centre-backs have recovery speed?** A high line needs defenders who can win a race back to goal.
3. **Is your goalkeeper comfortable outside the area?** Someone has to own the space behind a high line.
4. **Do you have an outlet?** A low block without a quick, direct option on the counter becomes a long afternoon of defending.
5. **What is the score?** A team protecting a lead and a team chasing a goal rarely want the same height.

Answering those honestly matters more than any preference for one style. A plain shape the players can run beats an admired one they cannot, and [clean sheets explained](/blog/clean-sheets-explained/) is a reminder that how often a team concedes depends on far more than its back four.
