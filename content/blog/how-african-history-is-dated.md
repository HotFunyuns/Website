African history is dated by combining five methods that are independent of one another: oral tradition collected under controlled conditions, radiocarbon and stratigraphy from excavation, historical linguistics, written sources produced within Africa, and external accounts written by visitors. No single one carries a chronology on its own. Confidence comes from convergence — when a king list, a radiocarbon range and an Arabic chronicle point to the same decades, the date is firm; when they do not, the honest output is a range with an attribution.

## The assumption that has to go first

A reader who assumes that societies without alphabetic writing have no recoverable history has absorbed a nineteenth-century position, not a finding. That position was racist in its foundation and it had institutional backing in European universities, where it shaped what was taught and what was thought worth funding. It is also factually wrong about large parts of the continent, which were producing written documents in Ge'ez and in Arabic script for centuries.

The correction was deliberate and it is documented. UNESCO records that in 1964, as African nations gained independence, it brought together leading African and international experts to launch the *General History of Africa*, an initiative to reframe the continent's history from an African perspective. The series now extends to eleven volumes and carries an associated programme for bringing that history into national education systems. Reframing, in that context, means more than adding chapters: it means deciding whose evidence counts.

## Oral tradition, collected under conditions

Oral tradition is not folklore with dates attached. Historians working with it record multiple independent versions of a narrative, note who has the right to recite it and in what setting, identify passages with a fixed formal structure that resists drift, and separate the parts that function as genealogy or charter from the parts that function as explanation. The result is then tested against everything else available.

What oral tradition supplies best is sequence and relationship: who succeeded whom, which lineage split from which, which settlement preceded which. What it rarely supplies unaided is a calendar year. King lists help here, because a list of reigns can be turned into elapsed time if the average reign length can be estimated and if the list can be anchored at one end by an externally dated event. Both of those steps carry error, and the error compounds backwards, which is why chronologies built this way are quoted as ranges that widen with depth. The general principle — that every dating method has a characteristic error and a characteristic failure mode — is set out in [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/).

## Radiocarbon and the southern African sequence

Excavation supplies absolute dates independent of any narrative. At Great Zimbabwe, radiocarbon dates from deposits associated with the stone enclosures, cross-checked against imported goods whose own dates are known, produced the chronology UNESCO now records: the site is described as a unique testimony to the Bantu civilization of the Shona between the eleventh and fifteenth centuries, covering nearly 80 hectares, and functioning as an important trading centre.

That attribution matters because the site spent decades being publicly credited to almost anyone else. UNESCO's own description still notes the old legend that made it the capital of the Queen of Sheba. Attributing monumental African architecture to distant outsiders was not a harmless eccentricity; it was one of the working assumptions the *General History of Africa* was created to dislodge.

The finer sequence remains genuinely open. A 2014 study in *PLOS ONE* led by researchers at the University of Zimbabwe and the country's National Museums and Monuments presented new evidence from Mapela Hill and argued that the conventional model — in which socio-political complexity emerges with the K2-to-Mapungubwe transition around 1200 to 1220 CE, and passes from Mapungubwe to Great Zimbabwe and then to Khami — is, in their words, clouded in controversy. Readers should note that this is a live scholarly debate among specialists, not a settled story with a single date.

## Historical linguistics: dating movement from words

Languages carry their own evidence. Comparing sound changes and vocabulary across a family lets linguists reconstruct how it branched, and statistical methods can put approximate dates on the branch points when they are calibrated against archaeology.

The best-known application is the Bantu expansion. A 2015 study in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* built a dated family tree of about 400 Bantu languages and used it to trace the routes taken. It places the start of the expansion out of West Central Africa around 5,000 years ago, finds that the movement followed savannah corridors rather than crossing unfamiliar rainforest, and reports that migration rates slowed by an average of about 300 years where speakers did enter rainforest. Older attempts to date language splits from a fixed rate of vocabulary loss — glottochronology in its original form — are not considered reliable, because the rate is not fixed. The modern approach states its results as models with error, and that is how they should be read.

## Written sources, African and foreign

Large parts of the continent are documented from the inside. UNESCO records Aksum's ruins as dating from between the first and thirteenth centuries CE, with monolithic obelisks, giant stelae and royal tombs, and notes that Ethiopian emperors continued to be crowned there long after the kingdom's political decline in the tenth century. Ethiopian manuscript traditions in Ge'ez extend the written record over centuries.

In the Sahel, Timbuktu was, in UNESCO's description, founded in the fifth century and reached its economic and cultural height in the fifteenth and sixteenth, with the University of Sankore, 180 Koranic schools and a manuscript trade running alongside the trade in salt and gold. The Great Mosque of Djingareyber traces its initial construction to Mansa Musa's return from pilgrimage and was rebuilt and enlarged between 1570 and 1583. Manuscript preservation and digitisation projects, including those documented by the Hill Museum & Manuscript Library across Africa, the Middle East and Asia, have made a great deal of this material consultable.

External accounts — Arabic geographers, later Portuguese and other European records — supply dates and names that local sources sometimes omit, and they carry the outlook of the people who wrote them. Distinguishing a document made inside a society from one made about it is the basic skill covered in [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/), and it does more work in African history than almost anywhere else.

## The practical upshot

When you meet a date in African history, ask which method produced it. A radiocarbon range is a measurement with stated error. A regnal date derived from a king list is an estimate that widens as it goes back. A date from a chronicle is as good as the chronicle's distance from the event. A date from a linguistic model is a model. All four are legitimate; they are not interchangeable, and a timeline that prints them as identical numbers is hiding the difference.
