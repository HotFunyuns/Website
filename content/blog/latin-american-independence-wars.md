Latin America did not win independence. Roughly a dozen separate territories did, at different times, by different means, and with different outcomes — and a large number of the people involved fought to prevent it. The single narrative arc running from Simón Bolívar's campaigns to a continent of republics is a teaching convenience that flattens most of what actually happened.

The scholarship reflects that. Reviewing a decade of research in the *Latin American Research Review*, Victor Uribe describes a field that had moved away from political narrative towards structural analysis, the Spanish imperial response, regional variation and popular participation — and that treats the independence period as running from roughly 1810 to about 1840 rather than terminating neatly at a founding date.

## The precondition and the trigger are not the same thing

It is worth separating two questions that get merged. What made the colonial order vulnerable, and what actually set the wars off?

The vulnerability was structural and long-standing. Alejandra Irigoin's study of Spanish colonial fiscal legacies describes a system in which the bodies that collected revenue and the bodies that spent it were constitutionally asymmetric, and in which representation for subjects and territories was ambiguous. That is a description of a state that functioned while its central authority was unquestioned and had no obvious way to reconstitute itself if that authority disappeared.

The trigger was the collapse of that authority after 1808, when the Spanish monarchy ceased to be able to govern. Local juntas formed to hold power in the king's name. Some of those juntas became independence movements; others did not; several fought each other. The distinction matters because "Spanish rule became illegitimate in 1808" explains why a crisis began, and does not explain why Mexico's war lasted eleven years, why Andean campaigns were still running in 1824, or why Brazil's separation from Portugal took a different form altogether.

## Who was actually fighting

The most useful corrective to the Bolívar-centred version is that Spanish Americans fought in large numbers on the royalist side.

Luz Marina Arias and Luis De la Calle demonstrate this in an unusual way. Studying Mexico's war of 1810–1821, they use the organisation of royalist militias as a municipal-level variable and trace its consequences nearly a century forward: municipalities that organised royalist militias had measurably stronger local institutions by 1900–1910 — around half a public servant more per thousand inhabitants than municipalities with no conflict activity — because wartime fiscal bodies such as local tax councils persisted. Whatever one makes of the long-run argument, the premise is the point here. Royalist militias were common enough, and local enough, to function as a variable.

The war was therefore a civil war as much as an anticolonial one, and describing it as colonists against Spaniards misidentifies both sides.

## Indigenous revolt came first, and was not the same thing

Andean resistance to colonial rule long predates the independence wars and had its own objectives. Leon Campbell's survey of research on Andean peasant revolts between 1750 and 1820 identifies the Túpac Amaru II rebellion of 1780, near Cuzco, as the most prominent of a sustained and violent pattern of indigenous uprising stretching from the colonial period into the twentieth century.

It is tempting to file 1780 as a precursor to 1810. The scholarship Campbell reviews does not support that, because the grievances were different: tribute, forced labour, local abuses by colonial officials. Indigenous communities that rose in 1780 had no obvious stake in a creole republic, and many did not get one. Folding indigenous revolt into a national origin story is a way of claiming it rather than understanding it.

## What independence did not change

This is where honesty is required rather than optional. The new republics did not abolish slavery at independence, and they did not dismantle the racial hierarchy of the colonial order. Ada Ferrer's observation about Haiti — that it was the only state in the hemisphere founded by formerly enslaved people and without slavery — carries the implication directly: the Spanish American republics were founded on a different basis, and most retained slavery, in some cases for decades.

Nor did independence deliver the strong states its advocates promised. Irigoin's argument is that the inherited fiscal system's incentives survived the change of flag: governments with constrained taxing capacity compensated through indirect taxation, borrowing and monetary creation, shifting costs onto other constituencies or onto the future, and the result was political fragmentation and economic underperformance across the nineteenth century compared with both the United States and Spain.

## The campaigns ran longer than the famous dates

National calendars fix independence to single days. The material record is less tidy. The Museo Nacional de Colombia's history collection — some 1,347 classified documentary items and around 600 testimonial objects — includes a Castilian royal standard recorded as taken during the independence campaign in 1824, flags sent by General Antonio José de Sucre to the Colombian government, eight letters in Bolívar's own hand with thirty-seven more addressed to him, and a gold civic garland presented to Bolívar by the people of Cuzco in 1825.

Those objects describe campaigning and diplomacy continuing well past the dates most countries celebrate, across territory that would later be three separate republics. The collection's own framing — protagonists of the history of Colombia, Peru and Venezuela — is itself a reminder that the political map came after the war, not before it.
