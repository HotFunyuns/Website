**No team in NFL history has gone 20-0.** Through the 2025 season, the league's only perfect season belongs to the 1972 Miami Dolphins, who finished 17-0: 14 regular-season wins, then three playoff wins ending in Super Bowl VII. The reason "20-0" is the modern number is arithmetic. Teams now play 17 regular-season games, and only a No. 1 seed skips the first playoff round, so the shortest possible perfect season today is 17 + 3 = 20 games.

That makes two separate questions worth answering properly: what actually happened in the record book, and why the finish line keeps moving.

## The one perfect season

The 1972 Dolphins won every game on a schedule that was then 14 games long, and then every playoff game. NFL.com's history of the team puts it plainly: they won every regular-season game "and all three playoff games, including Super Bowl VII." The league's own Record & Fact Book records the final step as Miami beating Washington 14-7 to complete "a 17-0 season, the first perfect-record regular-season and postseason mark in NFL history."

The playoff run, as the record book lists it:

| Round | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Divisional playoff | Miami 20, Cleveland 14 |
| AFC Championship | Miami 21, Pittsburgh 17 |
| Super Bowl VII | Miami 14, Washington 7 |

Three one-score games. A perfect season is not the same thing as a dominant one, and the margins are a useful reminder that even the only team to finish the job did it by a touchdown or less in every playoff game.

### Unbeaten is not the same as perfect

The Pro Football Hall of Fame describes the 1972 Dolphins as the first team "to finish a season unbeaten and untied", and the wording matters. Early in the league's history, some champions went unbeaten without winning every game. The Record & Fact Book's chronology notes that Canton went 11-0-1 in 1923 for its second consecutive undefeated season. Unbeaten, yes. Perfect, no. When people ask whether a team has "gone undefeated", the honest answer depends on whether a tie counts as a blemish, and the record books treat 1972 as the only season without one.

## The near misses

The record book names three teams that went unbeaten through a regular season and then lost their championship game.

| Season | Team | Regular season | What happened next |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2007 | New England Patriots | 16-0 | Won two playoff games, then lost Super Bowl XLII 17-14 to the New York Giants; finished 18-1 |
| 1942 | Chicago Bears | 11-0 | Lost the NFL Championship Game 14-6 to Washington |
| 1934 | Chicago Bears | 13-0 | Lost the NFL championship game 30-13 to the New York Giants |

The 2007 season is the closest anyone has come. The Patriots were the AFC's top seed, beat Jacksonville 31-20 and San Diego 21-12, and, in NFL.com's words, "carried an 18-0 record into Super Bowl XLII." In the record book's account of that game, the Giants' winning touchdown pass came with 35 seconds remaining, and the Patriots joined the 1934 and 1942 Bears as the only teams to go unbeaten in the regular season and lose the championship game. Sixteen regular-season wins is still the most by any team; the 2026 Record & Fact Book, which covers seasons through 2025, lists no team above it.

The 1934 Bears also appear in the record lists for consecutive wins within a season: 13, the entire schedule, before the championship-game loss.

## Why the modern number is 20

The phrase "20-0" only makes sense once you know two format changes.

**The schedule grew to 17 games in 2021.** NFL.com reported that the 2021 season would "feature each team playing 17 regular-season games and three preseason games for the first time." The Record & Fact Book calls it the first change to the schedule since 1978, when teams began playing 16 regular-season games.

**Only one team per conference gets a bye.** When owners expanded the playoffs to 14 teams for the 2020 season, they also changed who rests in the first round. As NFL.com put it, adding a team in each conference "means only the No.1 seeds in the AFC and NFC will earn a postseason bye." Under the old 12-team format two teams in each conference rested; the record book's 2007 results show New England, the AFC's top seed, and fellow division winner Indianapolis both entering at the Divisional round. Every playoff team below the top seed now plays on Wild Card weekend.

Put those together and the length of a perfect season depends on your seed:

| Era | Regular-season games | Playoff wins needed by a top seed | Shortest perfect season |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1972 | 14 | 3 | 17-0 |
| 2007 (16-game schedule, two byes per conference) | 16 | 3 | 19-0 |
| 2021 onward (17 games, one bye per conference) | 17 | 3 | 20-0 |
| 2021 onward, any seed below No. 1 | 17 | 4 | 21-0 |

In practice a team that won all 17 regular-season games would hold the best record in its conference and with it the No. 1 seed, which is why "20-0" has become the shorthand. It is also the answer to the search people actually type: an NFL season can last 17 regular-season games plus up to four playoff games, so a team can play as many as 21 games, or 20 if it earns the bye.

### What the perfect team would get in return

There is one cost the record books make explicit. The league's procedure for setting draft order is blunt about it: "The winner of the Super Bowl game shall select last." A 20-0 team would pick at the very back of the next draft, which is the trade every champion accepts and one reason sustained success is harder than one great season. [How draft order works](/blog/how-football-draft-order-works/) covers the reverse-standings logic behind that.

## Why nobody has done it

Twenty consecutive wins asks a team to avoid a single bad day across a whole regular season and postseason. A one-line illustration: if a team had a 90 percent chance of winning every single game, and each game were independent, it would finish 20-0 only about 12 percent of the time (0.9 multiplied by itself 20 times is roughly 0.12). Real games are not independent coin flips with fixed chances, so treat that as intuition rather than an estimate, but the shape of the problem is right. Long seasons punish even excellent teams for a single off night.

That is also why season length matters so much in simulations. [Understanding sports simulation probability](/blog/understanding-sports-sim-probability/) works through the same arithmetic for longer schedules and shows how quickly a perfect record becomes rarer as games are added.
