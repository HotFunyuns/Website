**Golf scoring is built entirely on one number: par.** Once you know what par is and that lower is better, every other term — birdie, bogey, eagle, "four under" — is a description of the distance from it.

## Par, and why it exists

Par is the number of strokes a hole is expected to take a competent player, driven mainly by the hole's length. Most holes are par 3, 4 or 5. A standard 18-hole course usually totals around 70 to 72.

The reason golf needs par at all is that holes differ enormously. Taking four shots on a 190-yard hole is a poor result; taking four on a 550-yard hole is a very good one. Par normalises them so a single number describes a round.

## The vocabulary

| Term | Relative to par | Common on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Albatross (double eagle) | −3 | Par 5s, very rare |
| Eagle | −2 | Par 5s, occasionally par 4s |
| Birdie | −1 | Any hole |
| Par | 0 | Any hole |
| Bogey | +1 | Any hole |
| Double bogey | +2 | Any hole |
| Triple bogey and worse | +3 or more | Usually after trouble |

A round is then expressed as the sum: shoot 68 on a par 72 course and you are "four under". Because the target is to take fewer strokes, **golf is one of the few sports where a lower number is better**, which is why scores are almost always quoted relative to par rather than as raw totals.

## Stroke play versus match play

The two main formats differ in one respect that changes strategy completely.

**Stroke play** counts every shot. A hole where everything goes wrong adds its full cost to your total, and there is no upper limit on the damage. This is the format used in most professional tournaments.

**Match play** scores hole by hole. Win a hole, lose a hole, or halve it. A hole where everything goes wrong costs you exactly one hole — the same as losing by a single shot.

That difference makes aggression cheaper in match play and expensive in stroke play. In stroke play, avoiding disasters is a scoring strategy in its own right; in match play, it frequently is not, because being safe on a hole your opponent is attacking simply loses the hole.

## Building a tournament total

A professional event is usually four rounds. The total is the sum, expressed against the sum of the pars.

This is why **consistency outperforms brilliance over four days**. A player who shoots 68-70-69-71 (four under on a par-72 course, cumulative −10) finishes ahead of one who shoots 64-75-66-74 (−9) despite the second player having the two best rounds of the week. The bad rounds do not disappear.

It is also why the middle rounds of a tournament are so often about damage limitation. There is a filter partway through most events — [how a golf cut works](/blog/how-a-golf-cut-works/) covers it — and surviving it is a prerequisite for everything else.

## Handicaps

Handicapping exists so players of different standards can compete meaningfully. A player's handicap produces an allowance which is subtracted from their gross score to give a net score.

The important conceptual point: **a handicap adjusts the score, not the course**. Both players face the same holes; the comparison is adjusted afterwards. The [USGA's Rules of Handicapping](https://www.usga.org/handicapping/roh/2020-rules-of-handicapping.html) set out how the calculation works in the systems that use it.

Professional stroke play does not use handicaps — everyone plays off scratch — which is why tournament scores are quoted gross.

## Penalties, briefly

Scores also move because of the rules rather than the shots: a ball out of bounds, a ball in a penalty area, an unplayable lie. Each carries a defined stroke cost, and those costs are where a hole goes from a bogey to a triple.

The full framework is published by the [USGA](https://www.usga.org/rules-hub.html) and the R&A. For a beginner, the practical takeaway is smaller than the rulebook: **penalties are the main source of scores that ruin a card**, which is why course management is largely about avoiding places where they happen.
