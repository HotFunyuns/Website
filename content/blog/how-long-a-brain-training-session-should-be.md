**Five to fifteen minutes is the right length for most practice sessions, and the reason is attention rather than discipline.** Performance on a repetitive, self-paced task degrades the longer you stay on it, so minutes thirty to forty are not simply extra practice — they are practice done worse. If you want a larger weekly total, the lever to pull is more days, not longer days.

That answer needs two qualifications, which the rest of this article supplies: where the published numbers come from, and how to find your own limit from a session record rather than from a general rule.

## What published guidance says about dose

The most concrete figure in this space comes from a federal practice guide. The Institute of Education Sciences guide [*Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics*](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/2) recommends that "interventions at all grade levels should devote about 10 minutes in each session to building fluent retrieval of basic arithmetic facts." That is a recommendation for structured intervention with school-age learners, not a prescription for adults using a drill app, and it should not be stretched into one. But it is a published, reviewed figure for how long a fluency block usefully runs, and it lands squarely inside the five-to-fifteen range rather than at thirty or sixty minutes.

The companion guide, [*Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning*](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1), leads with a different variable: its first recommendation is "space learning over time." Session length and session frequency are separate dials, and the evidence on the frequency dial is much stronger than anything you will find on the length dial. The existing guide to [building a daily practice habit that survives a bad week](/blog/building-a-daily-practice-habit/) works through what that means for adherence; this article is about the minutes inside one sitting.

## Why a long session stops paying

Sustained attention on a monotonous task declines in a measurable, well-documented way. The phenomenon is called the vigilance decrement, and a study in *Frontiers in Psychology*, [*Examining the Role of Task Requirements in the Magnitude of the Vigilance Decrement*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01504/full), defines it as "the decreased probability of detecting critical trials in such tasks as time on task increases." In that study participants worked through four blocks of a forty-minute task and showed significant performance degradation across the blocks, with reaction times increasing and accuracy declining as the task went on.

Two practical consequences follow.

The first is about data. The later part of a long session is your least representative performance, so a personal best set in minute two and an accuracy figure calculated across minute forty are not describing the same person on the same day. If you are trying to read progress from a record, long sessions make the record noisier.

The second is about what gets practised. A drill you work through while fatigued is still a drill, and rushed, inattentive repetitions are exactly the ones that install a sloppy method. That risk is the subject of [training accuracy before speed](/blog/accuracy-before-speed/), and it is the strongest argument for stopping earlier than you feel you could.

## A session you can actually build

Mental Math & Memory Games is our own app — Reign Creative publishes it — and its content is cut into short units, which makes it a convenient way to show what a session of a given length is physically made of. The shipped pieces include a 30-second lightning round, 60-second sprints on each operation, 75-second trainers for the applied topics, a 180-second math marathon, a ten-question accuracy round with no timer at all, and memory rounds that run in sets of roughly eight to twelve.

Stacked end to end, a 30-second lightning round, two 60-second sprints and one 75-second trainer come to 225 seconds of play — three minutes and forty-five seconds. Adding the 180-second marathon brings it to 405 seconds, or six minutes and forty-five seconds. The remaining minutes of a ten-minute sitting go on reading the feedback after each round, which is the part that turns repetition into practice rather than filler.

That arithmetic is the point. "Fifteen minutes" is not fifteen minutes of answering questions; it is perhaps eight to ten minutes of play and several minutes of looking at what went wrong. If you strip out the looking, you have built a longer session that teaches less. You can see the whole catalogue of modes and formats on the [Mental Math & Memory Games app page](/apps/mental-math-memory-games/), and the rest of the [brain training games hub](/blog/topics/brain-training-games/) covers how to sequence them.

## Finding your own limit from your own record

General advice sets a starting range. Your record sets the actual number.

The method is simple and takes one session. Run a fixed session — same mode, same difficulty — for longer than you normally would, and compare the first third with the last third on two numbers: accuracy, and median answer time. Use the median rather than the average, because one long stall distorts an average badly; [reading your response time numbers](/blog/reading-response-time-numbers/) explains why.

- **Accuracy holds and median time holds.** You have not reached your limit yet on this task.
- **Accuracy holds but median time drifts up.** You are still getting them right, slowly. This is usually the first sign of the decrement.
- **Accuracy falls.** You passed the useful end of the session somewhere earlier. Set your length a little below where the fall started.

Repeat it occasionally rather than daily. Session capacity varies with sleep, time of day and how much thinking you have already done, so one measurement is a starting point rather than a permanent setting.

## Frequency is the better lever

If you have thirty-five minutes a week to spend, five sessions of seven minutes will almost certainly serve you better than one session of thirty-five. That follows from the spacing recommendation above, and it has a second advantage that has nothing to do with cognition: a seven-minute commitment survives a bad week, and a thirty-five-minute one does not.

There is also a trap in judging any of this by how the session felt. The review [*Learning Versus Performance*](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/11/soderstorm_ra_learningvsperformance.pdf) by Soderstrom and Bjork makes the point directly: during training "what we can observe and measure is performance, which is often an unreliable index of whether the relatively long-term changes that constitute learning have taken place." A long session that felt productive is not evidence that it was, and a short session that felt too easy is not evidence that it was wasted.

None of this makes a drill app a cognitive intervention. Practice reliably improves performance on the task you practise; whether that carries anywhere else is a separate and much weaker claim, which the cornerstone guide [do brain training apps work](/blog/do-brain-training-apps-work/) handles in detail. Session length is a question about how to spend practice minutes well, not about what practice buys. More articles in this area sit in the [education and brain category](/blog/category/education-brain/).

## The short version

Start at ten minutes. Split it into short rounds with a pause to read the feedback after each. Do it on more days rather than for longer. Once a fortnight, run a deliberately long session and check whether accuracy and median time hold up in the last third — and let that, rather than a number from an article, set your ceiling.
