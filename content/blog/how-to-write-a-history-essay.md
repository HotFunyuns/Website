A history essay is an argument with evidence attached, and it is marked on the argument. The working structure is short: a question, a thesis that answers it, a sequence of paragraphs each making one claim and showing why the evidence supports it, an honest engagement with the strongest rival reading, and citations precise enough that a reader can check any sentence. Essays rarely fail because the writer did not know enough. They fail because the knowledge was never turned into a case.

This is general guidance. It is not a substitute for your own syllabus, your department's style requirements or your marking criteria, and where those conflict with anything here, they win.

## Start from a question, not a topic

"The French Revolution" is a topic. "Why did the revolutionary government turn on its own leadership?" is a question. Only the second can be answered, and only an answer can be argued.

The distinction matters because it determines everything downstream. A topic invites you to write down what you know in roughly chronological order, which produces a summary. A question forces selection: some of what you know is relevant to the answer and the rest is not, and deciding which is which is most of the intellectual work.

If you have been given a title rather than a question, convert it. Find the *how* or *why* buried in it and write that down as a sentence with a question mark. That sentence goes at the top of your notes and stays there.

## What a thesis has to do

Hamilton College's writing guide puts it directly: your thesis is "your take on the subject, your perspective, your explanation — that is, the case that you're going to argue". It is not a statement of what the essay will cover. It is the answer you are defending.

A usable thesis has three properties. It is **contestable** — a reasonable person who had read the same material could disagree. It is **specific** — it names the mechanism or the cause, not just the fact that causes existed. And it is **provable with the evidence you actually have**, which is why thesis-writing should come after your first pass through the sources rather than before.

A common trap is the thesis that lists factors: "the revolution had political, economic and social causes". That is true of every event and commits you to nothing. Rank them, weight them, or name the relationship between them, and you have an argument. The University of North Carolina's [history handout](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/history/) also suggests a structural use for the thesis: break it into its component parts and give each part a section, so the essay's shape follows from the claim.

## Paragraphs: claim, evidence, warrant

Each body paragraph should do one job. The reliable three-part shape is:

1. **Claim.** A topic sentence stating what this paragraph establishes — and stating it as a step in the argument, not as a subject heading.
2. **Evidence.** The specific material that supports it: a quotation from a document, a figure from a record, a described object, a historian's finding with attribution.
3. **Warrant.** The sentence explaining *why* that evidence supports that claim.

The third part is the one most often missing, and it is the difference between a paragraph that argues and a paragraph that merely gestures. Quoting a petition and moving on assumes the reader will draw the inference you intended. Spelling out why the petition's wording, timing or authorship supports your claim is the actual historical reasoning.

The UNC handout frames the underlying balance memorably: markers expect students to show both what they "know about the past, and can think about the past", and it warns that the knowing and the thinking "should balance each other out". An essay that is nine parts knowledge is a summary. An essay that is nine parts thinking is an opinion piece.

Keep the reader oriented. As Hamilton puts it, "your reader should always know where your argument has come from, where it is now, and where it is going" — which in practice means transitions that carry logic rather than chronology.

## Handling evidence

Historical evidence has to be read before it can be used, and reading it is a skill with a name. The Digital Inquiry Group's [historical thinking chart](https://www.inquirygroup.org/history-lessons/historical-thinking-chart) sets out four reading skills — sourcing, contextualisation, corroboration and close reading — with questions attached to each. In essay terms they translate into habits:

- **Sourcing** means saying who produced a document, when, and for whom, before you use what it says. A pamphlet written to persuade is evidence of what its author wanted believed, which may be more useful than evidence of what was true.
- **Contextualisation** means not importing present-day assumptions into a period that did not hold them.
- **Corroboration** means checking a claim against a source that could not have copied the first.
- **Close reading** means attending to what a document assumes as well as what it states.

Hamilton's guidance is to use "as many primary sources as practical" while also consulting scholarly secondary work from university presses and academic journals, and to distinguish carefully between scholarly and non-scholarly material. If you are unsure which category a source falls into, [primary and secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources/) sets out the distinction and why it shifts depending on the question you are asking. Dates deserve the same care: a figure that looks precise may be a midpoint or a convention, which [how historians date events](/blog/how-historians-date-events/) explains.

## Counter-argument is part of the argument

Address the strongest version of the reading that opposes yours, not a weak version you can dismiss. There are three places it can go: a dedicated section before your conclusion, a paragraph after each of your main claims, or woven through as concessions.

What makes it work is the response. "Some historians argue X" followed by nothing is a gesture. "Some historians argue X, on the strength of Y; that reading struggles with Z, which is why I weight the evidence differently" is an argument. Taking the rival position seriously is also the most reliable way to discover that your own thesis needs adjusting, which is a good outcome, not a setback.

## Citation, and why history uses notes

History conventionally uses notes-and-bibliography citation. The Chicago Manual of Style's [sample citations](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/tools_citationguide/citation-guide-1.html) show the pattern: a full citation in the first note, shortened citations afterwards, and an alphabetised bibliography listing everything used, with specific page numbers in notes and full page ranges in the bibliography.

Hamilton's guide is blunt about why parenthetical citation is discouraged in history: it is "inadequate to capture the richness of historical sources". A note can carry an archival reference, a manuscript folio, a translation choice and a short comment, none of which fit inside brackets mid-sentence. Follow whichever style your course requires, but expect footnotes and expect them to be precise.

## Drafting, and where a timeline app fits

Writing improves through a cycle rather than a single pass. The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide [Teaching Secondary Students to Write Effectively](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/22) gives its strongest recommendation to explicitly taught writing strategies used through a model-practice-reflect cycle, and a moderate recommendation to integrating writing with reading so that the features of good writing are visible in what you read. Applied alone, that means: read a well-made journal article for structure as well as content, draft, then reflect on what your draft does rather than what you meant it to do.

A browsable history corpus is useful at exactly one stage of this: the beginning. World History Timeline Sim is our own app, built by Reign Creative, and its lesson screens follow the same skeleton an argument needs — an introduction, a body, the people and dates involved, and a section on why the episode mattered — which makes them a reasonable way to see the shape before you write your own. A large glossary is likewise handy for pinning down a term precisely before you use it.

That is orientation, not research. The app's content carries no citations and has had no external scholarly review, so nothing in it belongs in a footnote, and it is not a substitute for teachers, scholarly books, archives or primary sources.
