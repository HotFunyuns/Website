Moderately hard, and hard in an unglamorous place. Marathi has no tones and a script that says what it means, so two of the things that make Thai or Cantonese frightening are simply absent. What it has instead is a grammar that asks you to agree with something on nearly every content word in the sentence, and that is a slow, cumulative tax rather than a single wall.

If you want the short version: budget a month for reading and a year for gender.

## What is easier than you expect

**There are no tones.** Pitch in Marathi does what it does in English — emphasis, question intonation, mood. It never distinguishes one word from another. Anyone who has bounced off a tonal language will feel the difference immediately; [what a tonal language actually is](/blog/what-is-a-tonal-language/) explains what you are being spared.

**The script is phonetic and finite.** Devanagari has about 36 consonant letters and 16 initial-vowel letters in the Balbodh form Marathi uses, laid out by place of articulation so the chart teaches itself. Marathi is also more phonetically transparent than Hindi in one specific respect: it keeps schwas that Hindi deletes, so the written form is closer to the spoken one.

**Verbs do not conjugate for person the way Romance verbs do.** There is no table of six endings per tense to memorise for every verb.

**There is a great deal of English in the language already.** Marathi has absorbed English vocabulary the way it previously absorbed Persian, so a certain amount of modern, technical and workplace vocabulary is free.

## What is genuinely hard

**Three genders, and no reliable rule.** Marathi preserves the Sanskrit neuter alongside masculine and feminine, which sets it apart from most modern Indo-Aryan languages. There are helpful tendencies — how a noun ends narrows the possibilities — but they are tendencies. A large part of gender assignment is learned noun by noun, and getting it wrong is audible because the rest of the sentence agrees with it.

**Agreement everywhere.** Declinable adjectives inflect for gender, number and case. Possessive markers change form. Verbs agree. The consequence is that a single wrong gender does not produce one wrong word, it produces a chain of wrong endings.

**Split ergativity.** Marathi is ergative with perfective transitive verbs and with obligative constructions, and nominative elsewhere. In plain terms: in some sentences the verb agrees with the subject, in others with the object, and in a third pattern with neither. English gives you nothing to hang this on, which is what makes it expensive — you are not translating a concept, you are building one.

**Subject–object–verb order.** Not hard to understand, surprisingly hard to produce at speed. The verb arriving last means you have to hold the whole clause in mind before you can finish it, and for months your sentences will come out in English order and then need reassembling.

**The numbers.** Marathi counting to a hundred is largely irregular: the decade words are unrelated to each other, the unit forms change shape inside compounds, and there is a productive pattern that builds the number nine less than a ten by subtracting from the *next* ten. [Marathi numbers 1 to 100](/blog/marathi-numbers-and-counting/) sets out why counting drills fail here.

## Where our learners actually slow down

We can be specific about this, because the shape of the Marathi course changed in response to it.

The alphabet unit was never the problem. People work through Devanagari and come out the other side. **The place the course lost people was the grammar block covering gender and verb agreement.** It was originally one long unit — genders introduced, adjective declension, then verb agreement, then the past tense — and it was comfortably the longest lesson in the course.

It was also where sessions ended and did not resume.

We split it. Gender assignment is now its own short unit with nothing else in it. Adjective agreement is second. Verb agreement in the present is third. The past tense and its agreement shift are fourth, and deliberately late. Each of those fits a single short session.

The honest caveat is that splitting a hard unit into four short units does not make the material easier. It makes it *finishable*, which is a different thing and, for a self-study course with nobody waiting on you, the more useful one. A learner who stops halfway through unit three has still learned genders and adjective agreement. A learner who stopped halfway through the old monolith had learned a fragment of everything and could use none of it.

## How long the early stages take

With short daily sessions rather than occasional long ones:

- **Weeks 1–2:** vowels, matras, first consonant rows, greetings, the two gendered forms of "how are you".
- **Weeks 3–4:** remaining consonants, Marathi's additional letters, numbers to twenty, family and food vocabulary.
- **Months 2–3:** present tense, adjective agreement, situational vocabulary, slow reading of real text with romanisation as a safety net.
- **Months 4–6:** past tense and the ergative pattern, conjunct fluency, listening at closer to natural speed.

None of that is conversation. Conversation needs other people, and no course replaces them — [Learn Marathi Language Fast](/apps/learn-marathi/) includes roleplay and speaking activities, but a roleplay does not interrupt you, mishear you or change the subject. It builds the material you bring to a conversation. The conversation itself is homework of a different kind.
