A general calorie counter and a low-carb tracker look almost identical on the surface. Both take a food, both give you macros back. The difference is one subtraction, performed dozens of times a week, and whether the app does it or you do.

That subtraction is also the reason low-carb comparisons go wrong. People compare feature lists when the thing that actually determines their daily experience is a counting convention buried three screens down.

## The subtraction is the product

Net carbs is not a regulated measurement. A US Nutrition Facts panel declares Total Carbohydrate with Dietary Fiber, Total Sugars and Added Sugars beneath it — the net figure is arithmetic performed afterwards, by you or by software, according to a convention someone chose. We take that apart properly in [net carbs, explained](/blog/net-carbs-explained-keto-tracking/).

The practical consequence for choosing an app is this: **when a tracker shows you a net carb number, it is showing you the output of its own rule.** The rule usually starts from total carbohydrate and removes dietary fiber, and most low-carb conventions also remove sugar alcohols and some newer sweeteners. Which sweeteners, and whether all fibers are treated the same, is where apps diverge.

So the first question is not "does this app do net carbs" — nearly all of them do — but "does it do them the way I intend to count, and does it do them without me thinking about it."

[Keto Diet Tracker: Low Carb](/apps/keto-diet-tracker/) subtracts fiber along with erythritol, allulose and monk fruit from total carbohydrate each time you log a food, and it can alert you when a day passes a limit you set yourself. That is a stated convention rather than a hidden one, which is the property that matters: a number you can reproduce beats a number that is theoretically purer.

### Cronometer

Cronometer is not a keto app, and it is worth including precisely because of that. Its features page lists "Track Any Diet," describing customisation for keto among others, and its strength is the nutrient panel rather than the carb rule.

On its pricing page the free Basic tier includes custom macro *and micronutrient* targets, a barcode scanner, custom foods, meals and recipes, and device syncing, with reports and charts over a seven-day window. Gold adds photo and voice logging, a Recipe Importer, a Fasting Timer, a Macro Scheduler, custom charts, coaching and unlimited historical data.

If your interest in low-carb is partly a worry about what a restricted diet does to your micronutrient intake — a reasonable worry, given that MedlinePlus notes low-carb diets can limit daily fiber — a nutrient-depth tracker answers a question a carb-focused one does not.

### MyFitnessPal

MyFitnessPal is the general tracker most people already have. It logs macros and micronutrients and has the broadest set of entry routes, and it can be used for low-carb eating by treating carbohydrate as the number you watch.

The relevant detail for a low-carb reader is where its line falls. MyFitnessPal's own Premium page lists "Custom macros and goals" and an "Intermittent fasting tracker" among Premium features. Setting a specific carbohydrate target is the entire point of low-carb tracking, so which side of the paywall that sits on is not a minor packaging question. Read their current Premium page before assuming either way.

## The half of low-carb tracking that is not food

This is where these apps separate more than they do on food logging, and it is under-discussed.

**Ketone readings.** Blood meters, urine strips and breath meters each produce a number, and people who use them want that number next to the day it belongs to. Carb Manager names ketone, blood glucose, insulin and GKI tracking; ours records blood, urine strip and breath readings including GKI. Neither app measures anything — a phone cannot. It stores what you type in, and a reading is a data point you collected, not a medical result.

**Fasting windows.** Fasting timers appear across the category but not always in the free tier: Cronometer's pricing page lists its Fasting Timer under Gold, MyFitnessPal's Premium page lists an intermittent fasting tracker under Premium, Carb Manager names intermittent fasting on its site, and ours includes a fasting timer in the free app. If you fast, check this specifically.

**Water and electrolytes.** Sodium, potassium and magnesium get attention in low-carb circles that they do not get in general nutrition apps. Ours logs all three alongside water. We are not going to tell you what to do with those numbers — that is a conversation for a healthcare professional — but if you are tracking them, having them in the same record as the food is the difference between a habit and three apps.

**Pick Carb Manager if** you want recipes, meal plans, a community and course content in the same place as the log, and you are happy to evaluate its tier structure on its current pricing page.

**Pick Cronometer if** your real question is nutritional adequacy rather than carbohydrate specifically, you want micronutrient targets you can set for free, and you would use its biometric tracking.

**Pick MyFitnessPal if** you are already logging there, most of your food is packaged, and low-carb is one phase in a longer relationship with a general tracker.

**Pick a different tool entirely if** carbohydrate is not actually your constraint. ## What none of these apps can do

None of them is a medical or diagnostic tool. They do not interpret a ketone reading, they do not assess whether a low-carb diet is appropriate for you, and they do not promise weight loss or any other outcome. MedlinePlus is direct on the point: a low-carbohydrate diet "can be safe, but you should talk to your health care provider before starting it."

They also cannot make an estimate exact. If you want to sanity-check what an app says about a generic food, USDA's **FoodData Central** is free to search and its data are in the public domain — comparing an app's entry against it takes a minute and occasionally changes your mind about the entry.
