"Aztec" is used in English as a general word for ancient Mexico, and it is one of the least accurate shorthands in world history. The Aztec state is a latecomer. Behind it sit roughly two and a half thousand years of Mesoamerican cities, writing systems, calendars and states — including one of the largest cities in the ancient world whose builders we still cannot confidently name.

Here is the sequence, with the dates attached to the evidence that supports them, and the open questions left open.

## The Formative Gulf Coast, and a calendar older than writing

The earliest of the familiar names belongs to the Gulf lowlands. What is usually called Olmec is a Formative-period tradition of monumental sculpture, earthen platforms and long-distance exchange, and the strongest recent work on it has come from the air rather than the trowel.

A 2023 study in *Science Advances* used aerial laser scanning to analyse the orientations of a large number of ceremonial complexes along the southern Gulf Coast, including many newly identified Formative sites dating from 1100 BCE to 250 CE. Mesoamerican civic and ceremonial buildings were largely aligned to sunrises or sunsets on particular dates, and the pattern of dates marked by those alignments points to a ritual calendar tied to subsistence. The headline finding is that orientations of complexes built between 1100 and 750 BCE represent the earliest evidence for use of the 260-day calendar — centuries earlier than its previously known appearance in textual records.

That is a remarkable claim about method as much as about chronology: a calendar reconstructed from the direction buildings face. It also reframes the Mesoamerican calendar as a shared regional inheritance rather than a Maya invention, which is how popular accounts usually present it. The general mechanics of ritual and solar calendars are covered in [how calendars and dating systems work](/blog/how-calendars-and-dating-systems-work/).

## Teotihuacan: the city without a people

UNESCO describes Teotihuacan, about 50 km north-east of Mexico City, as built between the first and seventh centuries CE, characterised by the vast size of its monuments — the Temple of Quetzalcoatl and the Pyramids of the Sun and the Moon — laid out on geometric and symbolic principles, and as one of the most powerful cultural centres in Mesoamerica, extending its cultural and artistic influence throughout the region and beyond.

What UNESCO does not say is who built it, and that is not an oversight. The city's builders are not securely identified with a single known ethnic or linguistic group. Even the name is second-hand: "Teotihuacan" is a Nahuatl name, glossed by UNESCO as the place where the gods were created, applied by people who encountered the ruins long after the city's abandonment. We do not know what its residents called it.

This is an unusually honest gap and worth dwelling on. A city of that scale, with apartment compounds, planned avenues, a distinctive art style and demonstrable reach, has no securely attached ethnonym. It is a good corrective to the assumption that every archaeological culture maps onto a people with a name.

Its reach, though, is now demonstrable. Lidar at the Classic Maya city of Tikal revealed a precinct that probably reproduces the Ciudadela and Temple of the Feathered Serpent at Teotihuacan — a finding published in *Antiquity* in 2021 that fits into a longer argument about whether Teotihuacan's presence in the Maya lowlands was a matter of invasion and resident foreign elites, or of softer stylistic and cosmopolitan exchange. The authors are explicit that Classic Maya cities are increasingly understood as places shaped by locals and non-locals alike.

## Monte Alban and the Zapotec state

UNESCO's Oaxaca inscription records Monte Alban as inhabited over a period of 1,500 years by a succession of peoples — Olmecs, Zapotecs and Mixtecs — with terraces, dams, canals, pyramids and artificial mounds literally carved out of the mountain, forming what it calls the symbols of a sacred topography.

Two things are worth extracting. A single site can belong to several traditions in sequence, so "the Zapotec capital" is a phase rather than a permanent identity. And the engineering — terraces, dams and canals on a levelled mountaintop — is the substance of the achievement, not a backdrop to the pyramids.

## The Maya, in the plural

The Maya are the best-known of these traditions and the most consistently misdescribed, usually by being made singular and then made extinct.

They were never one state. UNESCO dates Palenque's height to between AD 500 and 700, when its influence extended through the Usumacinta basin, and describes it as a prime example of a Maya sanctuary of the Classic period. Uxmal, in Yucatan, it dates to a founding around AD 700 with buildings from 700 to 1000 and a population it puts at some 25,000, with a layout revealing knowledge of astronomy.

Different regions, different centuries, different political fortunes. The so-called Classic Maya collapse affected the southern lowland cities and did not end Maya civilisation: northern centres such as Uxmal were flourishing while southern ones were being abandoned. And Maya peoples, languages and communities exist today, which makes the past tense wrong for the people even where it is right for a particular city.

The pattern of several regional centres declining at different times for overlapping reasons is the same analytic problem as [what caused the Bronze Age collapse](/blog/what-caused-the-bronze-age-collapse/), and it resists the single-cause story for the same reasons.

## The Toltec problem

Tula and the Toltecs deserve a caution rather than a summary. Much of what circulates about the Toltecs reaches us through accounts written in or after the Aztec period, in which a prestigious Tollan functions partly as a claim to legitimate ancestry. How that literary Tollan relates to the archaeological site of Tula is a genuine and long-running scholarly dispute.

The practical advice is to treat "Toltec" as a term whose referent depends on who is using it, and to go to specialist Mesoamerican archaeology rather than a general summary — including this one — before relying on anything specific about it.

## The framing that helps most

European history trains readers to look for one empire and date its fall, which is the habit examined in [why Rome fell](/blog/why-rome-fell/). Mesoamerica does not reward it. What you have instead is a shared cultural sphere — a calendar, a ballgame, maize agriculture, related religious ideas — supporting a long series of distinct, competing, overlapping states.

The Aztecs are the last chapter, not the subject.
