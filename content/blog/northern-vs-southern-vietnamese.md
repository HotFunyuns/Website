Which Vietnamese you are learning matters more for listening than for grammar. Northern and southern speech share one written language and understand each other, but they sound noticeably different, and a few everyday words change. This guide covers the main differences: consonants, tones, final sounds, vocabulary and numbers.

## The dialect map

Wikipedia describes three traditional dialect regions: **North (45%), Central (10%) and South (45%)**, and calls the regional varieties mutually intelligible, though it notes that the North-Central and Central varieties, with many vocabulary differences, are generally less mutually intelligible to Northern and Southern speakers. **Modern Standard Vietnamese is based on the Hanoi dialect.**

Movement has mixed the accents. Wikipedia notes that after the 1954 partition about **a million northerners moved south**, mainly to Saigon and its surroundings, and about 150,000 moved north, so many southern residents speak with a partly northern accent.

## Consonants at the start of a word

| Letter | North (Hanoi) | South (Saigon) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| d | z | y |
| gi | z | y |
| r | z | an r-sound |
| v | v | traditionally y; increasingly v |
| ch and tr | the same sound | traditionally distinct, increasingly merging |
| s and x | the same sound | traditionally distinct, increasingly merging |

The table simplifies Wikipedia's phonetic charts into English approximations. Two points stand out. In Hanoi, **d, gi and r all sound like z**, so spelling is the only guide to which letter a word uses. In the South, Wikipedia notes, **v was historically pronounced like y** in everyday speech but is becoming a **v** again, especially in careful speech and among educated speakers.

## Final consonants

The ends of syllables differ too. Wikipedia explains that Hanoi speech keeps its final consonants close to Middle Vietnamese, while in **Ho Chi Minh City final n and ng are no longer kept apart, nor t and c**: which of the pair you hear depends on the vowel before it.

Southern speech also simplifies one cluster. Wikipedia notes that spellings beginning **qu-, hu- or u-** followed by a vowel are usually pronounced with a plain **w** sound in the South, even among educated urban speakers.

## Tones: six and five

Vietnamese spelling marks six tones, and northern speech keeps all six apart. Wikipedia's phonology article explains that in southern accents **the hỏi and ngã tones merge** into a single mid falling-rising tone, which is **characteristic of southern speech**. So two words that differ only in having ◌̉ or ◌̃ are pronounced alike in a southern accent. [Vietnamese tones explained](/blog/vietnamese-tones-explained/) covers all six.

## Everyday words that differ

Wikipedia's comparison table, checked against Wiktionary's regional labels, includes:

| Meaning | North | South |
| --- | --- | --- |
| yes (polite) | vâng | dạ |
| spoon | thìa | muỗng |
| bowl | bát | chén |
| thousand | nghìn | ngàn |
| car | ô tô | xe hơi |
| fork | dĩa | nĩa |
| plate | đĩa | dĩa |
| this | này | nè |
| that | đấy | đó |

Note the trap in the last rows: **dĩa means "fork" in the North and "plate" in the South**. Wikipedia adds that most of these words are understood everywhere, and that mixed forms such as *dạ vâng* also exist.

Pronouns shift too. Southern speech often merges "that gentleman" into one word: northern *ông ấy* becomes southern **ổng**, and *bà ấy* becomes **bả**.

## A worked example: one answer, two accents

A shopkeeper confirms a price of 50,000 đồng.

- **In Hanoi**: *Vâng, năm mươi nghìn.* The *v* is a v.
- **In Saigon**: *Dạ, năm mươi ngàn.* The *d* of *dạ* sounds like y.

Same meaning, different words for "yes" and "thousand", and a different consonant sound. Numbers differ in one more place: Wikipedia gives 101 as *một trăm linh một* in the North and *một trăm lẻ một* in the South. [Vietnamese numbers](/blog/vietnamese-numbers/) covers the rest.

## Which variety should you learn?

Learn the one you will hear: your family's, your partner's, or the city you are moving to. Standard Vietnamese is based on the Hanoi dialect, but Wikipedia notes that the major dialects remain predominant in their own areas, and the varieties are mutually intelligible. Whichever you choose, listen to the other early so that it does not surprise you.
