To store trading cards so they last, put each card in a sleeve made of a stable plastic such as polypropylene, add a rigid holder for cards that must not bend, and keep the box somewhere below 75°F with relative humidity between about 15% and 65%, away from pipes, windows and food. Those figures and materials come from the US National Archives' guidance for family papers and photographs; trading cards are printed paper and card stock, so this guide applies that advice to a card collection.

## Why archival advice applies

The National Archives' guidance for **family papers and photographs** describes the risks that matter for printed paper: warmth speeds chemical decay and insect activity, damp allows mould, very dry air causes brittleness, and some plastics and adhesives damage what they touch. It does not mention trading cards, so treat it as the closest authoritative guide rather than card-specific rules.

## Choosing sleeves and holders

Start with the plastic that touches the card. The National Archives recommends sleeves of **stable plastics: polyester, polypropylene or polyethylene**, and lists **unknown plastics and polyvinyl chloride (PVC)** among materials to avoid, illustrated with an unstable plastic sheet that has become wavy and discoloured. Its guidance for photographs likewise says not to use plastics containing PVC.

| Layer | Purpose | What to look for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Soft sleeve | Protects the surface from scratches and fingerprints | Polypropylene or polyethylene, labelled PVC-free |
| Rigid holder or toploader | Stops bending | A stable plastic, sized so the sleeved card fits without force |
| Box or binder | Keeps cards together and out of light | Acid-free board for boxes; stable plastic pages for binders |

The Archives also notes sleeves should be **larger than the original**, because any part sticking out is likely to be damaged, and that only **one item should go in each sleeve**.

## Storage conditions

The National Archives gives clear numbers:

- **Temperature: below 75°F.** The lower the temperature, the longer items last, because cooler conditions slow chemical decay and reduce insect activity.
- **Relative humidity: below 65%**, to prevent mould and reduce insect activity.
- **But not too dry: below about 15%** relative humidity can cause brittleness.

It also advises keeping collections **away from sources of leaks and floods**, such as pipes, windows and roof leaks, and **away from food and water**, which attract insects and rodents.

## A worked example: choosing a shelf

You have three places for a box of cards, and leave a thermometer-hygrometer in each for a week. Suppose the readings are: garage, up to 84°F; basement, 62°F but 70% relative humidity, next to a water pipe; bedroom wardrobe on an inside wall, 68–72°F and 40–50% relative humidity. The garage fails the 75°F limit, the basement fails the 65% humidity limit and the pipe rule, and the wardrobe passes all three, so on the Archives' criteria the wardrobe wins. Keep the hygrometer in the box; if humidity climbs towards 65%, move the box somewhere drier.

## Boxes and binders

The Archives' rules for papers carry over to boxes:

- Containers should be **big enough for items to lie flat or stand upright without bending**.
- Boards and folders should be **lignin-free and acid-free** or buffered.
- If a box is not full, use a **spacer** so items cannot slump and bend.
- Avoid **overstuffing** albums and binders, which damages pages and what is attached to them.

## Handling

The Archives' advice for photographs fits cards well: handle items **by their edges**, avoid touching the image area, and wear **clean nitrile or cotton gloves** where possible, because oils and salts from skin can degrade surfaces over time. It also says to remove **paper clips, staples, rubber bands and adhesive notes**, which damage surfaces; for cards, that means never bundling them with rubber bands. For dust that could scratch a surface, the Archives suggests brushing lightly with a clean, soft brush or using a hand-operated puff blower, and avoiding canned air because the force of its air stream can cause damage.
