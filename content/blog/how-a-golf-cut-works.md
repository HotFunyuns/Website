**A cut removes most of the field partway through a tournament, and everyone removed goes home with nothing.** That second clause is what makes it strategically important. A cut is not a mild penalty for playing badly; it is a cliff.

## The mechanism

In a standard four-round event, the field plays two rounds, and then only players at or better than a threshold score continue.

The threshold is not fixed in advance. It is produced by a rule about *how many* players continue — commonly "the leading N players and anyone tied with the last of them". Whatever score that boundary falls on becomes the cut line for that week.

Two consequences:

- **The line moves.** It depends entirely on how the field scored. An easy course in calm conditions produces a low cut line; a difficult one in wind produces a high one.
- **Ties are included.** If seven players are tied on the boundary score, all seven continue, and the weekend field is larger than the nominal number.

Some events also use a secondary cut after three rounds when the field remains too large, though this is less common.

## Why they exist

The practical reason is daylight. A large field playing four rounds in pairs or threes does not fit into the tee times available over a weekend without starting in darkness.

The competitive reason is that the closing rounds are more interesting when everyone in them can still win. A player twenty shots back on Saturday adds nothing to the contest and occupies a tee time.

## What missing it actually costs

This is the part that changes how professionals play, and it is worth stating plainly:

| Outcome | Prize money | Ranking points | Week's result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Win | Maximum | Maximum | Season-changing |
| Finish mid-field | Modest | Modest | A normal week |
| Miss the cut | Usually none | Usually none | Lost entirely |

The gap between a poor finish and a missed cut is far larger than the gap between a poor finish and a decent one. A player who finishes fiftieth has a week. A player who misses by one shot does not.

## The strategic consequence

Because the downside is a cliff rather than a slope, the first two rounds reward a different approach from the last two.

**Rounds one and two: manage variance.** You cannot win here, and you can lose. The objective is to avoid the hole that costs three or four shots — which in practice means avoiding penalty areas, out of bounds and short-sided misses. Two rounds of unspectacular par golf makes almost every cut.

**Rounds three and four: manage position.** Once you are through, the calculation changes completely. Now the question is what finish is achievable, and risk is priced against the placings you would gain.

That reversal is the most useful single idea in tournament golf strategy. The same principle appears throughout sport — **risk should be priced by what a failure costs right now** — and [golf course management basics](/blog/golf-course-management-basics/) works through how to apply it shot by shot.

## Why cuts compound in a career

In [Golf Career Simulator Pro Tour](/apps/golf-career-simulator/), multi-round events include cuts, leaderboards, ties and playoffs, and finishes move rankings and earnings. Rankings and earnings then decide which events are open to you next season.

That creates a compounding effect that is easy to underestimate:

missed cut → no ranking points → lower ranking → weaker schedule next season → fewer opportunities to earn points

A run of missed cuts does not just cost the weeks it happened in. It narrows the following year. This is why the game rewards a conservative early-round approach far more than the raw scoring model alone would suggest — and it is a genuinely faithful reproduction of the pressure professional golf applies.
